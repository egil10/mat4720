# Mock Exam Solutions

These are model solutions written the way a top student would write them in the exam room: complete, with every step justified, but without unnecessary detours. After each sub-part you find **Grading notes / pitfalls** (what earns the points and what typically loses them) and a **time budget**. The time budgets add up to roughly 190–200 minutes per exam, which leaves 40–50 minutes for reading, planning and checking.

Conventions: $B$ is a real standard Brownian motion on $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t)_t,P)$ with the usual conditions; $\Delta B_k=B_{t_{k+1}}-B_{t_k}$, $\Delta t_k=t_{k+1}-t_k$; $Z\sim N(0,1)$ denotes a standard Gaussian variable, with $E[Z^2]=1$, $E[Z^4]=3$, $E[Z^6]=15$, $E[Z^8]=105$, $E[e^{aZ}]=e^{a^2/2}$. Theorem numbers follow the final syllabus (see the numbering warning at the beginning of the previous chapter).

## Solutions to Mock Exam 1

### Problem 1

#### 1(a) Proposition 2.3

**Statement.** Let $B=(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t)_t,(B_t)_t,P)$ be a Brownian motion. Then
1. $B_0=0$ a.s.;
2. for all $0\le t_1<\dots<t_m$, $(B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_m})$ is a centered Gaussian vector (i.e. $B$ is a centered Gaussian process);
3. $E[B_sB_t]=s\wedge t$.

Conversely, a process satisfying 1–3 is a natural Brownian motion, i.e. a Brownian motion with respect to its natural filtration $\mathcal G_t=\sigma(B_u:u\le t)$.

**Roadmap.** ($\Rightarrow$) Write $(B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_m})$ as a linear image of the independent Gaussian increments; compute the covariance by splitting $B_t=(B_t-B_s)+B_s$. ($\Leftarrow$) Increments are Gaussian with the right variance; independence from $\mathcal G_s$ follows because, in a Gaussian vector, uncorrelated means independent, and the events generated by finitely many $B_u$ form a $\pi$-system generating $\mathcal G_s$.

**Proof.** ($\Rightarrow$) Property 1 is part of the definition. For 2, let $0\le t_1<\dots<t_m$ and $t_0=0$. The increments $B_{t_k}-B_{t_{k-1}}$, $k=1,\dots,m$, are independent: $B_{t_k}-B_{t_{k-1}}$ is independent of $\mathcal F_{t_{k-1}}$, and all earlier increments are $\mathcal F_{t_{k-1}}$-measurable; by induction the joint law is the product of the marginals. Each increment is $N(0,t_k-t_{k-1})$. Hence $(B_{t_1}-B_{t_0},\dots,B_{t_m}-B_{t_{m-1}})$ is a centered Gaussian vector (independent Gaussian components), and $(B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_m})$ is its image under the linear map $(y_1,\dots,y_m)\mapsto(y_1,y_1+y_2,\dots,y_1+\dots+y_m)$, hence a centered Gaussian vector. For 3, let $s\le t$:
$$
E[B_sB_t]=E[(B_t-B_s)B_s]+E[B_s^2]=E[B_t-B_s]\,E[B_s]+s=s=s\wedge t,
$$
using that $B_t-B_s$ is independent of $\mathcal F_s\ni B_s$, and both are centered.

($\Leftarrow$) Assume 1–3 and let $0\le s<t$.

*Gaussian increments.* $B_t-B_s$ is a linear function of the Gaussian vector $(B_s,B_t)$, hence Gaussian, centered, and
$$
E[(B_t-B_s)^2]=E[B_t^2]-2E[B_sB_t]+E[B_s^2]=t-2s+s=t-s .
$$
So $B_t-B_s\sim N(0,t-s)$.

*Independence from $\mathcal G_s$.* Take $u_1,\dots,u_k\le s$. The vector $(B_t-B_s,B_{u_1},\dots,B_{u_k})$ is a linear image of $(B_t,B_s,B_{u_1},\dots,B_{u_k})$, hence jointly Gaussian, and
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(B_t-B_s,B_{u_j})=t\wedge u_j-s\wedge u_j=u_j-u_j=0 .
$$
For a jointly Gaussian vector, a component uncorrelated with the others is independent of them, so $B_t-B_s$ is independent of $(B_{u_1},\dots,B_{u_k})$. Fix a Borel set $C$ and consider the two finite measures $A\mapsto P(A\cap\{B_t-B_s\in C\})$ and $A\mapsto P(A)P(B_t-B_s\in C)$ on $\mathcal G_s$. They coincide on the class $\{\{(B_{u_1},\dots,B_{u_k})\in\Gamma\}:k\ge1,u_j\le s,\Gamma\text{ Borel}\}$, which contains $\Omega$, is stable under finite intersections and generates $\mathcal G_s$; by Carathéodory's criterion they coincide on $\mathcal G_s$. Thus $B_t-B_s$ is independent of $\mathcal G_s$. Together with $B_0=0$ and adaptedness to its own filtration, $B$ is a $(\mathcal G_t)$-Brownian motion. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- 2 points for a complete statement (both directions; the converse gives a *natural* Brownian motion, continuity is not part of the conclusion).
- Most common loss: claiming "uncorrelated, hence independent" without saying the vector is *jointly* Gaussian.
- Second common loss: proving independence of $B_t-B_s$ from each single $B_u$ (or finitely many) and stopping there; the extension to the $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal G_s$ needs the $\pi$-system argument.
- In ($\Rightarrow$), independence of the increments from each other must be argued (it follows from independence of the increment from the past $\sigma$-algebra).

**Time budget:** 15 minutes.

#### 1(b) Time inversion (Proposition 2.4(iv))

**Proof.** We verify 1–3 of Proposition 2.3 for $Z$.
1. $Z_0=0$ by definition.
2. For $0<t_1<\dots<t_m$, $(Z_{t_1},\dots,Z_{t_m})=(t_1B_{1/t_1},\dots,t_mB_{1/t_m})$ is a linear (diagonal) image of the centered Gaussian vector $(B_{1/t_1},\dots,B_{1/t_m})$, hence centered Gaussian. If some $t_j=0$, the corresponding component is the constant $0$, which is a (degenerate) centered Gaussian, so the vector is still centered Gaussian.
3. For $0<s\le t$: $E[Z_sZ_t]=st\,E[B_{1/s}B_{1/t}]=st\big(\tfrac1s\wedge\tfrac1t\big)=st\cdot\tfrac1t=s=s\wedge t$. If $s=0$ both sides are $0$.

By Proposition 2.3, $Z$ is a natural Brownian motion: it has the finite-dimensional distributions of Brownian motion.

*Continuity at $0$.* For $t>0$, $Z$ is continuous because $t\mapsto tB_{1/t}$ is a composition of continuous maps. At $t=0$ continuity is a statement about uncountably many times and is not determined by the finite-dimensional distributions. The argument in the notes: $Z$ and $B$ have the same finite-dimensional distributions, hence the same law on $\mathbb R^{\mathbb Q_+}$ (Proposition 1.19). The event $\{\lim_{t\to0^+,\,t\in\mathbb Q}x_t=0\}$ is measurable with respect to the coordinates at rational times, and it has probability $1$ for $B$ (continuity of $B$). So it has probability $1$ for $Z$. Since $Z$ is continuous on $]0,\infty[$, $\lim_{t\to0^+,t\in\mathbb Q}Z_t=0$ implies $\lim_{t\to0^+}Z_t=0$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- 3 points for the three conditions (the covariance computation $st(\tfrac1s\wedge\tfrac1t)=s\wedge t$ is the key line), 1 point for the continuity argument.
- Saying "$Z$ is obviously continuous" loses the last point: at $0$ it is not obvious; one needs the equality of laws on countably many coordinates.

**Time budget:** 8 minutes.

#### 1(c) Proposition 2.15

**Statement.** Let $B$ be a real Brownian motion and $\pi=\{s=t_0<t_1<\dots<t_m=t\}$ a partition of $[s,t]$ with mesh $|\pi|=\max_k(t_{k+1}-t_k)$. Let $S_\pi=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}|B_{t_{k+1}}-B_{t_k}|^2$. Then
$$
\lim_{|\pi|\to0}S_\pi=t-s\quad\text{in }L^2 .
$$
In particular, a.s. the paths of $B$ do not have finite variation on $[s,t]$ (and hence on no time interval).

**Roadmap.** Write $S_\pi-(t-s)$ as a sum of independent centered terms; then $E[(S_\pi-(t-s))^2]$ is the sum of variances $2\sum(\Delta t_k)^2\le2|\pi|(t-s)$. For the variation: $S_\pi\le\max_k|\Delta B_k|\cdot V$, and uniform continuity forces $S_\pi\to0$ on $\{V<\infty\}$, contradicting the $L^2$ limit.

**Proof.** Since $\sum_k\Delta t_k=t-s$,
$$
S_\pi-(t-s)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}Y_k,\qquad Y_k=(\Delta B_k)^2-\Delta t_k .
$$
The $Y_k$ are independent (functions of independent increments) and centered ($E[(\Delta B_k)^2]=\Delta t_k$). Hence for $k\ne h$, $E[Y_kY_h]=E[Y_k]E[Y_h]=0$ and
$$
E[(S_\pi-(t-s))^2]=\sum_kE[Y_k^2]=\sum_k(\Delta t_k)^2\,E\Big[\Big(\frac{(\Delta B_k)^2}{\Delta t_k}-1\Big)^2\Big]=2\sum_k(\Delta t_k)^2,
$$
because $\Delta B_k/\sqrt{\Delta t_k}\sim N(0,1)$ and $E[(Z^2-1)^2]=E[Z^4]-2E[Z^2]+1=3-2+1=2$. Therefore
$$
E[(S_\pi-(t-s))^2]\le2|\pi|\sum_k\Delta t_k=2|\pi|(t-s)\xrightarrow[|\pi|\to0]{}0 .
$$

*No finite variation.* Let $(\pi_n)$ be partitions of $[s,t]$ with $|\pi_n|\to0$. Since $S_{\pi_n}\to t-s$ in $L^2$, there is a subsequence (still denoted $\pi_n$) along which $S_{\pi_n}\to t-s$ a.s. Let $V(\omega)$ be the total variation of $u\mapsto B_u(\omega)$ on $[s,t]$. For every $\omega$,
$$
S_{\pi_n}(\omega)\le\max_k|\Delta B_k(\omega)|\sum_k|\Delta B_k(\omega)|\le\max_k|\Delta B_k(\omega)|\,V(\omega).
$$
The path is uniformly continuous on $[s,t]$, so $\max_k|\Delta B_k(\omega)|\to0$. Hence on $\{V<\infty\}$, $S_{\pi_n}\to0\ne t-s$. So $\{V<\infty\}$ is contained in the null set where $S_{\pi_n}\not\to t-s$, i.e. $P(V<\infty)=0$. Taking the countable union over rational intervals $[q,r]$, a.s. the paths have infinite variation on every interval. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Statement 1 point; $L^2$ computation 3 points (independence and the constant $2$ must appear); variation consequence 2 points.
- Forgetting to pass to an a.s. convergent subsequence: $L^2$ convergence alone does not give pointwise information.
- Writing $E[(Z^2-1)^2]=1$ (wrong) is a typical slip; the variance of $\chi^2_1$ is $2$.

**Time budget:** 12 minutes.

#### 1(d) Kolmogorov's continuity theorem and Brownian motion

**Statement (Corollary 1.16).** Let $X=(X_t)_{t\in I}$ be an $\mathbb R^d$-valued process on an interval $I$ such that there exist $\alpha>0$, $\beta>0$, $c>0$ with
$$
E[|X_t-X_s|^\beta]\le c|t-s|^{1+\alpha}\qquad\text{for all }s,t\in I .
$$
Then $X$ has a continuous modification $Y$, and for every $\gamma<\alpha/\beta$ the paths of $Y$ are $\gamma$-Hölder continuous on every bounded subinterval. (Theorem 1.15 is the version for a family $(X_y)_{y\in D}$ with $D\subset\mathbb R^m$ open, with exponent $m+\alpha$ instead of $1+\alpha$.)

**Application.** For $s<t$, $B_t-B_s\sim(t-s)^{1/2}Z$, so for every $\beta>0$,
$$
E[|B_t-B_s|^\beta]=E[|Z|^\beta]\,|t-s|^{\beta/2},
$$
and $E[|Z|^\beta]<\infty$. For $\beta>2$ this is of the form $c|t-s|^{1+\alpha}$ with $\alpha=\tfrac\beta2-1>0$. Hence $B$ has a continuous modification which is $\gamma$-Hölder for every $\gamma<\frac{\alpha}{\beta}=\frac12-\frac1\beta$. Letting $\beta\to\infty$ along a sequence (the modifications obtained for different $\beta$ are continuous and modifications of each other, hence indistinguishable), the paths are $\gamma$-Hölder for every $\gamma<\tfrac12$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Statement must have the exponent $1+\alpha$ (strictly larger than the dimension of the index set). With $\beta=2$ one gets $|t-s|^1$, which is **not** enough; this is the classic mistake.
- 1.5 points statement, 1.5 points application.

**Time budget:** 6 minutes.

### Problem 2

#### 2(a) Proposition 3.7 (d) and (e)

Recall: $W=E[X\mid\mathcal D]$ is the a.s. unique integrable $\mathcal D$-measurable random variable with $E[W\mathbf 1_D]=E[X\mathbf 1_D]$ for all $D\in\mathcal D$, equivalently $E[WV]=E[XV]$ for every bounded $\mathcal D$-measurable $V$ (Remark 3.5).

**(i)** Candidate: $Z\,E[X\mid\mathcal D]$. It is $\mathcal D$-measurable (product of $\mathcal D$-measurable variables) and integrable since $|Z\,E[X\mid\mathcal D]|\le\|Z\|_\infty|E[X\mid\mathcal D]|$. Also $ZX$ is integrable. For bounded $\mathcal D$-measurable $V$, $VZ$ is bounded and $\mathcal D$-measurable, so by the defining property of $E[X\mid\mathcal D]$,
$$
E\big[V\cdot Z\,E[X\mid\mathcal D]\big]=E\big[(VZ)\,E[X\mid\mathcal D]\big]=E[(VZ)X]=E[V\cdot ZX].
$$
Hence $E[ZX\mid\mathcal D]=Z\,E[X\mid\mathcal D]$ a.s.

**(ii)** Candidate: $E[X\mid\mathcal D]$, which is $\mathcal D$-measurable and integrable. For bounded $\mathcal D$-measurable $V$: $V$ is also $\mathcal D'$-measurable (as $\mathcal D\subset\mathcal D'$), so
$$
E\big[V\,E[X\mid\mathcal D']\big]=E[VX]=E\big[V\,E[X\mid\mathcal D]\big],
$$
where the first equality is the defining property of $E[X\mid\mathcal D']$ and the second that of $E[X\mid\mathcal D]$. This is exactly the defining property of $E\big[E[X\mid\mathcal D']\mid\mathcal D\big]$, so the two agree a.s. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Each part: candidate is measurable (0.5), integrable (0.5), test identity (1.5).
- Do not "prove" (ii) by using (ii) itself or the tower property of expectations in a circular way. The key observation is that $\mathcal D$-measurable test variables are also $\mathcal D'$-measurable.

**Time budget:** 8 minutes.

#### 2(b) Brownian conditional expectations

Let $Y=B_t-B_s$, independent of $\mathcal F_s$, $Y\sim N(0,t-s)$, so $E[Y]=E[Y^3]=0$, $E[Y^2]=t-s$.

*First expectation.* $B_t^3=(B_s+Y)^3=B_s^3+3B_s^2Y+3B_sY^2+Y^3$. By the freezing lemma (Lemma 3.15) with $\psi(x,\omega)=(x+Y(\omega))^3$, $E[B_t^3\mid\mathcal F_s]=\Phi(B_s)$ with
$$
\Phi(x)=E[(x+Y)^3]=x^3+3x^2E[Y]+3xE[Y^2]+E[Y^3]=x^3+3(t-s)x .
$$
Hence
$$
E[B_t^3\mid\mathcal F_s]=B_s^3+3(t-s)B_s .
$$

*Second expectation.* For $u\le s$, $E[B_u^2\mid\mathcal F_s]=B_u^2$; for $u\ge s$, as above $E[B_u^2\mid\mathcal F_s]=B_s^2+(u-s)$. Define the candidate
$$
C=\int_0^sB_u^2\,du+\int_s^t\big(B_s^2+(u-s)\big)\,du=\int_0^sB_u^2\,du+(t-s)B_s^2+\frac{(t-s)^2}2 .
$$
$C$ is $\mathcal F_s$-measurable ($\int_0^sB_u^2du$ is a limit of Riemann sums of $\mathcal F_s$-measurable variables, by continuity of paths) and integrable. For $A\in\mathcal F_s$, by Fubini (justified since $E\int_0^t\mathbf 1_AB_u^2\,du\le\int_0^tu\,du<\infty$ and $(u,\omega)\mapsto B_u(\omega)$ is jointly measurable),
$$
E\Big[\mathbf 1_A\int_0^tB_u^2\,du\Big]=\int_0^tE[\mathbf 1_AB_u^2]\,du=\int_0^tE\big[\mathbf 1_AE[B_u^2\mid\mathcal F_s]\big]\,du=E\Big[\mathbf 1_A\int_0^tE[B_u^2\mid\mathcal F_s]\,du\Big]=E[\mathbf 1_AC],
$$
where in the last integral we use the continuous version $u\mapsto B_{u\wedge s}^2+(u-s)^+$ (this is the point of Remark 3.18). Hence
$$
E\Big[\int_0^tB_u^2\,du\ \Big|\ \mathcal F_s\Big]=\int_0^sB_u^2\,du+(t-s)B_s^2+\frac{(t-s)^2}2 .
$$

*Martingale.* $B_t^3-3tB_t$ is adapted and integrable, and for $s\le t$,
$$
E[B_t^3-3tB_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=B_s^3+3(t-s)B_s-3tB_s=B_s^3-3sB_s . \qquad\square
$$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- 2 points for $E[B_t^3\mid\mathcal F_s]$, 2 for the integral (including the justification of the interchange), 1 for the martingale.
- Writing $E[B_u^2\mid\mathcal F_s]=B_s^2$ (forgetting the $u-s$) is the most common error, giving a wrong final answer.
- Without any justification of conditional Fubini you lose about 1 point; "by Remark 3.18 with the continuous version $u\mapsto B_{s\wedge u}^2+(u-s)^+$" is enough.

**Time budget:** 10 minutes.

#### 2(c) Theorem 4.5 (Doob's decomposition)

**Statement.** Let $(X_n)_{n\ge0}$ be a submartingale with respect to $(\mathcal F_n)$. There exist a martingale $(M_n)$ and an increasing predictable process $(A_n)$ (i.e. $A_0=0$, $A_n\le A_{n+1}$, $A_{n+1}$ is $\mathcal F_n$-measurable) such that $X_n=M_n+A_n$. The decomposition is unique (up to a.s. equality).

**Roadmap.** Existence: define $A$ by the compensator recursion $A_{n+1}=A_n+E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]-X_n$ and check that $X-A$ is a martingale. Uniqueness: the difference of two such decompositions is a predictable martingale starting at 0, hence 0.

**Proof.** *Existence.* Define $A_0=0$ and
$$
A_{n+1}=A_n+E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]-X_n,\qquad n\ge0 .
$$
By the submartingale property $E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]-X_n\ge0$, so $A$ is increasing. By induction $A_{n+1}$ is $\mathcal F_n$-measurable and integrable (a sum of integrable $\mathcal F_n$-measurable variables). Set $M_n=X_n-A_n$, which is adapted and integrable. Since $A_{n+1}$ is $\mathcal F_n$-measurable,
$$
E[M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]-A_{n+1}=E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]-A_n-E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]+X_n=X_n-A_n=M_n .
$$
So $M$ is a martingale and $X=M+A$.

*Uniqueness.* Let $X_n=M_n'+A_n'$ be another such decomposition. Then
$$
A'_{n+1}-A'_n=X_{n+1}-X_n-(M'_{n+1}-M'_n).
$$
Taking $E[\,\cdot\mid\mathcal F_n]$ and using that $A'_{n+1}-A'_n$ is $\mathcal F_n$-measurable and $M'$ is a martingale:
$$
A'_{n+1}-A'_n=E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]-X_n=A_{n+1}-A_n .
$$
Since $A_0=A'_0=0$, by induction $A'_n=A_n$ for all $n$, and then $M'_n=M_n$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Statement 2 points (must say *predictable* and $A_0=0$; without $A_0=0$ uniqueness fails).
- Existence 2.5 points; uniqueness 1.5 points.
- Common slip: forgetting to check integrability/measurability of $A_{n+1}$, or writing $A_{n+1}$ is $\mathcal F_{n+1}$-measurable (that is adaptedness, not predictability).

**Time budget:** 12 minutes.

#### 2(d) Doob decomposition of $|S_n|$

$X_n=|S_n|$ is adapted and integrable ($|S_n|\le n$). Compute $E[|S_{n+1}|\mid\mathcal F_n]$ by the freezing lemma ($\xi_{n+1}$ is independent of $\mathcal F_n$): $E[|S_{n+1}|\mid\mathcal F_n]=\Phi(S_n)$ with $\Phi(x)=\tfrac12|x+1|+\tfrac12|x-1|$. For an integer $x\ge1$, $\Phi(x)=\tfrac12(x+1)+\tfrac12(x-1)=x$; for $x\le-1$, $\Phi(x)=|x|$ similarly; and $\Phi(0)=1$. So
$$
E[|S_{n+1}|\mid\mathcal F_n]=|S_n|+\mathbf 1_{\{S_n=0\}}\ \ge\ |S_n| .
$$
Hence $|S_n|$ is a submartingale, and by the recursion of Theorem 4.5,
$$
A_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbf 1_{\{S_k=0\}},\qquad M_n=|S_n|-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbf 1_{\{S_k=0\}} .
$$
$A_n$ is the number of visits to $0$ before time $n$; it is increasing and predictable ($A_n$ depends on $S_0,\dots,S_{n-1}$). (This is a discrete Tanaka formula.)

*$E|S_4|$.* Since $E[M_4]=E[M_0]=0$,
$$
E|S_4|=E[A_4]=\sum_{k=0}^3P(S_k=0)=1+0+\tfrac12+0=\tfrac32 ,
$$
using $P(S_2=0)=P(\xi_1=-\xi_2)=\tfrac12$ and that $S_k$ is odd for odd $k$.

*Direct check.* $S_4=2N-4$ with $N\sim\operatorname{Bin}(4,\tfrac12)$: $P(S_4=\pm4)=\tfrac1{16}$, $P(S_4=\pm2)=\tfrac4{16}$, $P(S_4=0)=\tfrac6{16}$. So $E|S_4|=\frac{2\cdot4\cdot1+2\cdot2\cdot4}{16}=\frac{8+16}{16}=\frac32$. $\checkmark$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- 2 points for the conditional expectation (the case $S_n=0$ is the whole point), 1 for $A,M$, 1 for $E|S_4|$ with the check.
- Writing $A_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbf 1_{\{S_k=0\}}$ (including $k=n$) breaks predictability.

**Time budget:** 8 minutes.

#### 2(e) Stopped martingale

**Proof.** $M^\tau_n=M_{n\wedge\tau}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_k\mathbf 1_{\{\tau=k\}}+M_n\mathbf 1_{\{\tau\ge n\}}$ is $\mathcal F_n$-measurable and $|M^\tau_n|\le\sum_{k=0}^n|M_k|$ is integrable. Since $M^\tau_{n+1}=M^\tau_n$ on $\{\tau\le n\}$,
$$
M^\tau_{n+1}-M^\tau_n=(M_{n+1}-M_n)\mathbf 1_{\{\tau\ge n+1\}},\qquad\{\tau\ge n+1\}=\{\tau\le n\}^c\in\mathcal F_n .
$$
Hence $E[M^\tau_{n+1}-M^\tau_n\mid\mathcal F_n]=\mathbf 1_{\{\tau\ge n+1\}}E[M_{n+1}-M_n\mid\mathcal F_n]=0$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** The key is that $\{\tau\ge n+1\}\in\mathcal F_n$ (predictability of the indicator). Forgetting integrability/adaptedness costs half a point. No boundedness of $\tau$ is needed.

**Time budget:** 4 minutes.

### Problem 3

#### 3(a) Lemma 5.6

**Statement.** Let $X_t=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\xi_i\mathbf 1_{[t_i,t_{i+1})}(t)$ be elementary on $[a,b]$ ($a=t_0<\dots<t_n=b$, $\xi_i$ $\mathcal F_{t_i}$-measurable) with $X\in M^2([a,b])$, i.e. $\xi_i\in L^2$. Then
$$
E\Big[\int_a^bX_t\,dB_t\ \Big|\ \mathcal F_a\Big]=0,\qquad E\Big[\Big(\int_a^bX_t\,dB_t\Big)^2\ \Big|\ \mathcal F_a\Big]=E\Big[\int_a^bX_t^2\,dt\ \Big|\ \mathcal F_a\Big].
$$
In particular $E\big[\big(\int_a^bX_t\,dB_t\big)^2\big]=E\int_a^bX_t^2\,dt$.

**Roadmap.** Condition each term on $\mathcal F_{t_i}$ (or $\mathcal F_{t_j}$) where the increment $\Delta B_i$ is independent; then use the tower property to go down to $\mathcal F_a\subset\mathcal F_{t_i}$. Diagonal terms give $\xi_i^2\Delta t_i$, cross terms vanish.

**Proof.** Write $I=\sum_i\xi_i\Delta B_i$. Integrability: $\xi_i$ is $\mathcal F_{t_i}$-measurable and $\Delta B_i$ is independent of $\mathcal F_{t_i}$, so $E|\xi_i\Delta B_i|^2=E[\xi_i^2]\Delta t_i<\infty$; hence $I\in L^2$.

*Mean.* By the freezing lemma with $\psi(x,\omega)=x\,\Delta B_i(\omega)$: $E[\xi_i\Delta B_i\mid\mathcal F_{t_i}]=\xi_i\,E[\Delta B_i]=0$. Since $\mathcal F_a\subset\mathcal F_{t_i}$, the tower property gives $E[\xi_i\Delta B_i\mid\mathcal F_a]=0$, and summing, $E[I\mid\mathcal F_a]=0$.

*Second moment.* Expand
$$
I^2=\sum_i\xi_i^2(\Delta B_i)^2+2\sum_{i<j}\xi_i\xi_j\Delta B_i\Delta B_j .
$$
Diagonal: by the freezing lemma with $\psi(x,\omega)=x^2(\Delta B_i(\omega))^2$, $E[\xi_i^2(\Delta B_i)^2\mid\mathcal F_{t_i}]=\xi_i^2\Delta t_i$, so $E[\xi_i^2(\Delta B_i)^2\mid\mathcal F_a]=E[\xi_i^2\mid\mathcal F_a]\Delta t_i$.

Cross terms, $i<j$: $\xi_i\xi_j\Delta B_i$ is $\mathcal F_{t_j}$-measurable (as $t_{i+1}\le t_j$) and integrable: by Cauchy–Schwarz $E|\xi_i\xi_j\Delta B_i|\le(E\xi_j^2)^{1/2}(E[\xi_i^2(\Delta B_i)^2])^{1/2}<\infty$. Then $E|\xi_i\xi_j\Delta B_i\Delta B_j|=E|\xi_i\xi_j\Delta B_i|\,E|\Delta B_j|<\infty$ and, by the freezing lemma, $E[\xi_i\xi_j\Delta B_i\Delta B_j\mid\mathcal F_{t_j}]=\xi_i\xi_j\Delta B_i\,E[\Delta B_j]=0$; by the tower property its conditional expectation given $\mathcal F_a$ is $0$.

Therefore
$$
E[I^2\mid\mathcal F_a]=\sum_iE[\xi_i^2\mid\mathcal F_a]\,\Delta t_i=E\Big[\sum_i\xi_i^2\Delta t_i\ \Big|\ \mathcal F_a\Big]=E\Big[\int_a^bX_t^2\,dt\ \Big|\ \mathcal F_a\Big].
$$
Taking expectations gives the unconditional isometry. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Statement 1.5 points; mean 1.5; second moment 3 (cross terms must be handled, with the right conditioning $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal F_{t_j}$, $j>i$).
- Conditioning a cross term on $\mathcal F_{t_i}$ (the smaller index) does not work, because $\Delta B_i$ is then not known. Graders look for $\mathcal F_{t_j}$ with $j$ the larger index.
- Integrability of the cross terms is often forgotten; a one-line Cauchy–Schwarz suffices.

**Time budget:** 12 minutes.

#### 3(b) Theorem 5.9

**Statement.** If $X\in M^2([a,b])$, then
$$
E\Big[\int_a^bX_t\,dB_t\ \Big|\ \mathcal F_a\Big]=0,\qquad E\Big[\Big(\int_a^bX_t\,dB_t\Big)^2\ \Big|\ \mathcal F_a\Big]=E\Big[\int_a^bX_t^2\,dt\ \Big|\ \mathcal F_a\Big],
$$
and in particular $E\big[\big(\int_a^bX_t\,dB_t\big)^2\big]=E\int_a^bX_t^2\,dt$.

**Roadmap.** Approximate by elementary $X_n$ (Lemma 5.7); the integrals converge in $L^2$ (Definition 5.8). Conditional expectation is an $L^2$- and $L^1$-contraction. The squares of the integrals converge in $L^1$, and so do $\int X_n^2$. Pass to the limit in both identities of Lemma 5.6.

**Proof.** By Lemma 5.7 choose elementary $X_n\in M^2([a,b])$ with $E\int_a^b|X_n-X|^2dt\to0$. By Definition 5.8, $I_n=\int_a^bX_n\,dB\to I=\int_a^bX\,dB$ in $L^2$ (the limit exists because, by Lemma 5.6, $E[(I_n-I_m)^2]=E\int|X_n-X_m|^2dt$, so $(I_n)$ is Cauchy, and it does not depend on the approximating sequence by the same isometry).

*Mean.* Conditional expectation is a contraction on $L^2$ (Remark 3.10): $\|E[I_n\mid\mathcal F_a]-E[I\mid\mathcal F_a]\|_2\le\|I_n-I\|_2\to0$. Since $E[I_n\mid\mathcal F_a]=0$ by Lemma 5.6, $E[I\mid\mathcal F_a]=0$.

*Second moment.* By Cauchy–Schwarz,
$$
E|I_n^2-I^2|=E|(I_n-I)(I_n+I)|\le\|I_n-I\|_2\,\|I_n+I\|_2\to0,
$$
since $\|I_n+I\|_2$ is bounded. Similarly, on the product space,
$$
E\Big|\int_a^bX_n^2\,dt-\int_a^bX^2\,dt\Big|\le E\int_a^b|X_n-X||X_n+X|\,dt\le\|X_n-X\|_{M^2}\|X_n+X\|_{M^2}\to0 .
$$
Conditional expectation is a contraction on $L^1$, hence $E[I_n^2\mid\mathcal F_a]\to E[I^2\mid\mathcal F_a]$ and $E[\int X_n^2\mid\mathcal F_a]\to E[\int X^2\mid\mathcal F_a]$ in $L^1$. The left sides are equal for each $n$ by Lemma 5.6, hence the limits are equal a.s. Taking expectations gives the isometry. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Statement 1.5, mean 1.5, second moment 3.
- The essential step is that $L^2$ convergence of $I_n$ gives $L^1$ (not $L^2$) convergence of $I_n^2$; claiming "$I_n^2\to I^2$ in $L^2$" is false in general and loses a point.
- Mention why the conditional expectations converge (contraction property); just "passing to the limit" is not enough.

**Time budget:** 12 minutes.

#### 3(c) Computations

(i) $B^2$ is continuous and adapted (hence progressively measurable) and $E\int_0^TB_t^4\,dt=\int_0^T3t^2\,dt=T^3<\infty$, so $B^2\in M^2([0,T])$. By the Itô isometry,
$$
E\Big[\Big(\int_0^TB_t^2\,dB_t\Big)^2\Big]=\int_0^TE[B_t^4]\,dt=\int_0^T3t^2\,dt=T^3 .
$$

(ii) $B\in M^2([0,3])$ ($E\int_0^3B_s^2ds=\tfrac92$). Write $\int_0^2B\,dB=\int_0^3B_s\mathbf 1_{[0,2]}(s)\,dB_s$ and $\int_1^3B\,dB=\int_0^3B_s\mathbf 1_{[1,3]}(s)\,dB_s$. By the polarized isometry (Remark 5.10),
$$
E\Big[\int_0^2B_s\,dB_s\int_1^3B_s\,dB_s\Big]=\int_0^3E[B_s^2]\mathbf 1_{[0,2]}(s)\mathbf 1_{[1,3]}(s)\,ds=\int_1^2s\,ds=\frac32 .
$$

(iii) $E\int_0^Te^{2B_t}\,dt=\int_0^Te^{2t}\,dt<\infty$ (since $E[e^{2B_t}]=e^{2t}$), so $e^{B}\in M^2([0,T])$, its integral has mean $0$ and
$$
\operatorname{Var}\Big(\int_0^Te^{B_t}\,dB_t\Big)=E\int_0^Te^{2B_t}\,dt=\int_0^Te^{2t}\,dt=\frac{e^{2T}-1}2 .
$$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- (i) 1.5, (ii) 2, (iii) 1.5; each requires the $M^2$ check.
- In (ii), integrating over $[0,3]$ or $[0,2]$ instead of the intersection $[1,2]$ is the typical error.
- In (iii), $E[e^{2B_t}]=e^{2t}$, not $e^{t}$ (the variance of $2B_t$ is $4t$, and $e^{4t/2}=e^{2t}$).

**Time budget:** 10 minutes.

#### 3(d) $\int_0^tB\,dB$ via Riemann sums

Let $\pi_n=\{0=t_0<\dots<t_{m}=t\}$ with $|\pi_n|\to0$. Algebraically, $B_{t_{k+1}}^2-B_{t_k}^2=2B_{t_k}\Delta B_k+(\Delta B_k)^2$; summing over $k$ (telescoping, $B_0=0$):
$$
\sum_kB_{t_k}\Delta B_k=\frac12B_t^2-\frac12\sum_k(\Delta B_k)^2 .
$$
$B$ is continuous and adapted, so by Proposition 5.28 the left side converges in probability to $\int_0^tB_s\,dB_s$. By Proposition 2.15 the right side converges in $L^2$, hence in probability, to $\tfrac12B_t^2-\tfrac12t$. Limits in probability are a.s. unique, so $\int_0^tB_s\,dB_s=\tfrac12(B_t^2-t)$ a.s. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** The algebraic identity (1 point), the two limit theorems with correct modes of convergence (1.5), uniqueness of limits in probability (0.5).

**Time budget:** 6 minutes.

### Problem 4

#### 4(a) Itô's formula

**Statement (Theorem 6.7).** Let $X$ have stochastic differential $dX_t=F_t\,dt+G_t\,dB_t$ with $F\in M^1_{loc}([0,T])$, $G\in M^2_{loc}([0,T])$. Let $f:\mathbb R\times\mathbb R_+\to\mathbb R$ have continuous partial derivatives $f_t,f_x,f_{xx}$. Then $f(X_t,t)$ is an Itô process and
$$
df(X_t,t)=\Big(f_t(X_t,t)+f_x(X_t,t)F_t+\frac12f_{xx}(X_t,t)G_t^2\Big)dt+f_x(X_t,t)G_t\,dB_t ,
$$
equivalently $df(X_t,t)=f_t\,dt+f_x\,dX_t+\tfrac12f_{xx}\,d\langle X\rangle_t$ with $d\langle X\rangle_t=G_t^2\,dt$.

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** All three continuity assumptions on $f$ and the integrability classes of $F,G$ (1 point), correct formula with the $\tfrac12$ (2 points). The meaning of the differential notation (integral identity) is good to mention.

**Time budget:** 4 minutes.

#### 4(b) Moments and a cosine martingale

(i) With $f(x)=x^k$, $k\ge2$: $f'=kx^{k-1}$, $f''=k(k-1)x^{k-2}$, so
$$
B_t^k=\int_0^tkB_s^{k-1}\,dB_s+\frac{k(k-1)}2\int_0^tB_s^{k-2}\,ds .
$$
The integrand $kB^{k-1}$ is continuous adapted and $E\int_0^tB_s^{2k-2}ds=E[Z^{2k-2}]\int_0^ts^{k-1}ds<\infty$, so it is in $M^2([0,t])$ and the stochastic integral has mean zero (Theorem 5.9). Taking expectations and using Fubini (the integrand is nonnegative for even $k$ and absolutely integrable in general):
$$
m_k(t)=\frac{k(k-1)}2\int_0^tm_{k-2}(s)\,ds .
$$
With $m_0=1$: $m_2(t)=t$, $m_4(t)=6\int_0^ts\,ds=3t^2$, $m_6(t)=15\int_0^t3s^2\,ds=15t^3$.

(ii) $f(x,t)=e^{t/2}\cos x$: $f_t=\tfrac12f$, $f_x=-e^{t/2}\sin x$, $f_{xx}=-f$. Itô:
$$
dX_t=\Big(\frac12e^{t/2}\cos B_t-\frac12e^{t/2}\cos B_t\Big)dt-e^{t/2}\sin B_t\,dB_t=-e^{t/2}\sin B_t\,dB_t .
$$
For every $T$, $|e^{t/2}\sin B_t|\le e^{T/2}$ on $[0,T]$, so the integrand is in $M^2([0,T])$ and $X_t=1-\int_0^te^{s/2}\sin B_s\,dB_s$ is a martingale on $[0,T]$ (Theorem 5.16), for every $T$. Hence $E[X_t]=X_0=1$, i.e.
$$
E[\cos B_t]=e^{-t/2}.
$$
(Check: $E[\cos(\sqrt tZ)]=\operatorname{Re}E[e^{i\sqrt tZ}]=e^{-t/2}$.)

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** (i) 3 points: Itô (1), justification that the $dB$ part has mean zero (1), values (1). (ii) 2 points. Saying "stochastic integrals have mean zero" without checking $M^2$ loses a point: for $M^2_{loc}$ integrands this is false in general.

**Time budget:** 10 minutes.

#### 4(c) Ornstein–Uhlenbeck

(i) Let $U_t=x+\sigma\int_0^te^{\lambda s}\,dB_s$ (an Itô process, $dU_t=\sigma e^{\lambda t}dB_t$) and $X_t=e^{-\lambda t}U_t$. The factor $e^{-\lambda t}$ has differential $-\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dt$ with no $dB$ part, so the covariation term in the product rule vanishes:
$$
dX_t=-\lambda e^{-\lambda t}U_t\,dt+e^{-\lambda t}\sigma e^{\lambda t}\,dB_t=-\lambda X_t\,dt+\sigma\,dB_t,\qquad X_0=x .
$$
Uniqueness: the coefficients $b(x)=-\lambda x$, $\sigma(x)=\sigma$ are globally Lipschitz with linear growth, so Theorem 8.13 gives pathwise uniqueness.

(ii) The integrand $s\mapsto e^{-\lambda(t-s)}$ is deterministic and in $L^2([0,t])$, so $\int_0^te^{-\lambda(t-s)}dB_s$ is centered Gaussian (Proposition 5.19). Hence
$$
E[X_t]=e^{-\lambda t}x,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\sigma^2\int_0^te^{-2\lambda(t-s)}\,ds=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}\big(1-e^{-2\lambda t}\big).
$$
For $s\le t$, by the polarized isometry with integrands $e^{-\lambda(s-u)}\mathbf 1_{[0,s]}(u)$ and $e^{-\lambda(t-u)}\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}(u)$:
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\sigma^2\int_0^se^{-\lambda(s-u)}e^{-\lambda(t-u)}\,du=\sigma^2e^{-\lambda(s+t)}\frac{e^{2\lambda s}-1}{2\lambda}=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}\Big(e^{-\lambda(t-s)}-e^{-\lambda(t+s)}\Big).
$$

(iii) $X_t\sim N\big(e^{-\lambda t}x,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}(1-e^{-2\lambda t})\big)$. As $t\to\infty$ the mean tends to $0$ and the variance to $\sigma^2/(2\lambda)$; the characteristic functions converge, so $X_t\to N\big(0,\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}\big)$ in law (the stationary distribution).

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** (i) 2 points, (ii) 3, (iii) 1. Typical errors: variance $\sigma^2(1-e^{-2\lambda t})/\lambda$ (missing the 2), or a covariance that drops the term $-e^{-\lambda(t+s)}$ (that is the stationary covariance, valid only in the limit $s\to\infty$). Note that the product-rule computation needs no covariation term; say so.

**Time budget:** 12 minutes.

#### 4(d) Second moment via Itô

By Itô with $f(x)=x^2$:
$$
d(X_t^2)=2X_t\,dX_t+\sigma^2\,dt=(\sigma^2-2\lambda X_t^2)\,dt+2\sigma X_t\,dB_t .
$$
$X$ is continuous adapted and $E\int_0^tX_s^2\,ds=\int_0^t\big(e^{-2\lambda s}x^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}(1-e^{-2\lambda s})\big)ds<\infty$, so $2\sigma X\in M^2([0,t])$ and the stochastic integral has mean zero. With $m(t)=E[X_t^2]$ (continuous in $t$; Fubini applies since $E\int_0^tX_s^2ds<\infty$):
$$
m(t)=x^2+\int_0^t\big(\sigma^2-2\lambda m(s)\big)\,ds,\qquad\text{i.e.}\quad m'(t)=\sigma^2-2\lambda m(t),\ m(0)=x^2 .
$$
The solution is
$$
m(t)=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}+\Big(x^2-\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}\Big)e^{-2\lambda t}.
$$
Check with (c)(ii): $\operatorname{Var}(X_t)+E[X_t]^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}(1-e^{-2\lambda t})+x^2e^{-2\lambda t}=m(t)$. $\checkmark$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Itô (1), justification of zero mean (1), ODE and solution (1.5), check (0.5). Forgetting the $\sigma^2\,dt$ term (the Itô correction) gives a wrong ODE.

**Time budget:** 8 minutes.

### Problem 5

#### 5(a) Lemma 7.4

**Statement.** Let $(Z_t)_{0\le t\le T}$ be a strictly positive $(\mathcal F_t)$-martingale under $P$ with $Z_0=1$, define $dQ=Z_T\,dP$ on $\mathcal F_T$, and let $(Y_t)_{0\le t\le T}$ be adapted. Then $Y$ is an $(\mathcal F_t)$-martingale under $Q$ if and only if $(Z_tY_t)$ is an $(\mathcal F_t)$-martingale under $P$.

**Roadmap.** Key identity: for $\mathcal F_t$-measurable $V\ge0$ or $Q$-integrable, $E_Q[V]=E_P[Z_TV]=E_P[Z_tV]$ by the tower property. Apply it to $V=Y_t\mathbf 1_A$ and $V=Y_s\mathbf 1_A$ with $A\in\mathcal F_s$.

**Proof.** $Q$ is a probability measure since $Z_T>0$, $E_P[Z_T]=E_P[Z_0]=1$. For $\mathcal F_t$-measurable $V$ with $E_P[Z_T|V|]<\infty$,
$$
E_Q[V]=E_P[Z_TV]=E_P\big[E_P[Z_T\mid\mathcal F_t]V\big]=E_P[Z_tV].
$$
In particular $E_Q|Y_t|=E_P[Z_t|Y_t|]$: $Y_t\in L^1(Q)$ iff $Z_tY_t\in L^1(P)$. Adaptedness of $Y$ and of $ZY$ are equivalent since $Z$ is adapted and strictly positive. Let $s\le t$ and $A\in\mathcal F_s$. Applying the identity twice,
$$
E_Q[Y_t\mathbf 1_A]=E_P[Z_tY_t\mathbf 1_A],\qquad E_Q[Y_s\mathbf 1_A]=E_P[Z_sY_s\mathbf 1_A].
$$
($\Rightarrow$) If $Y$ is a $Q$-martingale, $E_Q[Y_t\mathbf 1_A]=E_Q[Y_s\mathbf 1_A]$, hence $E_P[Z_tY_t\mathbf 1_A]=E_P[Z_sY_s\mathbf 1_A]$ for all $A\in\mathcal F_s$, i.e. $E_P[Z_tY_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=Z_sY_s$.
($\Leftarrow$) Same chain read backwards. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Statement 1.5 (strict positivity, $Z_0=1$, $Q$ on $\mathcal F_T$); identity $E_Q[V]=E_P[Z_tV]$ with justification 1.5; both directions 2. Integrability equivalence is often forgotten.

**Time budget:** 10 minutes.

#### 5(b) Girsanov and Novikov

**Theorem 7.5 (Girsanov).** Let $B$ be an $m$-dimensional $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion under $P$ and $\Phi\in M^2_{loc}([0,T];\mathbb R^m)$. Suppose
$$
Z_t=\exp\Big(\int_0^t\Phi_s\cdot dB_s-\frac12\int_0^t|\Phi_s|^2\,ds\Big)
$$
is a martingale on $[0,T]$ and define $dQ=Z_T\,dP$. Then $\tilde B_t=B_t-\int_0^t\Phi_s\,ds$, $0\le t\le T$, is an $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion under $Q$.

**Theorem 7.6(a) (Novikov).** With $M_t=\int_0^t\Phi_s\cdot dB_s$, if $E_P\big[\exp\big(\tfrac12\int_0^T|\Phi_s|^2ds\big)\big]<\infty$, then $Z=e^{M-\frac12\langle M\rangle}$ is a uniformly integrable martingale on $[0,T]$. (Kazamaki: if $M$ is an $L^2$-bounded martingale with $E[e^{M_T/2}]<\infty$, same conclusion; Novikov implies Kazamaki.)

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** The sign: $\tilde B=B-\int\Phi$, with $+\int\Phi\cdot dB$ in the exponent. The martingale hypothesis on $Z$ is part of the theorem; omitting it loses a point.

**Time budget:** 5 minutes.

#### 5(c) A constant drift

(i) $Z$ is adapted and integrable ($E[Z_t]=e^{-\mu^2t/2}E[e^{-\mu B_t}]=1$). For $s\le t$, since $B_t-B_s$ is independent of $\mathcal F_s$,
$$
E[Z_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=Z_s\,E\big[e^{-\mu(B_t-B_s)-\frac12\mu^2(t-s)}\big]=Z_s\,e^{\frac12\mu^2(t-s)-\frac12\mu^2(t-s)}=Z_s .
$$

(ii) $Z$ is the stochastic exponential of $\Phi\equiv-\mu$ (deterministic, bounded). By (i) it is a martingale, so Girsanov applies: $\tilde B_t=B_t-\int_0^t(-\mu)\,ds=B_t+\mu t=W_t$ is a $Q$-Brownian motion on $[0,T]$.

(iii) Under $Q$, $B_T=W_T-\mu T$ with $W_T\sim N(0,T)$. Hence
$$
E_Q[B_T]=-\mu T,\qquad E_Q[B_T^2]=\operatorname{Var}_Q(W_T)+\mu^2T^2=T+\mu^2T^2,\qquad E_Q[e^{B_T}]=e^{-\mu T}E_Q[e^{W_T}]=e^{T/2-\mu T}.
$$
Direct check: $E_Q[e^{B_T}]=E_P[e^{B_T}Z_T]=e^{-\mu^2T/2}E_P[e^{(1-\mu)B_T}]=e^{-\mu^2T/2+(1-\mu)^2T/2}=e^{T/2-\mu T}$. $\checkmark$

(iv) For $\mu>0$ and $s\le t\le T$: $E_Q[B_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=E_Q[W_t\mid\mathcal F_s]-\mu t=W_s-\mu t=B_s-\mu(t-s)\le B_s$, and $B_t\in L^1(Q)$. So $B$ is a $Q$-supermartingale, and not a $Q$-martingale (its $Q$-mean $-\mu t$ is not constant).

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** 1 point each. In (ii) the sign of the drift is the classical trap: with density $e^{-\mu B_T-\mu^2T/2}$, the $Q$-Brownian motion is $B_t+\mu t$, so $B$ has drift $-\mu$ under $Q$.

**Time budget:** 10 minutes.

## Solutions to Mock Exam 2

### Problem 1

#### 1(a) Proposition 1.9

**Definition.** A process $X=(X_t)_{t\in T}$ with values in a measurable space $(E,\mathcal E)$ is *progressively measurable* if for every $t\in T$ the map $[0,t]\times\Omega\to E$, $(s,\omega)\mapsto X_s(\omega)$, is $\mathcal B([0,t])\otimes\mathcal F_t$-measurable.

**Statement (Proposition 1.9).** Let $E$ be a metric space with its Borel $\sigma$-algebra and $X$ an adapted process with right-continuous paths. Then $X$ is progressively measurable.

**Roadmap.** Fix $t$. Approximate $X$ on $[0,t]$ by processes that are piecewise constant in time, taking at each dyadic interval the value at the *right* endpoint (which is $\le t$, hence $\mathcal F_t$-measurable). These approximants are $\mathcal B([0,t])\otimes\mathcal F_t$-measurable, and they converge pointwise to $X$ by right-continuity.

**Proof.** Fix $t>0$ and $n\ge1$. Define on $[0,t]\times\Omega$
$$
X^{(n)}_s=\begin{cases}X_{(k+1)t/2^n}, & s\in\big[\tfrac{kt}{2^n},\tfrac{(k+1)t}{2^n}\big),\ k=0,\dots,2^n-1,\\ X_t, & s=t.\end{cases}
$$
*Measurability.* For $\Gamma\in\mathcal B(E)$,
$$
\{(s,\omega)\in[0,t]\times\Omega:X^{(n)}_s(\omega)\in\Gamma\}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{2^n-1}\Big[\tfrac{kt}{2^n},\tfrac{(k+1)t}{2^n}\Big)\times\{X_{(k+1)t/2^n}\in\Gamma\}\ \cup\ \big(\{t\}\times\{X_t\in\Gamma\}\big).
$$
Each $\{X_{(k+1)t/2^n}\in\Gamma\}\in\mathcal F_{(k+1)t/2^n}\subset\mathcal F_t$ (adaptedness, $(k+1)t/2^n\le t$), so each piece is a measurable rectangle in $\mathcal B([0,t])\otimes\mathcal F_t$. Hence $X^{(n)}$ is $\mathcal B([0,t])\otimes\mathcal F_t$-measurable.

*Convergence.* For $s<t$, $X^{(n)}_s=X_{s_n}$ where $s_n=(k+1)t/2^n$ is the right endpoint of the dyadic interval $[kt/2^n,(k+1)t/2^n)$ containing $s$. Thus $s<s_n\le s+t/2^n$, so $s_n\downarrow s$ (strictly from the right), and by right-continuity $X^{(n)}_s(\omega)\to X_s(\omega)$; for $s=t$, $X^{(n)}_t=X_t$. So $X^{(n)}\to X$ pointwise on $[0,t]\times\Omega$.

A pointwise limit of measurable maps into a metric space is measurable. Hence $(s,\omega)\mapsto X_s(\omega)$ is $\mathcal B([0,t])\otimes\mathcal F_t$-measurable on $[0,t]\times\Omega$. As $t$ was arbitrary, $X$ is progressively measurable. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Definition 1 point; construction 2 points; measurability 1; convergence 1.
- Using the *left* endpoint $X_{kt/2^n}$ gives convergence only for left-continuous processes; with right-continuous paths you must use right endpoints, and then you need them to be $\le t$ (that is why the grid is built on $[0,t]$).
- The metric-space assumption is what makes "pointwise limits of measurable maps are measurable" valid; mention it.

**Time budget:** 10 minutes.

#### 1(b) Carathéodory's criterion and Proposition 1.19

**Carathéodory's criterion.** Let $(E,\mathcal E)$ be a measurable space and $\mu_1,\mu_2$ finite measures on it. Let $\mathcal S\subset\mathcal E$ be stable under finite intersections with $\sigma(\mathcal S)=\mathcal E$. If $\mu_1(E)=\mu_2(E)$ and $\mu_1=\mu_2$ on $\mathcal S$, then $\mu_1=\mu_2$ on $\mathcal E$.

**Proof of Proposition 1.19.** Let
$$
\mathcal S=\big\{\{X_{t_1}\in\Gamma_1,\dots,X_{t_n}\in\Gamma_n\}:n\ge1,\ t_i\in T,\ \Gamma_i\in\mathcal B(E)\big\}.
$$
$\mathcal S$ is stable under finite intersections: the intersection of two such events is again of this form (merge the time lists; if a time appears in both, intersect the corresponding $\Gamma$'s). It generates $\sigma(X_t,t\in T)$, since it contains the generators $\{X_t\in\Gamma\}$ and consists of events of that $\sigma$-algebra. By equality of the finite-dimensional distributions $\mu_\pi$,
$$
P(X_{t_1}\in\Gamma_1,\dots,X_{t_n}\in\Gamma_n)=\mu_{(t_1,\dots,t_n)}(\Gamma_1\times\dots\times\Gamma_n)=P'(X_{t_1}\in\Gamma_1,\dots,X_{t_n}\in\Gamma_n),
$$
so $P=P'$ on $\mathcal S$, and $P(\Omega)=P'(\Omega)=1$. By Carathéodory's criterion, $P=P'$ on $\sigma(X_t,t\in T)$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Criterion 1.5 points (the condition $\mu_1(E)=\mu_2(E)$, or $\Omega\in\mathcal S$, is needed); proof 2.5 points (stability under intersection must be checked, not just claimed). Consequence worth one sentence: two processes with the same finite-dimensional distributions have the same law on $(E^T,\mathcal B(E)^T)$.

#### 1(c) Proposition 1.25

Recall: $\tau$ is a stopping time if $\{\tau\le t\}\in\mathcal F_t$ for all $t$, an optional time if $\{\tau<t\}\in\mathcal F_t$ for all $t$.

**Proof.** If $\tau$ is a stopping time,
$$
\{\tau<t\}=\bigcup_{n\ge1}\{\tau\le t-\tfrac1n\},\qquad\{\tau\le t-\tfrac1n\}\in\mathcal F_{t-1/n}\subset\mathcal F_t,
$$
so $\{\tau<t\}\in\mathcal F_t$ (for $t-\frac1n<0$ the event is empty).

Conversely, let $\tau$ be optional and $(\mathcal F_t)$ right-continuous. For each $k\ge1$,
$$
\{\tau\le t\}=\bigcap_{n\ge k}\{\tau<t+\tfrac1n\},\qquad\{\tau<t+\tfrac1n\}\in\mathcal F_{t+1/n}\subset\mathcal F_{t+1/k}\ (n\ge k).
$$
Hence $\{\tau\le t\}\in\mathcal F_{t+1/k}$ for every $k$, so $\{\tau\le t\}\in\bigcap_k\mathcal F_{t+1/k}=\mathcal F_{t+}=\mathcal F_t$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** The trick of intersecting only over $n\ge k$ is what places the event in $\mathcal F_{t+1/k}$; writing "$\bigcap_n\{\tau<t+1/n\}\in\mathcal F_t$" directly is unjustified.

#### 1(d) Proposition 1.28 (b), (c), (d)

### Problem 2

#### 2(a) Theorem 4.6

**Statement.** Let $X=(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_n)_n,(X_n)_n,P)$ be a supermartingale and $\tau_1\le\tau_2$ stopping times that are a.s. bounded. Then $X_{\tau_1},X_{\tau_2}$ are integrable and
$$
E[X_{\tau_2}\mid\mathcal F_{\tau_1}]\le X_{\tau_1}\quad\text{a.s.}
$$
(For a martingale, equality holds: Corollary 4.7.)

**Roadmap.** (1) Integrability: $|X_{\tau_i}|\le\sum_{j\le K}|X_j|$. (2) $X_{\tau_1}$ is $\mathcal F_{\tau_1}$-measurable. (3) Case $\tau_2\equiv K$: decompose $A\in\mathcal F_{\tau_1}$ along $\{\tau_1=j\}$ and use the supermartingale property at the deterministic time $j$. (4) General $\tau_2$: apply (3) to the stopped supermartingale $X^{\tau_2}$.

**Proof.** Let $K\in\mathbb N$ with $\tau_1\le\tau_2\le K$ (modify on a null set if necessary).

*Integrability.* $|X_{\tau_i}|=\big|\sum_{j=0}^KX_j\mathbf 1_{\{\tau_i=j\}}\big|\le\sum_{j=0}^K|X_j|\in L^1$.

*Measurability.* For a Borel set $\Gamma$ and $n\ge0$: $\{X_{\tau_1}\in\Gamma\}\cap\{\tau_1\le n\}=\bigcup_{j\le n}\{X_j\in\Gamma\}\cap\{\tau_1=j\}\in\mathcal F_n$, and $\{X_{\tau_1}\in\Gamma\}\in\mathcal F_\infty$. So $X_{\tau_1}$ is $\mathcal F_{\tau_1}$-measurable.

*Case $\tau_2\equiv K$.* Let $A\in\mathcal F_{\tau_1}$. For $j\le K$, $A\cap\{\tau_1=j\}=\big(A\cap\{\tau_1\le j\}\big)\setminus\big(A\cap\{\tau_1\le j-1\}\big)\in\mathcal F_j$. The supermartingale property gives $E[X_K\mathbf 1_C]=E\big[E[X_K\mid\mathcal F_j]\mathbf 1_C\big]\le E[X_j\mathbf 1_C]$ for $C\in\mathcal F_j$. Hence
$$
E[X_K\mathbf 1_A]=\sum_{j=0}^KE[X_K\mathbf 1_{A\cap\{\tau_1=j\}}]\le\sum_{j=0}^KE[X_j\mathbf 1_{A\cap\{\tau_1=j\}}]=E[X_{\tau_1}\mathbf 1_A].
$$
Let $W=E[X_K\mid\mathcal F_{\tau_1}]$; then $E[W\mathbf 1_A]\le E[X_{\tau_1}\mathbf 1_A]$ for all $A\in\mathcal F_{\tau_1}$. Taking $A=\{W>X_{\tau_1}\}\in\mathcal F_{\tau_1}$ gives $E[(W-X_{\tau_1})\mathbf 1_A]\le0$ with a nonnegative integrand on $A$, so $P(A)=0$, i.e. $W\le X_{\tau_1}$ a.s.

*General case.* The stopped process $X^{\tau_2}_n=X_{n\wedge\tau_2}$ is a supermartingale (indeed $E[X^{\tau_2}_{n+1}-X^{\tau_2}_n\mid\mathcal F_n]=\mathbf 1_{\{\tau_2\ge n+1\}}E[X_{n+1}-X_n\mid\mathcal F_n]\le0$). Apply the previous case to $X^{\tau_2}$ with the stopping time $\tau_1$ and the constant $K$: since $X^{\tau_2}_K=X_{\tau_2}$ and $X^{\tau_2}_{\tau_1}=X_{\tau_1\wedge\tau_2}=X_{\tau_1}$,
$$
E[X_{\tau_2}\mid\mathcal F_{\tau_1}]=E[X^{\tau_2}_K\mid\mathcal F_{\tau_1}]\le X^{\tau_2}_{\tau_1}=X_{\tau_1}. \qquad\square
$$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Statement 1.5 points (boundedness and $\tau_1\le\tau_2$ must be there); integrability and measurability 1.5; case $\tau_2=K$ 2.5; reduction 1.5.
- The step "$A\cap\{\tau_1=j\}\in\mathcal F_j$" is what graders look for.
- Going from "$E[W\mathbf 1_A]\le E[X_{\tau_1}\mathbf 1_A]$ for all $A$" to the a.s. inequality needs the choice $A=\{W>X_{\tau_1}\}$; don't skip it.

**Time budget:** 15 minutes.

#### 2(b) Characterization of martingales

(i) $\{\tau\le r\}=\emptyset$ if $r<s$; $=A$ if $s\le r<t$; $=\Omega$ if $r\ge t$. Since $A\in\mathcal F_s\subset\mathcal F_r$ for $r\ge s$, all three are in $\mathcal F_r$.

(ii) ($\Rightarrow$) If $X$ is a right-continuous martingale and $\tau\le c$ is a bounded stopping time, Theorem 4.33 with $\tau_1=0\le\tau_2=\tau$ gives $E[X_\tau\mid\mathcal F_0]=X_0$; taking expectations, $E[X_\tau]=E[X_0]$.

($\Leftarrow$) Let $s<t$ and $A\in\mathcal F_s$. Apply the hypothesis to the bounded stopping time $\tau=s\mathbf 1_A+t\mathbf 1_{A^c}$ from (i) and to $\tau\equiv t$:
$$
E[X_s\mathbf 1_A]+E[X_t\mathbf 1_{A^c}]=E[X_0]=E[X_t]=E[X_t\mathbf 1_A]+E[X_t\mathbf 1_{A^c}].
$$
Hence $E[X_s\mathbf 1_A]=E[X_t\mathbf 1_A]$ for all $A\in\mathcal F_s$. Since $X_s$ is $\mathcal F_s$-measurable and integrable, $E[X_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=X_s$.

(iii) Let $X$ be a supermartingale with $E[X_t]=c$ for all $t$. For $s\le t$, $D=X_s-E[X_t\mid\mathcal F_s]\ge0$ a.s. and $E[D]=E[X_s]-E[X_t]=0$. A nonnegative variable with zero mean vanishes a.s., so $E[X_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=X_s$.

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** (i) 1, (ii) 3 (the clever stopping time from (i) is the key; right-continuity is used only in "$\Rightarrow$"), (iii) 1.

**Time budget:** 10 minutes.

#### 2(c) Exit of Brownian motion from an interval

We use Corollary 4.34 (a stopped right-continuous martingale is a martingale), equivalently Theorem 4.33 applied with the bounded stopping times $0\le\tau\wedge n$. Theorem 4.33 (stopping theorem in continuous time): if $M$ is a right-continuous martingale and $\tau_1\le\tau_2$ are stopping times with $\tau_2$ a.s. bounded, then $E[M_{\tau_2}\mid\mathcal F_{\tau_1}]=M_{\tau_1}$; in particular $E[M_{\tau_2}]=E[M_0]$. Here $B$ and $B^2-t$ are continuous martingales and $\tau\wedge n\le n$, so the hypotheses hold. $\tau$ is a stopping time: it is the exit time of the open set $]-a,b[$ by a continuous adapted process (Proposition 1.27(a)).

(i) $B_t^2-t$ is a continuous martingale (Example 4.41). Hence for each $n$, $E[B_{\tau\wedge n}^2-\tau\wedge n]=0$, i.e.
$$
E[\tau\wedge n]=E[B_{\tau\wedge n}^2]\le\max(a,b)^2,
$$
since $B_{\tau\wedge n}\in[-a,b]$ by continuity and the definition of $\tau$. By monotone convergence, $E[\tau]=\lim_nE[\tau\wedge n]\le\max(a,b)^2<\infty$; in particular $\tau<\infty$ a.s.

(ii) $B$ is a continuous martingale, so $E[B_{\tau\wedge n}]=0$. As $n\to\infty$, $B_{\tau\wedge n}\to B_\tau$ a.s. (since $\tau<\infty$ a.s. and paths are continuous), and $|B_{\tau\wedge n}|\le\max(a,b)$. Dominated convergence gives $E[B_\tau]=0$. By continuity $B_\tau\in\{-a,b\}$. With $p=P(B_\tau=b)$:
$$
pb-(1-p)a=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(B_\tau=b)=\frac a{a+b},\qquad P(B_\tau=-a)=\frac b{a+b}.
$$

(iii) From (i), $E[\tau\wedge n]=E[B_{\tau\wedge n}^2]$. The left side tends to $E[\tau]$ by monotone convergence, the right side to $E[B_\tau^2]$ by dominated convergence (bound $\max(a,b)^2$). So
$$
E[\tau]=E[B_\tau^2]=b^2\frac a{a+b}+a^2\frac b{a+b}=\frac{ab(a+b)}{a+b}=ab .
$$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- (i) 3 points, (ii) 3, (iii) 2.
- The most serious error is applying the stopping theorem directly at $\tau$ without stopping at $\tau\wedge n$ (the theorem needs bounded stopping times), or passing to the limit without naming monotone/dominated convergence.
- Another error: assuming $\tau<\infty$ without proof. Part (i) is designed to give it for free.

### Problem 3

#### 3(a) Theorem 5.16

**Statement.** If $X\in M^2([0,T])$, then $I_t=\int_0^tX_s\,dB_s$ ($=\int_0^TX_s\mathbf 1_{\{s\le t\}}dB_s$) is a square integrable $(\mathcal F_t)$-martingale, it has a continuous version, and its associated increasing process is $\langle I\rangle_t=\int_0^tX_s^2\,ds$, i.e. $I_t^2-\int_0^tX_s^2\,ds$ is a martingale.

**Roadmap.** (1) Martingale property from the conditional mean in Theorem 5.9 applied on $[s,t]$. (2) Continuity: elementary approximants have continuous integral processes; Doob's inequality turns $M^2$ convergence into uniform $L^2$ convergence; a fast subsequence plus Borel–Cantelli gives uniform a.s. convergence, whose limit is a continuous version. (3) Increasing process from the conditional isometry on $[s,t]$ and uniqueness in Theorem 4.38.

**Proof.** *(1) Martingale.* $I_t$ is $\mathcal F_t$-measurable (Definition 5.15(b)) and in $L^2$ by the isometry. For $s<t$, $I_t-I_s=\int_s^tX_u\,dB_u$, and by Theorem 5.9 on $[s,t]$, $E[I_t-I_s\mid\mathcal F_s]=0$.

*(2) Continuous version.* Let $X_n$ be elementary with $E\int_0^T|X_n-X|^2\,ds\to0$ (Lemma 5.7), and $I^{(n)}_t=\int_0^tX_n\,dB$. For elementary $X_n=\sum_i\xi_i\mathbf 1_{[t_i,t_{i+1})}$, $I^{(n)}_t=\sum_i\xi_i(B_{t_{i+1}\wedge t}-B_{t_i\wedge t})$ is continuous in $t$, and it is a square integrable martingale by (1). So $I^{(n)}-I^{(m)}$ is a continuous square integrable martingale on $[0,T]$, and Doob's inequality (Theorem 4.32 with $p=2$, applied to the martingale extended by $I_t=I_T$ for $t\ge T$) plus the isometry give
$$
E\Big[\sup_{0\le t\le T}|I^{(n)}_t-I^{(m)}_t|^2\Big]\le4E\big[|I^{(n)}_T-I^{(m)}_T|^2\big]=4E\int_0^T|X_n-X_m|^2\,ds .
$$
Choose $n_1<n_2<\dots$ with $E\int_0^T|X_{n_{k+1}}-X_{n_k}|^2ds\le2^{-3k}$. By Chebyshev,
$$
P\Big(\sup_{t\le T}|I^{(n_{k+1})}_t-I^{(n_k)}_t|>2^{-k}\Big)\le2^{2k}\cdot4\cdot2^{-3k}=4\cdot2^{-k},
$$
which is summable. By Borel–Cantelli, a.s. $\sup_t|I^{(n_{k+1})}_t-I^{(n_k)}_t|\le2^{-k}$ for all large $k$, so $(I^{(n_k)})$ is a.s. uniformly Cauchy on $[0,T]$ and converges uniformly to a process $J$ with continuous paths (set $J=0$ on the exceptional null set). For each fixed $t$, $I^{(n_k)}_t\to I_t$ in $L^2$ (Corollary 5.13), hence a further subsequence converges a.s. to $I_t$; therefore $J_t=I_t$ a.s. So $J$ is a continuous version of $I$ (and it is adapted because the filtration contains the null sets).

*(3) Increasing process.* For $s<t$, $I_s$ and $I_t-I_s$ are in $L^2$, so $I_s(I_t-I_s)\in L^1$ and $E[I_s(I_t-I_s)\mid\mathcal F_s]=I_sE[I_t-I_s\mid\mathcal F_s]=0$. Hence, using the conditional isometry of Theorem 5.9 on $[s,t]$,
$$
E[I_t^2-I_s^2\mid\mathcal F_s]=E[(I_t-I_s)^2\mid\mathcal F_s]=E\Big[\int_s^tX_u^2\,du\ \Big|\ \mathcal F_s\Big].
$$
Rearranging, $E\big[I_t^2-\int_0^tX_u^2du\mid\mathcal F_s\big]=I_s^2-\int_0^sX_u^2du$: the process $I^2-\int_0^\cdot X^2du$ is a martingale. Since $A_t=\int_0^tX_u^2du$ is continuous, adapted, increasing with $A_0=0$, the uniqueness in Theorem 4.38 gives $\langle I\rangle=A$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Statement 1.5; martingale 1; continuity 3; increasing process 1.5.
- Continuity is the heart of the proof. Typical gaps: no subsequence (Doob's inequality gives $L^2$ convergence of the sup, not a.s. convergence); forgetting to identify the uniform limit with $I_t$ for each $t$.
- In (3), the expansion $I_t^2-I_s^2=(I_t-I_s)^2+2I_s(I_t-I_s)$ must be used with the cross term's conditional expectation vanishing.

**Time budget:** 15 minutes.

#### 3(b) Proposition 5.36

**Statement.** If $X\in M^2_{loc}([0,T])$ and $I_t=\int_0^tX_s\,dB_s$, then $I$ is a continuous local martingale with associated increasing process $\langle I\rangle_t=\int_0^tX_s^2\,ds$; that is, $I_t^2-\int_0^tX_s^2\,ds$ is a continuous local martingale, and $\int_0^\cdot X_s^2ds$ is the unique continuous adapted increasing process $A$ with $A_0=0$ having this property.

**Roadmap.** Localize with $\tau_n=\inf\{t:\int_0^tX_s^2ds>n\}\wedge T$; the stopped integrals are integrals of $M^2$ integrands, so Theorem 5.16 applies. Uniqueness: the difference of two candidates is a continuous local martingale of finite variation, hence zero.

**Proof.** Let $\tau_n=\inf\{t\in[0,T]:\int_0^tX_s^2ds>n\}\wedge T$ (stopping times; $\int_0^tX^2ds$ is continuous and adapted). Then $X\mathbf 1_{[0,\tau_n)}\in M^2([0,T])$ since $\int_0^TX_s^2\mathbf 1_{\{s<\tau_n\}}ds\le n$, and (Theorem 5.21 / the localization construction)
$$
I_{t\wedge\tau_n}=\int_0^tX_s\mathbf 1_{\{s<\tau_n\}}\,dB_s .
$$
By Theorem 5.16 applied to $X\mathbf 1_{[0,\tau_n)}$, $I^{\tau_n}$ is a continuous square integrable martingale and
$$
I_{t\wedge\tau_n}^2-\int_0^tX_s^2\mathbf 1_{\{s<\tau_n\}}ds=\Big(I^2-\int_0^\cdot X_s^2ds\Big)_{t\wedge\tau_n}
$$
is a martingale. Since $\int_0^TX_s^2ds<\infty$ a.s., $\tau_n=T$ for all large $n$ a.s., so $P(\tau_n<T)\to0$ and $(\tau_n)$ is a localizing sequence (in the corrected sense for $[0,T]$). Hence $I$ and $I^2-\int_0^\cdot X^2ds$ are continuous local martingales.

*Uniqueness.* If $A'$ is another continuous adapted increasing process with $A'_0=0$ such that $I^2-A'$ is a continuous local martingale, then $D=A-A'=(I^2-A')-(I^2-A)$ is a continuous local martingale with $D_0=0$ and finite variation. Stopping at $\rho_k=\inf\{t:|D_t|\ge k\text{ or }V_t(D)\ge k\}\wedge T$ (and at a localizing sequence), $D^{\rho_k}$ is a bounded continuous martingale of bounded variation; by Theorem 4.37 it is constant, i.e. $0$. Letting $k\to\infty$, $D\equiv0$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Statement 1; localization with correct stopping times 2.5; uniqueness 1.5. Writing "$I_{t\wedge\tau_n}=\int_0^{t}X_s\mathbf 1_{\{s\le\tau_n\}}dB_s$" is also accepted. Forgetting that on $[0,T]$ one needs $P(\tau_n<T)\to0$ (not just $\tau_n\uparrow T$) is a minor loss.

### Problem 4

#### 4(a) Product rule (Proposition 6.6)

**Statement.** Let $dX_i(t)=F_i(t)\,dt+G_i(t)\,dB_t$, $i=1,2$, with $F_i\in M^1_{loc}$, $G_i\in M^2_{loc}$. Then
$$
d(X_1X_2)(t)=X_1(t)\,dX_2(t)+X_2(t)\,dX_1(t)+G_1(t)G_2(t)\,dt .
$$

**Roadmap.** (1) The two Brownian identities (from Riemann sums). (2) Constant coefficients on an interval: expand both sides and compare, using the two identities. (3) Elementary coefficients: apply (2) on each interval of a common partition and add up.

**Proof.** *(1) The identities.* For a partition of $[0,t]$ with mesh $\to0$: $B_t^2=\sum(B_{t_{k+1}}^2-B_{t_k}^2)=2\sum B_{t_k}\Delta B_k+\sum(\Delta B_k)^2\to2\int_0^tB\,dB+t$ in probability (Propositions 5.28 and 2.15); and $tB_t=\sum(t_{k+1}B_{t_{k+1}}-t_kB_{t_k})=\sum t_k\Delta B_k+\sum B_{t_{k+1}}\Delta t_k\to\int_0^ts\,dB_s+\int_0^tB_s\,ds$ (Proposition 5.28 for the first sum, Riemann sums of a continuous path for the second). The same identities hold for the Brownian motion $W_s=B_{a+s}-B_a$ with respect to $(\mathcal F_{a+s})_s$.

*(2) Constant coefficients on $[a,b]$.* Suppose that on $[a,b]$, $F_i,G_i$ are constant in time, equal to $\mathcal F_a$-measurable random variables, and write $x_i=X_i(a)$, $s=t-a$, $W_s=B_{a+s}-B_a$. Then $X_i(a+s)=x_i+F_is+G_iW_s$ and
$$
X_1X_2=x_1x_2+(x_1F_2+x_2F_1)s+(x_1G_2+x_2G_1)W_s+F_1F_2s^2+(F_1G_2+F_2G_1)sW_s+G_1G_2W_s^2 .
$$
On the other hand (random $\mathcal F_a$-measurable factors can be taken out of the integrals, Theorem 5.30, slide 5.31),
$$
\int_0^sX_1\,dX_2=x_1F_2s+\frac{F_1F_2}2s^2+G_1F_2\int_0^sW_u\,du+x_1G_2W_s+F_1G_2\int_0^su\,dW_u+G_1G_2\int_0^sW_u\,dW_u,
$$
and symmetrically for $\int_0^sX_2\,dX_1$. Adding these and $G_1G_2s$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
&(x_1F_2+x_2F_1)s+F_1F_2s^2+(x_1G_2+x_2G_1)W_s\\
&\quad+(F_1G_2+F_2G_1)\Big(\int_0^sW_u\,du+\int_0^su\,dW_u\Big)+G_1G_2\Big(2\int_0^sW_u\,dW_u+s\Big).
\end{aligned}
$$
By (1), $\int_0^sW_udu+\int_0^su\,dW_u=sW_s$ and $2\int_0^sW\,dW+s=W_s^2$, so this equals $X_1(a+s)X_2(a+s)-x_1x_2$. This is the product rule on $[a,b]$.

*(3) Elementary coefficients.* If $F_i,G_i$ are elementary, take a common partition $0=t_0<\dots<t_n=T$ on whose intervals they are constant (with $\mathcal F_{t_k}$-measurable values). By (2), for $t\in[t_k,t_{k+1}]$,
$$
X_1(t)X_2(t)-X_1(t_k)X_2(t_k)=\int_{t_k}^tX_1\,dX_2+\int_{t_k}^tX_2\,dX_1+\int_{t_k}^tG_1G_2\,du .
$$
Summing over the intervals (telescoping) gives the identity on $[0,t]$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Statement 1; identities 1; constant-coefficient case 3; elementary case 1. The common error is forgetting the covariation term or handling only deterministic coefficients without noting that $\mathcal F_a$-measurable constants can be pulled out (Theorem 5.30).

#### 4(b) Proposition 6.13

**Statement.** Let $X\in M^2_{loc}([0,T])$, $\lambda\in\mathbb C$, and

dY_t=e^{U_t}dU_t+\frac12e^{U_t}\lambda^2X_t^2\,dt=Y_t\Big(-\frac{\lambda^2}2X_t^2dt+\lambda X_tdB_t+\frac{\lambda^2}2X_t^2dt\Big)=\lambda X_tY_t\,dB_t .
$$
Since $Y_0=1$, $Y_t=1+\lambda\int_0^tY_sX_s\,dB_s$. #### 4(c) Geometric Brownian motion

(i) Let $f(y,t)=x\exp\big((b-\tfrac12\sigma^2)t+\sigma y\big)$, so $X_t=f(B_t,t)$. Then $f_t=(b-\frac12\sigma^2)f$, $f_y=\sigma f$, $f_{yy}=\sigma^2f$ and by Itô,
$$

$$
The coefficients $b(x)=bx$, $\sigma(x)=\sigma x$ are globally Lipschitz with linear growth (Assumption 8.8), so by Theorem 8.13 pathwise uniqueness holds: any other solution is indistinguishable from $X$.

$$

\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=x^2e^{2bt}\big(e^{\sigma^2t}-1\big).
$$

$$

#### 5(a) Theorem 7.10 ($m=1$)

**Statement.** Let $B$ be a Brownian motion with augmented natural filtration $(\mathcal G_t)$ and $T>0$. Every $Z\in L^2(\Omega,\mathcal G_T,P)$ has a unique representation
$$
Z=c+\int_0^TH_s\,dB_s,\qquad c\in\mathbb R,\ H\in M^2([0,T])\ (\text{progressively measurable for }(\mathcal G_t)),
$$
and necessarily $c=E[Z]$.

**Roadmap.** Uniqueness by the isometry. Existence: let $\mathcal H$ be the set of all such $Z$. (1) $\mathcal H$ is a closed linear subspace of $L^2(\mathcal G_T)$. (2) $\mathcal H$ contains the exponentials $e_f(T)$ for deterministic $f\in L^2([0,T])$. (3) These exponentials are total in $L^2(\mathcal G_T)$ (the technical step). Hence $\mathcal H=L^2(\mathcal G_T)$.

**Proof.** *Uniqueness.* Taking expectations, $c=E[Z]$ (the integral has mean $0$). If $c+\int H_1dB=c+\int H_2dB$, then $\int_0^T(H_1-H_2)dB=0$ and by the isometry $E\int_0^T|H_1-H_2|^2ds=0$, so $H_1=H_2$ $dt\otimes dP$-a.e.

*Step 1: $\mathcal H$ is a closed subspace.* It is clearly linear. Let $Z_n=c_n+\int_0^TH_n\,dB\in\mathcal H$ be Cauchy in $L^2$. Then $|c_n-c_m|=|E[Z_n-Z_m]|\le\|Z_n-Z_m\|_2$, so $(c_n)$ is Cauchy, $c_n\to c$. Hence $J_n=\int_0^TH_ndB=Z_n-c_n$ is Cauchy in $L^2$, and by the isometry $(H_n)$ is Cauchy in the Hilbert space $M^2([0,T])$, $H_n\to H$. Then $Z_n=c_n+\int H_ndB\to c+\int H\,dB$ in $L^2$ (isometry), so the limit of $(Z_n)$ is in $\mathcal H$.

*Step 2: exponentials belong to $\mathcal H$.* For deterministic $f\in L^2([0,T])$ let
$$
e_f(t)=\exp\Big(\int_0^tf(s)\,dB_s-\frac12\int_0^tf(s)^2\,ds\Big).
$$
By Proposition 6.13 ($\lambda=1$), $e_f(T)=1+\int_0^Tf(s)e_f(s)\,dB_s$. Since $\int_0^sf\,dB\sim N(0,\int_0^sf^2)$,
$$
E[e_f(s)^2]=e^{-\int_0^sf^2}E\big[e^{2\int_0^sf\,dB}\big]=e^{-\int_0^sf^2}e^{2\int_0^sf^2}=e^{\int_0^sf^2}\le e^{\int_0^Tf^2},
$$
so $E\int_0^Tf(s)^2e_f(s)^2ds\le e^{\int_0^Tf^2}\int_0^Tf^2<\infty$. Hence $fe_f\in M^2([0,T])$ and $e_f(T)\in\mathcal H$ (with $c=1$).

*Step 3: totality.* We show: if $Y\in L^2(\mathcal G_T)$ and $E[Ye_f(T)]=0$ for all $f$, then $Y=0$. With $f\equiv0$, $E[Y]=0$, so $E[Y^+]=E[Y^-]=:a$. If $a=0$, $Y=0$. Otherwise define probabilities $dQ_\pm=\frac{Y^\pm}a\,dP$ on $\mathcal G_T$. For a step function $f=\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_j\mathbf 1_{(t_{j-1},t_j]}$ we have $\int_0^Tf\,dB=\sum_j\lambda_j(B_{t_j}-B_{t_{j-1}})=:S_\lambda$ and $e_f(T)=e^{S_\lambda-v_\lambda/2}$ with $v_\lambda$ deterministic, so the orthogonality says $E_{Q_+}[e^{S_\lambda}]=E_{Q_-}[e^{S_\lambda}]$ for all $\lambda\in\mathbb R^n$. **Technical step (skipped, as allowed):** this equality of Laplace transforms on all of $\mathbb R^n$ implies (by analytic continuation to $z\in\mathbb C$ and evaluating at $z=i$) equality of the characteristic functions, hence $Q_+=Q_-$ on $\sigma(B_{t_1},\dots,B_{t_n})$. Using all partitions, $Q_+$ and $Q_-$ agree on the cylinder events, a $\pi$-system generating $\mathcal F^B_T$; by Carathéodory's criterion $Q_+=Q_-$ on $\mathcal F^B_T$, and since both are absolutely continuous with respect to $P$, also on the augmented $\mathcal G_T$. Thus $Y^+=Y^-$ a.s.; as they have disjoint supports, $Y^+=Y^-=0$, contradicting $a>0$. So $Y=0$.

*Conclusion.* By Step 3 the closed linear span of $\{e_f(T)\}$ is $L^2(\mathcal G_T)$ (its orthogonal complement is $\{0\}$). By Steps 1–2 this span is contained in the closed subspace $\mathcal H$. Hence $\mathcal H=L^2(\mathcal G_T)$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Statement 1.5 (natural augmented filtration is essential); uniqueness 1; Step 1 2; Step 2 2; Step 3 structure and correct description of the skipped part 1.5.
- In Step 1, the constants must be handled separately before using the isometry.
- In Step 2, show $fe_f\in M^2$; "by Itô" alone is not enough because the representation must be in $M^2$, not $M^2_{loc}$.

### Problem 6

#### 6(a) Lemma 8.27 (Tanaka's example)

**Statement.** Consider $dX_t=\operatorname{sgn}(X_t)\,dW_t$, $X_0=0$, with $\operatorname{sgn}(x)=1$ for $x>0$ and $-1$ for $x\le0$. Then there is a weak solution, uniqueness in law holds, pathwise uniqueness fails, and there is no strong solution.

**Roadmap.** Start from a Brownian motion $\beta$ and *define* $W=\int\operatorname{sgn}(\beta)\,d\beta$: by Lévy, $W$ is a Brownian motion, and $\beta$ solves the SDE driven by $W$. Every solution is a Brownian motion (Lévy again), giving uniqueness in law. $-\beta$ is another solution driven by the same $W$. A strong solution would be $\mathcal F^W$-adapted, but $\mathcal F^W\subset\mathcal F^{|\beta|}$ by Tanaka's formula.

**Proof.** *Weak existence.* Let $\beta$ be a Brownian motion on $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),P)$ and $W_t=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\,d\beta_s$. The integrand is bounded and progressively measurable, so $W$ is a continuous square integrable martingale with $\langle W\rangle_t=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)^2ds=t$ (since $\operatorname{sgn}^2\equiv1$). By Lévy's characterization, $W$ is an $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion. Moreover, since $dW=\operatorname{sgn}(\beta)\,d\beta$,
$$
\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\,dW_s=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)^2\,d\beta_s=\beta_t .
$$
So $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),P,\beta,W)$ is a weak solution.

*Uniqueness in law.* If $(X,W')$ is any solution on some filtered space, then $X_t=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(X_s)dW'_s$ is a continuous martingale (bounded integrand) with $\langle X\rangle_t=t$, hence a Brownian motion by Lévy's characterization. All solutions have the law of Brownian motion (Wiener measure).

*Pathwise uniqueness fails.* With $W,\beta$ as above, consider $-\beta$. Since $\operatorname{sgn}(-x)=-\operatorname{sgn}(x)-2\cdot\mathbf 1_{\{x=0\}}$ (check: for $x\ne0$ trivially; at $x=0$, $-1=-(-1)-2$),
$$
\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(-\beta_s)\,dW_s=-\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\,dW_s-2\int_0^t\mathbf 1_{\{\beta_s=0\}}\,dW_s=-\beta_t-2\int_0^t\mathbf 1_{\{\beta_s=0\}}\,dW_s .
$$
The last integral vanishes a.s.: $E\big[\big(\int_0^t\mathbf 1_{\{\beta_s=0\}}dW_s\big)^2\big]=\int_0^tP(\beta_s=0)\,ds=0$. Hence $-\beta$ is also a solution driven by the same $W$ with the same initial value, but $P(\beta_t=-\beta_t\ \forall t)=P(\beta\equiv0)=0$. Pathwise uniqueness fails.

*No strong solution.* Suppose $X$ is a strong solution driven by some Brownian motion $W$; then $X$ is adapted to the augmented filtration $\mathcal F^W$. By uniqueness in law, $X$ is a Brownian motion and $W=\int\operatorname{sgn}(X)\,dX$. Tanaka's formula gives $W_t=|X_t|-L_t(X)$, a functional of $(|X_s|)_{s\le t}$, so $\mathcal F^W_t\subset\mathcal F^{|X|}_t$, hence $\mathcal F^X_t\subset\mathcal F^{|X|}_t$ (augmented). This is absurd: the law of the Brownian motion $X$ is invariant under $X\mapsto-X$, which leaves $|X|$ unchanged, so for $A\in\mathcal F^{|X|}_t$, $P(\{X_t>0\}\cap A)=P(\{X_t<0\}\cap A)$; hence $P(X_t>0\mid\mathcal F^{|X|}_t)=\tfrac12$, whereas $\{X_t>0\}\in\mathcal F^X_t\subset\mathcal F^{|X|}_t$ would force this conditional probability to be $\mathbf 1_{\{X_t>0\}}\in\{0,1\}$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Weak existence 2.5 (including $\langle W\rangle_t=t$ and the "associativity" $\int H\,dW=\int H\operatorname{sgn}(\beta)\,d\beta$); uniqueness in law 1.5; pathwise non-uniqueness 2; non-existence of strong solution 2.
- With the convention $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=-1$, $\operatorname{sgn}(-x)\ne-\operatorname{sgn}(x)$ at $0$; a top answer handles the null set $\{\beta_s=0\}$ as above. Saying "obviously $-\beta$ is a solution" is accepted with a small deduction.
- The non-existence argument must say *why* $\mathcal F^\beta\subset\mathcal F^{|\beta|}$ is impossible.

#### 6(b) Karatzas–Shreve strong solution

Let $B$ be a standard Brownian motion on $(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)$ and $(\mathcal F^B_t)$ its natural filtration augmented by the $P$-null sets (for a random initial value, augment also by $\sigma(\eta)$). A continuous process $X$ is a **strong solution** of $dX_t=b(X_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(X_t,t)\,dB_t$, $X_0=x$, if
1. $X$ is adapted to $(\mathcal F^B_t)$;
2. $P(X_0=x)=1$;
3. $\int_0^t\big(|b(X_s,s)|+|\sigma(X_s,s)|^2\big)ds<\infty$ a.s. for every $t$;
4. a.s., $X_t=x+\int_0^tb(X_s,s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma(X_s,s)\,dB_s$ for every $t$.

It is equivalent to Baldi's definition (a solution exists, adapted to the given filtration, for every Brownian motion on every prescribed space): a K–S strong solution is a non-anticipative functional $X=\Phi(B)$, and $\tilde X=\Phi(\tilde B)$ solves the equation on any other space.

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** The crucial point is adaptedness to the (augmented) filtration *generated by $B$*, not to an arbitrary filtration. 2 points.

#### 1(d) Law of the iterated logarithm

**Theorem 2.16.** For a real Brownian motion $X$, a.s.:
(a) $\limsup_{t\to0^+}\frac{X_t}{\sqrt{2t\log\log(1/t)}}=1$; (b) $\liminf_{t\to0^+}\frac{X_t}{\sqrt{2t\log\log(1/t)}}=-1$; (c) $\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{X_t}{\sqrt{2t\log\log t}}=1$; (d) $\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{X_t}{\sqrt{2t\log\log t}}=-1$.

*Consequences.* By (c) and (d), a.s. there is $t_0(\omega)$ with $|X_t|\le2\sqrt{2t\log\log t}$ for $t\ge t_0$, so $|X_t|/t\le2\sqrt{2\log\log t/t}\to0$. Also by (c), (d), $\limsup X_t=+\infty$ and $\liminf X_t=-\infty$, so for every level $x$ there are arbitrarily large times $s<u$ with $X_s<x<X_u$; by continuity (intermediate value theorem) $X$ hits $x$ between them. Hence every level is visited at arbitrarily large times.

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Statement 1, deductions 1. Mixing up $\log\log(1/t)$ (at $0$) and $\log\log t$ (at $\infty$) is common.

**Time budget:** 4 minutes.

### Problem 2

#### 2(a) Proposition 4.11

If $X$ is a supermartingale, then for every $a<b$ and $k\in\mathbb N$,
$$
(b-a)\,E[\gamma^k_{a,b}]\le E[(X_k-a)^-],
$$
where $\gamma^k_{a,b}$ is the number of completed upcrossings of $[a,b]$ by $(X_n)$ up to time $k$.

**Time budget:** 2 minutes.

#### 2(b) Theorem 4.12

**Statement.** Let $X$ be a supermartingale with $\sup_nE[X_n^-]<\infty$. Then $X_n$ converges a.s. to a finite limit $X_\infty$ (and $X_\infty\in L^1$).

**Roadmap.** (1) Upcrossing inequality + monotone convergence: the total number of upcrossings of every $[a,b]$ is a.s. finite. (2) Non-convergence means some rational interval is upcrossed infinitely often. (3) Fatou plus $L^1$-boundedness gives a finite limit.

**Proof.** *Step 1.* Fix $a<b$ and let $\gamma_{a,b}=\lim_k\gamma^k_{a,b}$ (increasing in $k$). Since $(x-a)^-\le|a|+x^-$, Proposition 4.11 gives
$$
(b-a)E[\gamma^k_{a,b}]\le|a|+E[X_k^-]\le|a|+\sup_nE[X_n^-]=:C_a<\infty .
$$
By monotone convergence $E[\gamma_{a,b}]\le C_a/(b-a)<\infty$, so $\gamma_{a,b}<\infty$ a.s.

*Step 2.* 
$$
\Big\{\liminf_nX_n<\limsup_nX_n\Big\}=\bigcup_{a<b,\ a,b\in\mathbb Q}\Big\{\liminf_nX_n<a<b<\limsup_nX_n\Big\}\subset\bigcup_{a<b\in\mathbb Q}\{\gamma_{a,b}=\infty\},
$$
since if the path is below $a$ infinitely often and above $b$ infinitely often it completes infinitely many upcrossings. The right side is a countable union of null sets. Hence $X_\infty=\lim_nX_n$ exists a.s. in $[-\infty,+\infty]$.

*Step 3.* For a supermartingale $E[X_n]\le E[X_0]$, and $|x|=x+2x^-$, so
$$
E|X_n|=E[X_n]+2E[X_n^-]\le E[X_0]+2\sup_kE[X_k^-]<\infty .
$$
By Fatou, $E|X_\infty|\le\liminf_nE|X_n|<\infty$. Thus $X_\infty$ is integrable, in particular finite a.s. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Statement 1; Step 1 2; Step 2 1.5; Step 3 1.5. Forgetting that the limit could be $\pm\infty$ (Step 3) is the most common gap; also the bound $(x-a)^-\le|a|+x^-$ should be written.

**Time budget:** 12 minutes.

#### 2(c) Lemma 4.18

If $X$ is a nonnegative submartingale, then for every $p>1$ and $n\in\mathbb N$,
$$
E\Big[\Big(\max_{0\le i\le n}X_i\Big)^p\Big]\le\Big(\frac p{p-1}\Big)^pE[X_n^p],
$$
in the extended sense if the right side is infinite.

**Time budget:** 2 minutes.

#### 2(d) Theorem 4.17 (with Lemma 4.18)

**Statement.** Let $M$ be a martingale bounded in $L^p$, $p>1$, $M^*=\sup_n|M_n|$ and $q=\frac p{p-1}$. Then $M^*\in L^p$ and $\|M^*\|_p\le q\sup_n\|M_n\|_p$.

**Proof of Lemma 4.18.** Let $X^*_n=\max_{i\le n}X_i$. We may assume $E[X_n^p]<\infty$. Then $X_i\in L^p$ for $i\le n$, since $0\le X_i\le E[X_n\mid\mathcal F_i]$ and conditional Jensen gives $X_i^p\le E[X_n^p\mid\mathcal F_i]$; so $X^*_n\le\sum_{i\le n}X_i\in L^p$. The maximal inequality (Theorem 4.9, (4.5)) applied to the supermartingale $-X$ gives, since $\{\min_i(-X_i)\le-\lambda\}=\{X^*_n\ge\lambda\}$,
$$
\lambda P(X^*_n\ge\lambda)\le E\big[X_n\mathbf 1_{\{X^*_n\ge\lambda\}}\big],\qquad\lambda>0 .
$$
Hence, by Tonelli,
$$
E[(X^*_n)^p]=\int_0^\infty p\lambda^{p-1}P(X^*_n\ge\lambda)\,d\lambda\le\int_0^\infty p\lambda^{p-2}E\big[X_n\mathbf 1_{\{X^*_n\ge\lambda\}}\big]d\lambda=\frac p{p-1}E\big[X_n(X^*_n)^{p-1}\big].
$$
By Hölder with exponents $p$ and $q=\frac p{p-1}$,
$$
E[(X^*_n)^p]\le q\,(E[X_n^p])^{1/p}\big(E[(X^*_n)^p]\big)^{(p-1)/p}.
$$
If $E[(X^*_n)^p]=0$ there is nothing to prove; otherwise divide (it is finite) to get $\|X^*_n\|_p\le q\|X_n\|_p$, which is the lemma.

**Proof of Theorem 4.17.** $|M|$ is a nonnegative submartingale (conditional Jensen with the convex function $|\cdot|$). By Lemma 4.18,
$$
E\Big[\max_{i\le n}|M_i|^p\Big]\le q^pE|M_n|^p\le q^p\sup_kE|M_k|^p .
$$
As $n\to\infty$, $\max_{i\le n}|M_i|\uparrow M^*$; by monotone convergence $E[(M^*)^p]\le q^p\sup_kE|M_k|^p<\infty$. Taking $p$-th roots gives the claim. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Theorem from Lemma: 2 points (submartingale $|M|$, monotone convergence). Proof of the Lemma: 3 points (maximal inequality with the indicator on the right side, layer-cake formula, Hölder and the division with the finiteness check). Dividing by a possibly infinite quantity is the classical gap.

**Time budget:** 14 minutes.

#### 2(e) A product martingale

(i) $M_n$ is $\mathcal F_n$-measurable, nonnegative, and $E[M_n]=E[\xi_1]^n=1$. Since $\xi_{n+1}$ is independent of $\mathcal F_n$, $E[M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=M_nE[\xi_{n+1}]=M_n$. So $M$ is a nonnegative martingale and converges a.s. (Corollary 4.13). Moreover $\{M_n\ne0\}=\{\xi_1=\dots=\xi_n=2\}$ decreases in $n$ with $P(M_n\ne0)=2^{-n}\to0$, so a.s. $M_n=0$ for all large $n$: $M_\infty=0$.

(ii) $E|M_n-M_\infty|=E[M_n]=1\not\to0$: no $L^1$ convergence (so $M$ is not uniformly integrable, Theorem 4.26). $E[M_n^2]=4^n\cdot2^{-n}=2^n\to\infty$: $M$ is not bounded in $L^2$. Consistency: Theorem 4.12 needs only $\sup E[M_n^-]<\infty$ (here $0$) and gives a.s. convergence only. If $M$ were bounded in $L^p$ for some $p>1$, Theorem 4.19 would give $L^p$ (hence $L^1$) convergence to $M_\infty=0$, contradicting $E[M_n]=1$.

(iii) $E[M_{n+1}^2\mid\mathcal F_n]=M_n^2E[\xi_{n+1}^2]=2M_n^2$. The compensator increments are $A_{n+1}-A_n=2M_n^2-M_n^2=M_n^2$, so
$$
M_n^2=N_n+A_n,\qquad A_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_k^2,\qquad N_n=M_n^2-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_k^2 .
$$
Check: $E[A_n]=\sum_{k<n}2^k=2^n-1$, so $E[N_n]=2^n-(2^n-1)=1=N_0$. $\checkmark$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** (i) 1.5, (ii) 2, (iii) 1.5. In (ii) the explanation must link to the theorems; a student who claims "a.s. convergence plus $L^1$-boundedness gives $L^1$ convergence" has made exactly the error this example refutes.

**Time budget:** 10 minutes.

### Problem 3

#### 3(a) Lemma 5.26

**Statement.** If $X\in M^2_{loc}([a,b])$, then for every $\varepsilon>0$ and $\delta>0$,
$$
P\Big(\Big|\int_a^bX_t\,dB_t\Big|>\varepsilon\Big)\le P\Big(\int_a^bX_t^2\,dt>\delta\Big)+\frac\delta{\varepsilon^2}.
$$

**Roadmap.** Truncate the integrand at the time its energy exceeds $\delta$; the truncated integrand is in $M^2$ with energy $\le\delta$ and coincides with $X$ on $\{\int X^2\le\delta\}$. Split the event accordingly; use locality and Chebyshev + isometry.

**Proof.** Let $\tau=\inf\{t\in[a,b]:\int_a^tX_s^2ds>\delta\}\wedge b$, a stopping time, and $\tilde X_t=X_t\mathbf 1_{\{t<\tau\}}$. Then $\int_a^b\tilde X_t^2dt=\int_a^{\tau}X_t^2dt\le\delta$, so $\tilde X\in M^2([a,b])$ with $E\int_a^b\tilde X^2dt\le\delta$. On $A=\{\int_a^bX_t^2dt\le\delta\}$ we have $\tau=b$, so $\tilde X_t=X_t$ for all $t\in[a,b)$; by locality (Remark 5.25), $\int_a^bX\,dB=\int_a^b\tilde X\,dB$ a.s. on $A$. Therefore
$$
P\Big(\Big|\int_a^bX\,dB\Big|>\varepsilon\Big)\le P(A^c)+P\Big(\Big|\int_a^b\tilde X\,dB\Big|>\varepsilon\Big)\le P(A^c)+\frac{E\big[(\int_a^b\tilde X\,dB)^2\big]}{\varepsilon^2}\le P(A^c)+\frac\delta{\varepsilon^2},
$$
by Chebyshev and the Itô isometry. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Statement 1; truncation 1.5; locality 1; Chebyshev/isometry 1.5. The locality step (equality *on the event* $A$) is what graders look for; a modification at the single point $t=b$ is irrelevant.

**Time budget:** 8 minutes.

#### 3(b) Proposition 5.27

**Statement.** Let $X,X_n\in M^2_{loc}([a,b])$ with $\int_a^b|X_t-X_n(t)|^2dt\to0$ in probability. Then $\int_a^bX_n\,dB\to\int_a^bX\,dB$ in probability.

**Proof.** By linearity and Lemma 5.26 applied to $X_n-X\in M^2_{loc}$: for every $\varepsilon,\delta>0$,
$$
P\Big(\Big|\int_a^bX_n\,dB-\int_a^bX\,dB\Big|>\varepsilon\Big)\le P\Big(\int_a^b|X_n-X|^2dt>\delta\Big)+\frac\delta{\varepsilon^2}.
$$
The first term tends to $0$, so $\limsup_n$ of the left side is $\le\delta/\varepsilon^2$; let $\delta\downarrow0$. $\square$

**Time budget:** 4 minutes.

#### 3(c) Proposition 5.28 (slides 5.29)

**Statement.** Let $X\in M^2_{loc}([a,b])$ be continuous (and adapted). For every sequence of deterministic partitions $\pi_n=\{a=t_{n,0}<\dots<t_{n,m_n}=b\}$ with $|\pi_n|\to0$,
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{m_n-1}X_{t_{n,k}}\big(B_{t_{n,k+1}}-B_{t_{n,k}}\big)\xrightarrow{P}\int_a^bX_t\,dB_t .
$$

**Proof.** Let $X^{(n)}_t=\sum_kX_{t_{n,k}}\mathbf 1_{[t_{n,k},t_{n,k+1})}(t)$. This is an elementary process ($X_{t_{n,k}}$ is $\mathcal F_{t_{n,k}}$-measurable) in $M^2_{loc}$, and by Definition 5.5 its integral is exactly the Riemann sum. For each $\omega$, the path $t\mapsto X_t(\omega)$ is uniformly continuous on $[a,b]$; with $w_\omega$ its modulus of continuity,
$$
\sup_{t\in[a,b)}|X_t(\omega)-X^{(n)}_t(\omega)|\le w_\omega(|\pi_n|)\to0,\qquad\text{so}\qquad\int_a^b|X_t-X^{(n)}_t|^2dt\le(b-a)\,w_\omega(|\pi_n|)^2\to0
$$
for every $\omega$, hence in probability. Proposition 5.27 gives the claim. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Elementary approximant with left endpoints 1.5; uniform continuity 2; application of 5.27 1; statement 0.5. Note that the left endpoint is essential for adaptedness (Remark 5.29 (slides 5.30)).

**Time budget:** 7 minutes.

#### 3(d) Proposition 5.33 (slides 5.34)

**Statement.** Every nonnegative local martingale $M$ is a supermartingale; in particular $M_t\in L^1$ and $E[M_t]\le E[M_0]$.

**Proof.** Let $(\tau_n)$ reduce $M$: $\tau_n\uparrow\infty$ a.s. and $M^{\tau_n}$ is a martingale. For $s\le t$, $M_{t\wedge\tau_n}\to M_t$ and $M_{s\wedge\tau_n}\to M_s$ a.s. By the conditional Fatou lemma (Proposition 3.8(b), applicable since $M_{t\wedge\tau_n}\ge0$),
$$
E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s]\le\liminf_nE[M_{t\wedge\tau_n}\mid\mathcal F_s]=\liminf_nM_{s\wedge\tau_n}=M_s .
$$
Taking expectations, $E[M_t]\le E[M_s]\le E[M_0]<\infty$ ($M_0=M^{\tau_n}_0$ is integrable), so $M_t$ is integrable; $M$ is adapted by definition. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Fatou's lemma must be the *conditional* one, and nonnegativity is what allows it. Integrability of $M_t$ is part of the conclusion and is often omitted.

#### 3(e) Two Riemann sums

First sum: $B_{t_{k+1}}\Delta B_k=B_{t_k}\Delta B_k+(\Delta B_k)^2$, so
$$
\sum_kB_{t_{k+1}}\Delta B_k=\sum_kB_{t_k}\Delta B_k+\sum_k(\Delta B_k)^2\xrightarrow{P}\int_0^tB_s\,dB_s+t=\frac12(B_t^2+t),
$$
by Propositions 5.28 and 2.15 and $\int_0^tB\,dB=\tfrac12(B_t^2-t)$.

Second sum: $B^2$ is continuous and adapted, so by Proposition 5.28 the sum converges in probability to $\int_0^tB_s^2\,dB_s$. By Itô, $d(B_t^3)=3B_t^2dB_t+3B_tdt$, hence
$$
\sum_kB_{t_k}^2\Delta B_k\xrightarrow{P}\int_0^tB_s^2\,dB_s=\frac13B_t^3-\int_0^tB_s\,ds .
$$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** 1.5 each. The right-endpoint sum does *not* converge to the Itô integral: the extra $t$ is the point of the exercise.

### Problem 4

#### 4(a) Itô's formula for polynomials in space

**Statement.** As in Theorem 6.7: if $dX_t=F_tdt+G_tdB_t$ ($F\in M^1_{loc}$, $G\in M^2_{loc}$) and $f$ has continuous $f_t,f_x,f_{xx}$, then
$$
df(X_t,t)=\Big(f_t(X_t,t)+f_x(X_t,t)F_t+\frac12f_{xx}(X_t,t)G_t^2\Big)dt+f_x(X_t,t)G_t\,dB_t .
$$

**Roadmap.** (1) $f(x)=x^n$ by induction using the product rule. (2) $f(x,t)=g(t)x^n$ by the product rule with the finite variation process $g(t)$. (3) Linearity.

**Proof.** *Step 1: $d(X^n)=nX^{n-1}dX+\frac12n(n-1)X^{n-2}G^2dt$.* For $n=1$ this is trivial (and for $n=0$ both sides vanish). Assume it for $n$. Then $X^n$ is an Itô process with diffusion coefficient $nX^{n-1}G$ (in $M^2_{loc}$, since $X$ has continuous, hence pathwise bounded, paths). By the product rule (Proposition 6.6) applied to $X\cdot X^n$, with covariation $d\langle X,X^n\rangle=G\cdot nX^{n-1}G\,dt$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
d(X^{n+1})&=X\,d(X^n)+X^n\,dX+nX^{n-1}G^2\,dt\\
&=nX^n\,dX+\tfrac12n(n-1)X^{n-1}G^2\,dt+X^n\,dX+nX^{n-1}G^2\,dt\\
&=(n+1)X^n\,dX+\tfrac12(n+1)n\,X^{n-1}G^2\,dt,
\end{aligned}
$$
since $\frac12n(n-1)+n=\frac12n(n+1)$. This is the claim for $n+1$.

*Step 2.* For $g\in C^1$, $g(t)$ is an Itô process with $dg=g'(t)dt$ and zero diffusion coefficient. The product rule (no covariation term) gives
$$
d\big(g(t)X_t^n\big)=g'(t)X_t^n\,dt+g(t)\,d(X_t^n)=g'(t)X_t^n\,dt+g(t)nX_t^{n-1}dX_t+\tfrac12g(t)n(n-1)X_t^{n-2}G_t^2\,dt,
$$
which is Itô's formula for $f(x,t)=g(t)x^n$: $f_t=g'x^n$, $f_x=gnx^{n-1}$, $f_{xx}=gn(n-1)x^{n-2}$.

*Step 3.* Both sides of Itô's formula are linear in $f$, so summing Step 2 over $k=0,\dots,n$ gives the formula for $f(x,t)=\sum_kg_k(t)x^k$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Statement 2; induction 4 (the covariation term $nX^{n-1}G^2dt$ and the arithmetic $\frac12n(n-1)+n=\frac12n(n+1)$); time factor 1; linearity 1. A frequent slip is to forget that the induction uses the product rule for two *Itô processes*, so the covariation term must be computed from the two diffusion coefficients.

**Time budget:** 14 minutes.

#### 4(b) Proposition 6.15

**Statement.** Let $X\in M^2([0,T])$ with $\int_0^TX_s^2ds\le k$ a.s. for a deterministic constant $k>0$. Then for every $x>0$,
$$
P\Big(\sup_{0\le t\le T}\Big|\int_0^tX_s\,dB_s\Big|\ge x\Big)\le2e^{-x^2/(2k)} .
$$

**Roadmap.** Exponentiate: for $\theta>0$, $Y=e^{\theta M-\frac{\theta^2}2A}$ is a positive supermartingale; on $\{M_t\ge x\}$, $Y_t\ge e^{\theta x-\theta^2k/2}$. The maximal inequality gives $P(\sup M\ge x)\le e^{-\theta x+\theta^2k/2}$; optimize at $\theta=x/k$; then do the same for $-M$.

**Proof.** Let $M_t=\int_0^tX_sdB_s$ and $A_t=\int_0^tX_s^2ds\le k$. For $\theta>0$ let $Y_t=\exp(\theta M_t-\frac{\theta^2}2A_t)$. By Proposition 6.13, $Y$ is a positive continuous local martingale, hence a supermartingale (Proposition 5.33 (slides 5.34)) with $Y_0=1$. Since $A_t\le k$,
$$
\{M_t\ge x\}=\{e^{\theta M_t}\ge e^{\theta x}\}\subset\{Y_t\ge e^{\theta x-\theta^2k/2}\}.
$$
By the maximal inequality for continuous supermartingales (Theorem 4.28, (4.15)) with $\lambda=e^{\theta x-\theta^2k/2}$,
$$
P\Big(\sup_{t\le T}M_t\ge x\Big)\le P\Big(\sup_{t\le T}Y_t\ge\lambda\Big)\le\frac{E[Y_0]+E[Y_T^-]}\lambda=e^{-\theta x+\theta^2k/2}.
$$
The exponent $-\theta x+\frac12\theta^2k$ is minimized at $\theta=x/k$, with value $-\frac{x^2}{2k}$. So $P(\sup_tM_t\ge x)\le e^{-x^2/(2k)}$. The same holds for $-M=\int(-X)dB$, which has the same bracket. Since $\{\sup_t|M_t|\ge x\}\subset\{\sup_tM_t\ge x\}\cup\{\sup_t(-M_t)\ge x\}$, the bound $2e^{-x^2/(2k)}$ follows. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Statement 1; supermartingale property 1; inclusion with $A_t\le k$ 1; maximal inequality 1; optimization and the symmetric part 1. Note that no martingale property of $Y$ is needed: the supermartingale property suffices.

**Time budget:** 10 minutes.

### Problem 5

#### 5(a) Girsanov's theorem under bounded energy

**Statement.** See Theorem 7.5 (Exam 1, Solution 5(b)): if $Z=\exp(\int\Phi\cdot dB-\frac12\int|\Phi|^2ds)$ is a martingale on $[0,T]$ and $dQ=Z_TdP$, then $\tilde B_t=B_t-\int_0^t\Phi_sds$ is an $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion under $Q$ on $[0,T]$.

**Roadmap.** By Theorem 4.42 it suffices that $Y^\lambda_t=\exp(i\langle\lambda,\tilde B_t\rangle+\frac12|\lambda|^2t)$ is a $Q$-martingale for each $\lambda$. By Lemma 7.4 this is equivalent to $Z_tY^\lambda_t$ being a $P$-martingale. Compute: $ZY^\lambda$ is the complex stochastic exponential of $\Phi+i\lambda$, which has bounded energy, so Proposition 7.3 applies.

**Proof.** Assume $\int_0^T|\Phi_s|^2ds\le K$. Then $Z$ is a martingale by Proposition 7.3, strictly positive with $Z_0=1$, and $Q$ is a probability measure equivalent to $P$ on $\mathcal F_T$. $\tilde B$ is continuous, adapted, $\tilde B_0=0$. Fix $\lambda\in\mathbb R^m$ and let $Y_t=\exp(i\langle\lambda,\tilde B_t\rangle+\frac12|\lambda|^2t)$, a bounded adapted complex process. With $\Psi_s=\Phi_s+i\lambda$ (so $\Psi^2=|\Phi|^2-|\lambda|^2+2i\langle\lambda,\Phi\rangle$ in the bilinear notation of Definition 7.2):
$$
\begin{aligned}
Z_tY_t&=\exp\Big(\int_0^t\Phi_s\cdot dB_s-\frac12\int_0^t|\Phi_s|^2ds+i\langle\lambda,B_t\rangle-i\int_0^t\langle\lambda,\Phi_s\rangle ds+\frac12|\lambda|^2t\Big)\\
&=\exp\Big(\int_0^t\Psi_s\cdot dB_s-\frac12\int_0^t\Psi_s^2\,ds\Big).
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $\int_0^T|\Psi_s|^2ds=\int_0^T(|\Phi_s|^2+|\lambda|^2)ds\le K+|\lambda|^2T$, Proposition 7.3 shows that $ZY$ is a (complex) $P$-martingale. Applying Lemma 7.4 to the real and imaginary parts ($\operatorname{Re}(ZY)=Z\operatorname{Re}Y$, $\operatorname{Im}(ZY)=Z\operatorname{Im}Y$), $Y$ is a $Q$-martingale. As $\lambda$ is arbitrary, Theorem 4.42 (applied on $(\Omega,\mathcal F_T,(\mathcal F_t)_{t\le T},Q)$) shows that $\tilde B$ is an $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion under $Q$. $\square$

*(General case, as in the notes: localize with $\tau_n=\inf\{t:\int_0^t|\Phi|^2>n\}$, apply the above to $\Phi\mathbf 1_{[0,\tau_n)}$, and pass to the limit using that $Z_{t\wedge\tau_n}\to Z_t$ in $L^1$ by Scheffé's argument since $Z$ is a martingale.)*

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Statement 1.5; reduction via 4.42 and 7.4 2; the algebra of $ZY$ 2; bounded energy of $\Psi$ and Proposition 7.3 1.5. Sign errors in the exponent ($-i\int\langle\lambda,\Phi\rangle$) are the usual problem; verify by expanding $\Psi^2$.

#### 5(b) A measure change giving an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

(i) Here $\Phi_t=\theta B_t$ and $|\Phi_t|^2=\theta^2B_t^2=\theta^2tZ^2$ in law. For $\delta>0$ with $2\delta\theta^2T<1$ and $t\le T$, using $E[e^{aZ^2}]=(1-2a)^{-1/2}$ for $a<\frac12$:
$$

$$

#### 6(a) Theorem 8.10

**Statement.** Assume $b,\sigma$ are measurable with $|b(x,t)|,|\sigma(x,t)|\le M(1+|x|)$. Let $p\ge2$ and $\eta\in L^p$ be $\mathcal F_0$-measurable. If $\xi$ is a (continuous adapted) solution of $\xi_t=\eta+\int_0^tb(\xi_s,s)ds+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)dB_s$ on $[0,T]$, then for a constant $c=c(p,T,M)$,
$$
E\Big[\sup_{0\le s\le T}|\xi_s|^p\Big]\le c\big(1+E|\eta|^p\big),\qquad E\Big[\sup_{0\le s\le t}|\xi_s-\eta|^p\Big]\le c\,t^{p/2}\big(1+E|\eta|^p\big),\ t\le T .
$$

**Roadmap.** (1) Localize at $\tau_R=\inf\{t:|\xi_t|\ge R\}\wedge T$ so that all quantities are finite. (2) Estimate the drift by Hölder and the stochastic integral by Proposition 6.21; linear growth gives $v(t)\le C_0+C_1\int_0^tv(r)dr$. (3) Gronwall, then $R\to\infty$ (Fatou/monotone convergence). (4) Second bound: same estimates without the constant term, using the first bound.

**Proof.** *Step 1.* Let $\tau_R=\inf\{t\ge0:|\xi_t|\ge R\}\wedge T$ and $v_R(t)=E[\sup_{s\le t}|\xi_{s\wedge\tau_R}|^p]$. By continuity, $|\xi_{s\wedge\tau_R}|\le\max(R,|\eta|)$, so $v_R(t)\le E[\max(R,|\eta|)^p]<\infty$; $v_R$ is bounded and increasing (measurable). Moreover
$$
\xi_{t\wedge\tau_R}=\eta+\int_0^tb(\xi_s,s)\mathbf 1_{\{s<\tau_R\}}ds+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\mathbf 1_{\{s<\tau_R\}}dB_s,
$$
and $|\xi_s|\mathbf 1_{\{s<\tau_R\}}\le|\xi_{s\wedge\tau_R}|$.

*Step 2.* With $|x+y+z|^p\le3^{p-1}(|x|^p+|y|^p+|z|^p)$:
$$
v_R(t)\le3^{p-1}\Big(E|\eta|^p+E\Big[\sup_{s\le t}\Big|\int_0^sb\,\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}dr\Big|^p\Big]+E\Big[\sup_{s\le t}\Big|\int_0^s\sigma\,\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}dB_r\Big|^p\Big]\Big).
$$
Drift: by Hölder, $\sup_{s\le t}|\int_0^sb\mathbf 1dr|^p\le t^{p-1}\int_0^t|b(\xi_r,r)|^p\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}dr$, and $|b(\xi_r,r)|^p\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}\le M^p2^{p-1}(1+|\xi_{r\wedge\tau_R}|^p)$. So the drift term is at most $T^{p-1}M^p2^{p-1}\int_0^t(1+v_R(r))dr$.

Stochastic term: by Proposition 6.21 (both inequalities; the integrand is bounded by $M(1+R)$ on $\{r<\tau_R\}$, so it is in $M^2$),
$$
E\Big[\sup_{s\le t}\Big|\int_0^s\sigma\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}dB_r\Big|^p\Big]\le c_pt^{\frac{p-2}2}E\int_0^t|\sigma(\xi_r,r)|^p\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}dr\le c_pT^{\frac{p-2}2}M^p2^{p-1}\int_0^t(1+v_R(r))dr .
$$
Hence, with $C_1=3^{p-1}2^{p-1}M^p(T^{p-1}+c_pT^{(p-2)/2})$,
$$
v_R(t)\le3^{p-1}E|\eta|^p+C_1T+C_1\int_0^tv_R(r)\,dr,\qquad0\le t\le T .
$$

*Step 3.* By Gronwall's lemma (Lemma 8.9; $v_R$ bounded measurable),
$$
v_R(T)\le\big(3^{p-1}E|\eta|^p+C_1T\big)e^{C_1T}\le c\,(1+E|\eta|^p),
$$
with $c=c(p,T,M)$ independent of $R$. As $R\to\infty$, $\tau_R=T$ eventually (continuous paths are bounded on $[0,T]$), so $\sup_{s\le T}|\xi_{s\wedge\tau_R}|\to\sup_{s\le T}|\xi_s|$ a.s., and by Fatou $E[\sup_{s\le T}|\xi_s|^p]\le c(1+E|\eta|^p)$.

*Step 4.* $\xi_t-\eta=\int_0^tb\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma\,dB$. As in Step 2 (now without localization: by Step 3, $E\int_0^T|\sigma(\xi_r,r)|^pdr<\infty$),
$$
E\Big[\sup_{s\le t}|\xi_s-\eta|^p\Big]\le2^{p-1}\big(t^{p-1}+c_pt^{\frac{p-2}2}\big)M^p2^{p-1}\int_0^t\big(1+E|\xi_r|^p\big)dr\le2^{2p-2}M^p\big(t^{p-1}+c_pt^{\frac{p-2}2}\big)\,t\,\big(1+c(1+E|\eta|^p)\big).
$$
Since $t^{p}\le T^{p/2}t^{p/2}$ and $t^{p/2}$ appears in the second term, the right side is at most $c'(p,T,M)\,t^{p/2}(1+E|\eta|^p)$. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Statement 1.5; localization 1.5; estimates of the two terms 2.5; Gronwall and removal of localization 1.5; second estimate 1.
- Applying Gronwall to $E[\sup|\xi|^p]$ without knowing it is finite is the classical gap (Gronwall needs a bounded function); the localization fixes it.
- The constants must not depend on a Lipschitz constant: only linear growth is used.

#### 1(a) Existence and uniqueness of conditional expectation

**Statement (integrable case of Definition and Theorem 3.4).** Let $X\in L^1$ and $\mathcal D\subset\mathcal F$ a sub-$\sigma$-algebra. There exists a $\mathcal D$-measurable integrable $Z$ with $\int_DZ\,dP=\int_DX\,dP$ for all $D\in\mathcal D$, and it is unique up to a.s. equality.

**Proof.** *Existence for $X\ge0$.* $Q(D)=\int_DX\,dP$, $D\in\mathcal D$, is a finite measure on $(\Omega,\mathcal D)$ (countable additivity by monotone convergence; $Q(\Omega)=E[X]<\infty$), and $Q\ll P|_{\mathcal D}$: if $D\in\mathcal D$ and $P(D)=0$ then $Q(D)=0$. By the Radon–Nikodym theorem on $(\Omega,\mathcal D)$ there is a $\mathcal D$-measurable $Z\ge0$ with $Q(D)=\int_DZ\,dP$ for all $D\in\mathcal D$; $Z$ is integrable since $E[Z]=Q(\Omega)<\infty$.

*General $X$.* Write $X=X^+-X^-$ and let $Z^\pm$ be the variables just constructed for $X^\pm$. Then $Z=Z^+-Z^-$ is $\mathcal D$-measurable, integrable, and $\int_DZ\,dP=\int_DX^+dP-\int_DX^-dP=\int_DX\,dP$ (no $\infty-\infty$ since all terms are finite).

*Uniqueness.* Let $Z_1,Z_2$ both satisfy the definition and $D=\{Z_1>Z_2\}\in\mathcal D$. Then $\int_D(Z_1-Z_2)\,dP=\int_DX\,dP-\int_DX\,dP=0$, with $Z_1-Z_2>0$ on $D$, so $P(D)=0$, i.e. $Z_1\le Z_2$ a.s. By symmetry $Z_1=Z_2$ a.s. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Radon–Nikodym on the *smaller* $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal D$ (so that the density is $\mathcal D$-measurable) is the key point; using it on $\mathcal F$ would just return $X$. Uniqueness via the event $\{Z_1>Z_2\}$ (1.5 points).

#### 1(d) Proposition 1.29

**Proof.** Let $\Gamma\in\mathcal E$. We must show $\{X_\tau\in\Gamma\}\in\mathcal F_\tau$, i.e. $\{X_\tau\in\Gamma\}\in\mathcal F_\infty$ and $\{X_\tau\in\Gamma\}\cap\{\tau\le t\}\in\mathcal F_t$ for all $t$. #### 2(a) Theorem 4.19

**Statement.** For $p>1$, a martingale $(M_n)$ is bounded in $L^p$ if and only if it converges a.s. and in $L^p$ (to some $M_\infty\in L^p$).

#### 2(b) Theorem 4.37 (bounded variation case)

**Statement.** Let $M$ be a square integrable continuous martingale. If $M$ has finite variation, then $P(M_t=M_0\text{ for all }t\ge0)=1$.

#### 2(c) Theorem 4.42

**Statement.** Let $X$ be an $m$-dimensional continuous $(\mathcal F_t)$-adapted process with $X_0=0$ a.s. such that for every $\lambda\in\mathbb R^m$,
$$
Y^\lambda_t=\exp\Big(i\langle\lambda,X_t\rangle+\frac12|\lambda|^2t\Big)
$$
is an $(\mathcal F_t)$-martingale. Then $X$ is an $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion.

**Auxiliary fact (Baldi, Exercise 4.5).** If $Y$ is an $\mathbb R^m$-valued random variable and $\mathcal G$ a $\sigma$-algebra with $E[e^{i\langle\lambda,Y\rangle}\mid\mathcal G]=\varphi(\lambda)$ deterministic for every $\lambda$, then $Y$ is independent of $\mathcal G$ and has characteristic function $\varphi$.

*Proof of the fact.* Taking expectations, $\varphi$ is the characteristic function of $Y$. Let $A\in\mathcal G$ with $P(A)>0$ and $P_A=P(\cdot\cap A)/P(A)$. Then $E_{P_A}[e^{i\langle\lambda,Y\rangle}]=E[e^{i\langle\lambda,Y\rangle}\mathbf 1_A]/P(A)=E[\varphi(\lambda)\mathbf 1_A]/P(A)=\varphi(\lambda)$. So $Y$ has the same law under $P_A$ as under $P$: $P(Y\in C,A)=P(Y\in C)P(A)$ for all Borel $C$; trivially also if $P(A)=0$. Hence $Y$ is independent of $\mathcal G$.

**Proof of the theorem.** Let $s<t$. The martingale property $E[Y^\lambda_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=Y^\lambda_s$, multiplied by the bounded $\mathcal F_s$-measurable factor $e^{-i\langle\lambda,X_s\rangle-\frac12|\lambda|^2t}$, gives
$$
E\big[e^{i\langle\lambda,X_t-X_s\rangle}\mid\mathcal F_s\big]=e^{-\frac12|\lambda|^2(t-s)}\qquad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb R^m .
$$
By the auxiliary fact, $X_t-X_s$ is independent of $\mathcal F_s$ and has characteristic function $e^{-\frac12|\lambda|^2(t-s)}$, i.e. $X_t-X_s\sim N(0,(t-s)I_m)$. Together with $X_0=0$, continuity and adaptedness, $X$ is an $m$-dimensional $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion (Definition 2.5). $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Statement 1.5; conditional characteristic function 1.5; auxiliary fact with proof 2; conclusion 1. A common gap is to conclude independence from "the conditional characteristic function equals the unconditional one" without any argument.

since $P(B_s>0)=P(B_s<0)=\frac12$ and $P(B_s=0)=0$ for $s>0$. Next, $B_t^2-t=\int_0^t2B_sdB_s$ with $2B\in M^2$, and $E[W_t]=0$, so
$$
E[B_t^2W_t]=E[(B_t^2-t)W_t]=E\Big[\int_0^t2B_sdB_s\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(B_s)dB_s\Big]=2\int_0^tE[B_s\operatorname{sgn}(B_s)]\,ds=2\int_0^tE|B_s|\,ds,
$$
using $x\operatorname{sgn}(x)=|x|$ (also at $x=0$). With $E|B_s|=\sqrt{2s/\pi}$,
$$
E[B_t^2W_t]=2\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\int_0^t\sqrt s\,ds=\frac43\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\,t^{3/2}>0 .
$$
If $W_t$ and $B_t$ were independent, we would have $E[B_t^2W_t]=E[B_t^2]E[W_t]=0$. So $W_t$ and $B_t$ are uncorrelated but not independent (and a fortiori the processes $W,B$ are not independent).

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** (i) 3 points: the complex stochastic exponential, the boundedness giving a true martingale, and Theorem 4.42. (ii) 3 points. A common wrong argument: "$W$ and $B$ are jointly Gaussian and uncorrelated, hence independent"; the pair $(W_t,B_t)$ is not jointly Gaussian.

### Problem 3

#### 3(a) Proposition 6.21 (bounded case)

**Statement.** Let $p\ge2$ and $X\in M^2_{loc}([0,T])$. There is $c_p>0$ depending only on $p$ such that
$$
E\Big[\sup_{0\le t\le T}\Big|\int_0^tX_s\,dB_s\Big|^p\Big]\le c_p\,E\Big[\Big(\int_0^TX_s^2\,ds\Big)^{p/2}\Big],\qquad E\Big[\Big(\int_0^TX_s^2\,ds\Big)^{p/2}\Big]\le T^{\frac{p-2}2}E\int_0^T|X_s|^p\,ds,
$$
in the extended sense if the right sides are infinite.

**Roadmap.** (1) Itô's formula for $|M_t|^p$; the $dB$ part is a true martingale by boundedness. (2) $E|M_T|^p\le\frac{p(p-1)}2E[(M^*)^{p-2}A_T]$. (3) Doob + Hölder and division. (4) The second inequality is Hölder in time.

**Proof.** Let $M_t=\int_0^tX\,dB$, $A_t=\langle M\rangle_t=\int_0^tX_s^2ds$, $M^*=\sup_{t\le T}|M_t|$; by assumption $|M|\le C$ and $A_T\le C$. For $p=2$: $E[(M^*)^2]\le4E[M_T^2]=4E[A_T]$ by Doob and the isometry, so $c_2=4$ works. Let $p>2$. The function $f(x)=|x|^p$ is $C^2$ with $f'(x)=p|x|^{p-2}x$, $f''(x)=p(p-1)|x|^{p-2}$. By Itô's formula,
$$
|M_t|^p=\int_0^tp|M_s|^{p-2}M_sX_s\,dB_s+\frac{p(p-1)}2\int_0^t|M_s|^{p-2}X_s^2\,ds .
$$
The integrand of the stochastic integral satisfies $E\int_0^Tp^2|M_s|^{2p-2}X_s^2ds\le p^2C^{2p-2}E[A_T]<\infty$, so it is in $M^2$ and the integral has mean zero. Hence
$$
E|M_T|^p=\frac{p(p-1)}2E\int_0^T|M_s|^{p-2}X_s^2ds\le\frac{p(p-1)}2E\big[(M^*)^{p-2}A_T\big].
$$
By Doob's inequality (Theorem 4.32), $E[(M^*)^p]\le\big(\frac p{p-1}\big)^pE|M_T|^p$. With $K_p=\frac{p(p-1)}2\big(\frac p{p-1}\big)^p$ and Hölder with exponents $\frac p{p-2}$ and $\frac p2$:
$$
E[(M^*)^p]\le K_pE\big[(M^*)^{p-2}A_T\big]\le K_p\big(E[(M^*)^p]\big)^{\frac{p-2}p}\big(E[A_T^{p/2}]\big)^{\frac2p}.
$$
All quantities are finite (boundedness). If $E[(M^*)^p]=0$ there is nothing to prove; otherwise divide by $(E[(M^*)^p])^{(p-2)/p}$ and raise to the power $p/2$:
$$
E[(M^*)^p]\le K_p^{p/2}E\big[A_T^{p/2}\big]=c_pE\Big[\Big(\int_0^TX_s^2ds\Big)^{p/2}\Big],\qquad c_p=K_p^{p/2}.
$$
For the second inequality, Hölder in $ds$ with exponents $\frac p2$ and $\frac p{p-2}$ gives pathwise $\int_0^TX_s^2ds\le\big(\int_0^T|X_s|^pds\big)^{2/p}T^{(p-2)/p}$; raise to $p/2$ and take expectations. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Statement 1; Itô for $|x|^p$ and the martingale argument 1.5; Doob + Hölder + division 2.5; second inequality 1. Dividing without the finiteness remark, and forgetting that $|x|^p\in C^2$ requires $p\ge2$, are the typical deductions.

#### 3(c) A time-changed martingale

$X_s=e^s$ is deterministic, in $M^2_{loc}([0,\infty))$, and $A_t=\int_0^te^{2s}ds=\frac12(e^{2t}-1)\to\infty$. By Theorem 6.17 and Corollary 6.20 there is a Brownian motion $W$ with $M_t=W_{A_t}$. By the law of the iterated logarithm (Theorem 2.16(c)) for $W$, $\limsup_{u\to\infty}W_u/\sqrt{2u\log\log u}=1$ a.s. Since $t\mapsto A_t$ is a continuous increasing bijection of $[0,\infty)$ onto itself, the limsup along $u=A_t$, $t\to\infty$, is the same:
$$
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{M_t}{\sqrt{2A_t\log\log A_t}}=1\quad\text{a.s.},\qquad2A_t=e^{2t}-1,
$$
which is the claim. For $X_t=e^{-t}M_t$ write
$$
\frac{X_t}{\sqrt{\log t}}=\frac{M_t}{\sqrt{2A_t\log\log A_t}}\cdot r(t),\qquad r(t)=\frac{e^{-t}\sqrt{(e^{2t}-1)\log\log A_t}}{\sqrt{\log t}} .
$$
Now $e^{-2t}(e^{2t}-1)\to1$ and $\log A_t=2t-\log2+\log(1-e^{-2t})$, so $\log\log A_t=\log t+\log2+o(1)$ and $\log\log A_t/\log t\to1$. Hence $r(t)\to1$ (deterministic), and since multiplying by a positive factor tending to $1$ does not change the limsup,
$$
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{X_t}{\sqrt{\log t}}=1\quad\text{a.s.}
$$
($X$ is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process $dX=-X\,dt+dB$, $X_0=0$.)

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Time change and identification of $A_t$ (1.5), LIL along a deterministic time change (1.5), asymptotics of $r(t)$ (1). Note that only the deterministic nature and continuity of $A$ are needed to transfer the limsup.

### Problem 4

#### 4(a) Theorem 8.13

**Statement.** Under Assumption 8.8 ($|b(x,t)|,|\sigma(x,t)|\le M(1+|x|)$, $|b(x,t)-b(y,t)|,|\sigma(x,t)-\sigma(y,t)|\le L|x-y|$), let $\eta$ be square integrable and $\mathcal F_0$-measurable. On the given Brownian filtered space there is a continuous adapted process $\xi\in M^2([0,T])$ with
$$
\xi_t=\eta+\int_0^tb(\xi_s,s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\,dB_s,\qquad0\le t\le T .
$$
If $\tilde\xi$ is another solution with the same $\eta$ and $B$, then $P(\xi_t=\tilde\xi_t\ \forall t\in[0,T])=1$.

**Roadmap.** Key estimate: for $Y,Y'\in M^2$, $E\sup_{s\le t}|\Phi(Y)_s-\Phi(Y')_s|^2\le C\int_0^tE|Y_r-Y'_r|^2dr$ with $C=L^2(2T+8)$, where $\Phi(Y)$ is the right-hand side. Uniqueness: apply it to two solutions and use Gronwall. Existence: Picard iteration, $\Delta_n(t)\le K(Ct)^n/n!$, Borel–Cantelli for uniform a.s. convergence, then pass to the limit in the equation.

**Proof.** For a continuous adapted $Y\in M^2([0,T])$ define $\Phi(Y)_t=\eta+\int_0^tb(Y_s,s)ds+\int_0^t\sigma(Y_s,s)dB_s$. By linear growth $E\int_0^T|b(Y_s,s)|^2ds\le2M^2(T+E\int_0^TY_s^2ds)<\infty$ and similarly for $\sigma$, so $\sigma(Y,\cdot)\in M^2$, the stochastic integral is a continuous square integrable martingale and $\Phi(Y)$ is continuous, adapted and in $M^2$ (indeed $E\sup_t|\Phi(Y)_t|^2<\infty$).

*Key estimate.* Using $(a+b)^2\le2a^2+2b^2$, Cauchy–Schwarz for the $ds$ integral and the maximal estimate $E\sup_{s\le t}|\int_0^sH\,dB|^2\le4E\int_0^tH^2dr$:
$$
E\sup_{s\le t}|\Phi(Y)_s-\Phi(Y')_s|^2\le2t\,E\int_0^t|b(Y_r,r)-b(Y'_r,r)|^2dr+8E\int_0^t|\sigma(Y_r,r)-\sigma(Y'_r,r)|^2dr\le C\int_0^tE|Y_r-Y'_r|^2dr,
$$
with $C=L^2(2T+8)$.

*Uniqueness.* Let $\xi,\tilde\xi$ be solutions. By Theorem 8.10 with $p=2$, $E\sup_{t\le T}|\xi_t|^2$ and $E\sup_{t\le T}|\tilde\xi_t|^2$ are finite, so $v(t)=E\sup_{s\le t}|\xi_s-\tilde\xi_s|^2$ is bounded. Since $\xi=\Phi(\xi)$ and $\tilde\xi=\Phi(\tilde\xi)$, the key estimate gives $v(t)\le C\int_0^tE|\xi_r-\tilde\xi_r|^2dr\le C\int_0^tv(r)dr$. Gronwall's lemma with constant $0$ gives $v\equiv0$, so $\sup_{t\le T}|\xi_t-\tilde\xi_t|=0$ a.s.: the solutions are indistinguishable.

*Existence.* Let $\xi^0_t\equiv\eta$ and $\xi^{n+1}=\Phi(\xi^n)$. By the first paragraph and induction, each $\xi^n$ is continuous, adapted, with $E\sup_t|\xi^n_t|^2<\infty$. Let $\Delta_n(t)=E\sup_{s\le t}|\xi^{n+1}_s-\xi^n_s|^2$. First,
$$
\Delta_0(t)=E\sup_{s\le t}\Big|\int_0^sb(\eta,r)dr+\int_0^s\sigma(\eta,r)dB_r\Big|^2\le(2T+8)\cdot2M^2T(1+E\eta^2)=:K .
$$
By the key estimate, $\Delta_n(t)\le C\int_0^tE|\xi^n_r-\xi^{n-1}_r|^2dr\le C\int_0^t\Delta_{n-1}(r)dr$, hence by induction
$$
\Delta_n(t)\le K\frac{(Ct)^n}{n!},\qquad0\le t\le T .
$$
By Chebyshev, $P\big(\sup_{t\le T}|\xi^{n+1}_t-\xi^n_t|>2^{-n}\big)\le4^nK(CT)^n/n!$, which is summable. By Borel–Cantelli, a.s. $\sup_t|\xi^{n+1}_t-\xi^n_t|\le2^{-n}$ for large $n$, so $\xi^n$ converges uniformly on $[0,T]$ a.s. to a continuous adapted process $\xi$. Also $\sum_n\|\sup_t|\xi^{n+1}_t-\xi^n_t|\|_{L^2}\le\sum_n\sqrt{K(CT)^n/n!}<\infty$, so $\xi^n\to\xi$ in $L^2(\Omega;\sup_t|\cdot|)$; in particular $E\sup_t|\xi_t|^2<\infty$ and $\xi\in M^2([0,T])$.

*Passing to the limit.* By the key estimate with $Y=\xi^n$, $Y'=\xi$:
$$
E\sup_{t\le T}|\xi^{n+1}_t-\Phi(\xi)_t|^2=E\sup_{t\le T}|\Phi(\xi^n)_t-\Phi(\xi)_t|^2\le CT\,E\sup_{t\le T}|\xi^n_t-\xi_t|^2\to0 .
$$
Since also $\xi^{n+1}\to\xi$ uniformly in $L^2$, $\xi=\Phi(\xi)$, i.e. $\xi$ solves the equation. $\square$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.**
- Statement 2; key estimate 2.5; uniqueness 2.5; Picard bound 2.5; convergence and limit 2.5.
- In uniqueness, Gronwall needs $v$ bounded: cite Theorem 8.10 (or localize). Many answers skip this.
- In existence, $L^2$ convergence of $\xi^n(t)$ for each $t$ is not enough to get a continuous limit; one needs the uniform (sup) control and Borel–Cantelli or completeness of $L^2(\Omega;C[0,T])$.

#### 4(b) Theorem 8.22 (Proposition 8.22 on the slides)

**Statement.** Under Assumption 8.8, let $\xi^1,\xi^2$ be solutions driven by the same Brownian motion with initial data $(\eta_1,u)$ and $(\eta_2,v)$, $u\le v$, where $\eta_1\in L^p(\mathcal F_u)$, $\eta_2\in L^p(\mathcal F_v)$, $p\ge2$. Then
$$
E\Big[\sup_{v\le t\le T}|\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t|^p\Big]\le c(L,T,M,p)\big(1+E|\eta_1|^p\big)\big(|u-v|^{p/2}+E|\eta_1-\eta_2|^p\big).
$$

**Roadmap.** (1) Same starting time $v$: Lipschitz estimates with Proposition 6.21 and Gronwall give $E\sup|\xi-\xi'|^p\le C_1E|\eta-\eta'|^p$. (2) For $t\ge v$, $\xi^1$ solves the equation from time $v$ with initial value $\xi^1_v$. (3) Apply (1) with $\eta=\xi^1_v$, $\eta'=\eta_2$ and estimate $E|\xi^1_v-\eta_1|^p$ by the second a priori bound of Theorem 8.10.

**Proof.** *Step 1.* Let $\xi,\xi'$ solve the equation on $[v,T]$ with $\mathcal F_v$-measurable initial values $\eta,\eta'\in L^p$. Put $D_t=\xi_t-\xi'_t$ and $w(t)=E\sup_{v\le s\le t}|D_s|^p$, finite by Theorem 8.10. Since
$$
D_t=(\eta-\eta')+\int_v^t\big(b(\xi_r,r)-b(\xi'_r,r)\big)dr+\int_v^t\big(\sigma(\xi_r,r)-\sigma(\xi'_r,r)\big)dB_r,
$$
Hölder for the $dr$ integral, Proposition 6.21 (both inequalities) for the stochastic integral, and the Lipschitz bounds give
$$
w(t)\le3^{p-1}\Big(E|\eta-\eta'|^p+\big(T^{p-1}+c_pT^{\frac{p-2}2}\big)L^p\int_v^tE|D_r|^pdr\Big)\le3^{p-1}E|\eta-\eta'|^p+C_2\int_v^tw(r)\,dr .
$$
By Gronwall, $w(T)\le3^{p-1}e^{C_2T}E|\eta-\eta'|^p=:C_1E|\eta-\eta'|^p$.

*Step 2.* Subtracting the equation for $\xi^1$ at times $t\ge v$ and $v$:
$$
\xi^1_t=\xi^1_v+\int_v^tb(\xi^1_r,r)dr+\int_v^t\sigma(\xi^1_r,r)dB_r,\qquad t\ge v,
$$
and $\xi^1_v$ is $\mathcal F_v$-measurable and in $L^p$ (Theorem 8.10). So $\xi^1$ restricted to $[v,T]$ is the solution starting at time $v$ from $\xi^1_v$.

*Step 3.* By Step 1 with $\eta=\xi^1_v$, $\eta'=\eta_2$, and by the second estimate of Theorem 8.10 (for the solution started at $(\eta_1,u)$, evaluated at $t=v$):
$$
\begin{aligned}
E\sup_{v\le t\le T}|\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t|^p&\le C_1E|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p\le C_12^{p-1}\big(E|\xi^1_v-\eta_1|^p+E|\eta_1-\eta_2|^p\big)\\
&\le C_12^{p-1}\big(c(v-u)^{p/2}(1+E|\eta_1|^p)+E|\eta_1-\eta_2|^p\big)\\
&\le c'\big(1+E|\eta_1|^p\big)\big(|u-v|^{p/2}+E|\eta_1-\eta_2|^p\big). \qquad\square
\end{aligned}
$$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Statement 1.5; Step 1 2.5; Step 2 1; Step 3 2. The idea of "restarting" $\xi^1$ at time $v$ is what makes the different starting times manageable; students who only treat $u=v$ get at most 4 points.

**Time budget:** 15 minutes.

#### 4(c) Theorem 8.25 (weak existence via Girsanov)

**Assumption 8.24.** $\sigma:\mathbb R^m\times[0,T]\to\mathbb R^{m\times m}$ is measurable, symmetric, locally Lipschitz in $x$ uniformly in $t$, with $|\sigma(x,t)|\le C(1+|x|)$, and every eigenvalue of $\sigma(x,t)$ is at least $\lambda>0$, uniformly in $(x,t)$; in particular $\sigma^{-1}$ exists with operator norm $\le1/\lambda$. The drift $b:\mathbb R^m\times[0,T]\to\mathbb R^m$ is bounded and measurable.

**Statement (existence part).** Under Assumption 8.24, $d\xi_t=b(\xi_t,t)dt+\sigma(\xi_t,t)dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$, has a weak solution on $[0,T]$.

**Roadmap.** Solve the driftless equation strongly. Choose $\Phi=\sigma^{-1}b$ along the solution; it is bounded, so the stochastic exponential is a martingale. Under the new measure, $B=\tilde B+\int\Phi\,ds$ with $\tilde B$ Brownian, and substituting gives the equation with drift $b$.

**Proof.** *Step 1.* The driftless equation $d\xi_t=\sigma(\xi_t,t)dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$, has locally Lipschitz coefficients with linear growth, so by Theorem 8.17 (quoted as a tool; the slides state this step as "strong existence" for the driftless equation) it has a (strong) solution $\xi$ on $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),P)$ driven by the $P$-Brownian motion $B$.

*Step 2.* Let $\Phi_t=\sigma(\xi_t,t)^{-1}b(\xi_t,t)$. It is progressively measurable (measurable function of the continuous adapted $\xi$ and of $t$) and $|\Phi_t|\le\|b\|_\infty/\lambda$. Hence $\int_0^T|\Phi_t|^2dt\le T\|b\|_\infty^2/\lambda^2$, and by Proposition 7.3 (or Novikov)
$$
Z_t=\exp\Big(\int_0^t\Phi_s\cdot dB_s-\frac12\int_0^t|\Phi_s|^2ds\Big)
$$
is a martingale on $[0,T]$. Define $d\tilde P=Z_T\,dP$ on $\mathcal F_T$.

*Step 3.* By Girsanov (Theorem 7.5), $\tilde B_t=B_t-\int_0^t\Phi_sds$ is an $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion under $\tilde P$ on $[0,T]$.

*Step 4.* Since $\tilde P\sim P$, the stochastic integral $\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)dB_s$ (a limit in $P$-probability, hence in $\tilde P$-probability, of integrals of elementary processes) satisfies, under $\tilde P$, $\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)dB_s=\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)d\tilde B_s+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\Phi_sds$ (the diffusion coefficient is unchanged, the drift becomes $\sigma\Phi$; see "the drift of an Itô process after a measure change"). As $\sigma(\xi_s,s)\Phi_s=b(\xi_s,s)$,
$$
\xi_t=x+\int_0^tb(\xi_s,s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\,d\tilde B_s,\qquad0\le t\le T,\ \tilde P\text{-a.s.}
$$
The integrability conditions ($b(\xi,\cdot)\in M^1_{loc}$, $\sigma(\xi,\cdot)\in M^2_{loc}$) are pathwise and are unaffected by the equivalent change of measure. Hence $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),\tilde P,\xi,\tilde B)$ is a weak solution. $\square$

*(Remark: $\xi$ is in general not adapted to the filtration of $\tilde B$; that is why this is only a weak solution. Uniqueness in law, the other half of Theorem 8.25, is not required.)*

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Assumption 1.5; Steps 1–3 4 (boundedness of $\Phi$ is what gives the martingale property); Step 4 1.5. The common error is the sign: $\tilde B=B-\int\Phi$, so $dB=d\tilde B+\Phi\,dt$ and the drift added is $\sigma\Phi=b$.

### Problem 5

#### 5(a) Hitting time of a Brownian motion with drift

Fix $\alpha>0$ and let $\lambda=-\mu+\sqrt{\mu^2+2\alpha}>0$, the positive root of $\lambda\mu+\frac12\lambda^2=\alpha$. The process
$$
N_t=\exp\Big(\lambda B_t-\frac{\lambda^2}2t\Big)=\exp\Big(\lambda X_t-\big(\lambda\mu+\tfrac{\lambda^2}2\big)t\Big)=\exp(\lambda X_t-\alpha t)
$$
is a continuous martingale (Example 4.31). $\tau_a$ is a stopping time (hitting time of the closed set $\{a\}$ by a continuous process, equivalently the exit time of the open set $]-\infty,a[$, Proposition 1.27(a)). By Corollary 4.34, $N_{t\wedge\tau_a}$ is a martingale, so $E[N_{t\wedge\tau_a}]=1$. For $t<\tau_a$ we have $X_t<a$, hence (since $\lambda>0$, $\alpha>0$)
$$
0<N_{t\wedge\tau_a}\le e^{\lambda a}.
$$
As $t\to\infty$: on $\{\tau_a<\infty\}$, $N_{t\wedge\tau_a}\to e^{\lambda a-\alpha\tau_a}$ (using $X_{\tau_a}=a$ by continuity); on $\{\tau_a=\infty\}$, $N_t\le e^{\lambda a-\alpha t}\to0$. By dominated convergence,
$$
E\big[e^{\lambda a-\alpha\tau_a}\mathbf 1_{\{\tau_a<\infty\}}\big]=1,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad E\big[e^{-\alpha\tau_a}\mathbf 1_{\{\tau_a<\infty\}}\big]=e^{-\lambda a}=\exp\Big(-a\big(\sqrt{\mu^2+2\alpha}-\mu\big)\Big).
$$

*$P(\tau_a<\infty)$.* As $\alpha\downarrow0$, $e^{-\alpha\tau_a}\mathbf 1_{\{\tau_a<\infty\}}\uparrow\mathbf 1_{\{\tau_a<\infty\}}$; by monotone convergence
$$
P(\tau_a<\infty)=\exp\big(-a(|\mu|-\mu)\big)=\begin{cases}1,&\mu\ge0,\\ e^{2\mu a},&\mu<0.\end{cases}
$$

*$E[\tau_a]$ for $\mu>0$.* Then $\tau_a<\infty$ a.s. Let $\psi(\alpha)=a(\sqrt{\mu^2+2\alpha}-\mu)$, so $E[e^{-\alpha\tau_a}]=e^{-\psi(\alpha)}$ and $\psi(0)=0$. Since $\alpha\mapsto(1-e^{-\alpha\tau_a})/\alpha$ increases to $\tau_a$ as $\alpha\downarrow0$ (the function $x\mapsto(1-e^{-x})/x$ is decreasing), monotone convergence gives
$$
E[\tau_a]=\lim_{\alpha\downarrow0}\frac{1-e^{-\psi(\alpha)}}\alpha=\psi'(0)=\frac{a}{\sqrt{\mu^2+2\alpha}}\Big|_{\alpha=0}=\frac a\mu .
$$
For $\mu=0$: $\psi(\alpha)=a\sqrt{2\alpha}$ and $(1-e^{-a\sqrt{2\alpha}})/\alpha\sim a\sqrt2/\sqrt\alpha\to\infty$, so $E[\tau_a]=\infty$ (although $\tau_a<\infty$ a.s.).

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** Choice of $\lambda$ and the martingale 2; bounded stopped martingale and the limit on both events 3; $P(\tau_a<\infty)$ 1.5; $E[\tau_a]$ with justification 1.5. Taking the negative root $\lambda<0$ destroys the bound $N_{t\wedge\tau_a}\le e^{\lambda a}$; the event $\{\tau_a=\infty\}$ must be treated (it has positive probability when $\mu<0$).

**Time budget:** 15 minutes.

#### 5(b) Brownian bridge

(i) For $t<1$, $s\mapsto(1-s)^{-1}$ is bounded on $[0,t]$, so $U_t=\int_0^t\frac{dB_s}{1-s}$ is well defined, $dU_t=\frac{dB_t}{1-t}$. By the product rule with the deterministic $C^1$ factor $1-t$ (no covariation):
$$
dZ_t=-U_t\,dt+(1-t)\frac{dB_t}{1-t}=-\frac{Z_t}{1-t}\,dt+dB_t,\qquad Z_0=0 .
$$

(ii) For $t_1<\dots<t_n<1$, $(Z_{t_1},\dots,Z_{t_n})$ is a linear image of the Wiener integrals $(U_{t_1},\dots,U_{t_n})$, which are jointly centered Gaussian (Proposition 5.19). For $s\le t<1$, by the polarized isometry,
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(Z_s,Z_t)=(1-s)(1-t)\int_0^s\frac{du}{(1-u)^2}=(1-s)(1-t)\Big(\frac1{1-s}-1\Big)=s(1-t).
$$

(iii) $E[Z_t^2]=t(1-t)\to0$ as $t\to1$, i.e. $Z_t\to0$ in $L^2$.

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** 2 points each. The integral $\int_0^s(1-u)^{-2}du=\frac1{1-s}-1=\frac s{1-s}$ is where arithmetic slips occur.

#### 5(c) Representation of $\int_0^TB_s^2ds$

(i) As in Exam 1, Problem 2(b),
$$
M_t=E\Big[\int_0^TB_s^2ds\ \Big|\ \mathcal F_t\Big]=\int_0^tB_s^2ds+(T-t)B_t^2+\frac{(T-t)^2}2 .
$$
Its stochastic differential, by Itô ($d(B_t^2)=2B_tdB_t+dt$) and the product rule with the $C^1$ factor $T-t$:
$$
dM_t=B_t^2dt-B_t^2dt+(T-t)(2B_tdB_t+dt)-(T-t)dt=2(T-t)B_t\,dB_t .
$$
Since $M_0=\frac{T^2}2$ and $M_T=\int_0^TB_s^2ds$:
$$
\int_0^TB_s^2ds=\frac{T^2}2+\int_0^T2(T-t)B_t\,dB_t,
$$
with $H_t=2(T-t)B_t\in M^2([0,T])$ and $c=\frac{T^2}2=E\big[\int_0^TB_s^2ds\big]$.

(ii) Itô/product rule for $(T-t)B_t^2$: $d\big((T-t)B_t^2\big)=-B_t^2dt+(T-t)(2B_tdB_t+dt)$. Integrating over $[0,T]$ (both endpoint values are $0$):
$$
0=-\int_0^TB_t^2dt+\int_0^T2(T-t)B_tdB_t+\int_0^T(T-t)dt\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\int_0^TB_t^2dt=\frac{T^2}2+\int_0^T2(T-t)B_tdB_t .
$$

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** (i) 4 points (conditional expectation 2, differential 2), (ii) 2 points. ### Problem 6

**(a) False.** Take $Y\in L^1\setminus L^2$ (e.g. $Y$ with density proportional to $(1+|x|)^{-5/2}$: the mean is finite since $\int^\infty x^{-3/2}dx<\infty$, the variance infinite since $\int^\infty x^{-1/2}dx=\infty$) and the constant filtration $\mathcal F_n=\mathcal F$. Then $M_n=E[Y\mid\mathcal F_n]=Y$ for all $n$: a martingale, uniformly integrable (a single integrable variable, Example 4.21), but $E[M_n^2]=\infty$.

**(b) False.** Tanaka's equation $dX=\operatorname{sgn}(X)dW$, $X_0=0$ (Lemma 8.27): weak solutions exist, pathwise uniqueness fails ($\beta$ and $-\beta$), but every solution is a Brownian motion, so uniqueness in law holds.

**(c) False.** Let $\xi_k$ be i.i.d. $\pm1$ with probability $\frac12$, $S_n=\xi_1+\dots+\xi_n$ and $\tau=\inf\{n:S_n=1\}$, a.s. finite (Baldi, Exercise 5.2: from Example 4.15, $P(S_{\tau_{a,1}}=1)=\frac a{a+1}\to1$ as $a\to\infty$). With $\tau_1=0\le\tau_2=\tau$: $E[S_\tau\mid\mathcal F_0]=1\ne0=S_0$. (Same with Brownian motion and $\tau=\inf\{t:B_t=1\}$.)

**(d) True.** For $t\in[0,T]$, the supermartingale property gives $1=E[Z_0]\ge E[Z_t]\ge E[Z_T]=1$, so $E[Z_t]=1$ is constant on $[0,T]$. A supermartingale with constant expectation is a martingale (Baldi, Exercise 5.1).

**Grading notes / pitfalls.** 1 + 1 points each. In (a), a counterexample must be concrete enough to verify; the constant filtration trick is the fastest. In (d), the monotonicity of $t\mapsto E[Z_t]$ for supermartingales is the key.
