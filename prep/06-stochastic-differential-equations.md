## 2. Core definitions and facts

### 2.1 Setting

Let $0\le u\le T$, state dimension $m$, noise dimension $d$. The coefficients are measurable functions
$$
b:\mathbb R^m\times[0,T]\to\mathbb R^m,\qquad \sigma:\mathbb R^m\times[0,T]\to\mathbb R^{m\times d}.
$$
The SDE is
$$
d\xi_t=b(\xi_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(\xi_t,t)\,dB_t,\qquad \xi_u=x .
$$
$b$ is the **drift**, $\sigma$ the **diffusion coefficient**. The differential notation is shorthand for the integral equation
$$
\xi_t=x+\int_u^t b(\xi_s,s)\,ds+\int_u^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\,dB_s,\qquad u\le t\le T .
$$

Recall the integrand classes: $M^p_{loc}([u,T])$ is the class of progressively measurable processes $X$ with $\int_u^T|X_s|^p\,ds<\infty$ a.s.; $M^p([u,T])$ requires $E\int_u^T|X_s|^p\,ds<\infty$.

### 2.2 Weak solution (Definition 8.1)

A **(weak) solution** of the SDE with initial condition $\xi_u=x$ is a tuple $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),P,\xi,B)$ consisting of a filtered probability space and two processes such that

(a) $B$ is a standard $d$-dimensional $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion;

(b) $\xi$ is continuous and adapted (hence progressively measurable), $b(\xi_\cdot,\cdot)\in M^1_{loc}$, $\sigma(\xi_\cdot,\cdot)\in M^2_{loc}$, and for every $t\in[u,T]$, almost surely,
$$
\xi_t=x+\int_u^t b(\xi_s,s)\,ds+\int_u^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\,dB_s .
$$

**Why "weak":** the probability space, the filtration and the Brownian motion are **part of the solution**; they are not prescribed in advance. The integrability requirements are exactly what is needed for both integrals to exist on every finite interval.

Since both sides of the integral equation are continuous in $t$, "for every $t$, a.s." automatically upgrades to "a.s., for every $t$".

### 2.3 Strong solution (Definition 8.2, Baldi)

The SDE **has strong solutions** if, for **every** standard $d$-dimensional Brownian motion $B$ on **every prescribed** filtered probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),P)$, there exists an adapted continuous process $\xi$ satisfying the integral equation on $[u,T]$, with the integrability requirements of Definition 8.1.

So for strong solutions the probability space and the Brownian motion are **given**; only $\xi$ is to be found.

### 2.4 Strong solution (Karatzas–Shreve, notes page 8.14)

Let $(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)$ carry a standard Brownian motion $B$, and let $(\overline{\mathcal F}^B_t)$ be the **augmented natural filtration** of $B$ (generated by $B_s$, $s\le t$, plus the $P$-null sets). A continuous process $X$ is a **strong solution** (in the sense of Karatzas–Shreve) if

1. $X$ is $(\overline{\mathcal F}^B_t)$-adapted;
2. $P(X_0=x)=1$;
3. $\int_0^t\big(|b(X_s,s)|+|\sigma(X_s,s)|^2\big)\,ds<\infty$ a.s. for every $t$;
4. a.s., for all $t$: $X_t=x+\int_0^t b(X_s,s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma(X_s,s)\,dB_s$.

(The notes have the $ds$ and $dB_s$ swapped in item 4 by a slip of the pen. The version above is the correct one.)

So a K–S strong solution is a solution that is a **non-anticipative functional of the driving Brownian path**: the solution is "produced by the noise alone".

**Equivalence with Baldi's definition (notes page 8.15).**

- *Baldi $\Rightarrow$ K–S.* Take any Brownian motion $B$ and equip its space with the filtration $(\overline{\mathcal F}^B_t)$; $B$ is a Brownian motion for it. Baldi's definition says there is a solution adapted to **this** prescribed filtration, which is exactly a K–S strong solution.
- *K–S $\Rightarrow$ Baldi.* Let $(X,B)$ be a K–S strong solution on some space. Since $X$ is $\overline{\mathcal F}^B$-adapted, there is a measurable, **non-anticipative** map $\Phi:C([0,T];\mathbb R^d)\to C([0,T];\mathbb R^m)$ with $X=\Phi(B)$ a.s. (functional representation / Doob–Dynkin lemma; "non-anticipative" means that $\Phi(w)_t$ only depends on $(w_s)_{s\le t}$). Now take **any** prescribed filtered space with a Brownian motion $\tilde B$ and define $\tilde X=\Phi(\tilde B)$. Then:
  - $\tilde X$ is adapted to $\overline{\mathcal F}^{\tilde B}_t\subseteq\mathcal F_t$ (under the usual conditions), hence to the prescribed filtration;
  - $(\tilde X,\tilde B)$ has the same law as $(X,B)$, because it is the same measurable function of a process with the same law;
  - the SDE identity transfers to the new space: the stochastic integral $\int_0^t\sigma(X_s,s)\,dB_s$ is a limit in probability of integrals of adapted elementary integrands, which are explicit functionals of the pair $(X,B)$; equality of joint laws therefore transfers the identity $X_t=x+\int b\,ds+\int\sigma\,dB$ to $(\tilde X,\tilde B)$ (same argument as in Lemma 8.21).

  Hence $\tilde X$ is a solution on the prescribed space, which is Baldi's definition.

### 2.5 Uniqueness notions (Definitions 8.3, 8.4)

If $\xi$ is a solution (weak or strong), the map $\omega\mapsto\xi_\cdot(\omega)\in C([u,T];\mathbb R^m)$ is measurable and the **law** of $\xi$ is the image measure $P\circ\xi^{-1}$ on path space.

- **Uniqueness in law (Def 8.3):** any two solutions with the same deterministic initial condition have the same law on $C([u,T];\mathbb R^m)$. The two solutions may live on **different** probability spaces with **different** Brownian motions.
- **Pathwise uniqueness (Def 8.4):** any two solutions $\xi,\tilde\xi$ on the **same** filtered probability space, driven by the **same** Brownian motion and with the same initial condition, are indistinguishable: $P(\xi_t=\tilde\xi_t\ \forall t\in[u,T])=1$.

**Relations (slide 9 and standard facts):**

- Strong existence $\Rightarrow$ weak existence (trivially).
- **Yamada–Watanabe:** weak existence + pathwise uniqueness $\Rightarrow$ strong existence + uniqueness in law.
- Uniqueness in law does **not** imply pathwise uniqueness, and weak existence does **not** imply strong existence: **Tanaka's example** (Lemma 8.27).
- Strong existence does **not** imply uniqueness in law: $dX=\mathbf 1_{\{X\neq0\}}\,dB$, $X_0=0$ (notes page 8.15) has the two strong solutions $X\equiv0$ and $X=B$.

Summary table:

| Equation | Weak existence | Uniqueness in law | Pathwise uniqueness | Strong existence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lipschitz + linear growth (A) | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| $dX=b(X)dt+dB$, $b$ bounded measurable | yes (8.25) | yes (8.25) | (yes, Zvonkin–Veretennikov; not in syllabus) | (yes; not in syllabus) |
| Tanaka $dX=\operatorname{sgn}(X)\,dW$ | yes | yes | **no** | **no** |
| $dX=\mathbf 1_{\{X\neq0\}}dB$, $X_0=0$ | yes | **no** | **no** | yes |
| $dX=3X^{1/3}dt+3X^{2/3}dB$, $X_0=0$ | yes | **no** | **no** | yes |

### 2.6 Itô's formula and the generator (Remark 8.5)

Let $a=\sigma\sigma^T$ ($m\times m$, symmetric, positive semidefinite) and
$$
L_t=\frac12\sum_{i,j=1}^m a_{ij}(x,t)\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}+\sum_{i=1}^m b_i(x,t)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}.
$$
For $f\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb R^m\times[0,T])$ and a solution $\xi$,
$$
df(\xi_t,t)=(\partial_t f+L_tf)(\xi_t,t)\,dt+\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^d\partial_if(\xi_t,t)\,\sigma_{ij}(\xi_t,t)\,dB^j_t .
$$
If $\partial_tf+L_tf=0$, then $f(\xi_t,t)$ is a **local martingale** (a true martingale if the $dB$-integrand is in $M^2$). For $m$-dimensional Brownian motion ($b=0,\sigma=I_m$), $L=\frac12\Delta$.

In dimension one: $L=\frac12\sigma(x,t)^2\,\partial_{xx}+b(x,t)\,\partial_x$.

### 2.7 Examples 8.6 and 8.7

**Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (Example 8.6).** $d\xi_t=-\lambda\xi_t\,dt+\sigma\,dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$, $\lambda,\sigma\in\mathbb R$:
$$
\xi_t=e^{-\lambda t}x+e^{-\lambda t}\int_0^te^{\lambda s}\sigma\,dB_s .
$$
It is a Gaussian process. *Derivation by variation of constants (notes page 8.1):* the homogeneous equation $d\xi=-\lambda\xi\,dt$ has solution $xe^{-\lambda t}$. Look for $\xi_t=e^{-\lambda t}Z_t$. By the product rule, $d(e^{-\lambda t}Z_t)=-\lambda e^{-\lambda t}Z_t\,dt+e^{-\lambda t}dZ_t$ (the bracket vanishes because $e^{-\lambda t}$ has finite variation). This equals $-\lambda\xi_t\,dt+\sigma\,dB_t$ iff $e^{-\lambda t}dZ_t=\sigma\,dB_t$, i.e. $Z_t=x+\int_0^t\sigma e^{\lambda s}dB_s$.

Multidimensional version: $d\xi_t=-\Lambda\xi_t\,dt+\Sigma\,dB_t$ has solution $\xi_t=e^{-t\Lambda}x+e^{-t\Lambda}\int_0^te^{s\Lambda}\Sigma\,dB_s$ (matrix exponentials).

**Geometric Brownian motion (Example 8.7).** $d\xi_t=b\xi_t\,dt+\sigma\xi_t\,dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$:
$$
\xi_t=x\exp\Big(\big(b-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\big)t+\sigma B_t\Big).
$$
If $x>0$ the paths stay positive and $\xi_t$ is lognormal. One cannot apply Itô's formula to $\log\xi$ **before** knowing $\xi>0$ (log is not $C^2$ on $\mathbb R$). The correct route: **guess** the formula from a formal computation and then **verify** it with Itô's formula applied to the exponential, which is $C^2$ on all of $\mathbb R$. Uniqueness then follows from Theorem 8.13 (the coefficients are Lipschitz).

### 2.8 Assumption (A) (Assumption 8.8)

$b,\sigma$ are measurable in $(x,t)$, and there are constants $M,L>0$ such that for all $x,y\in\mathbb R^m$ and $t\in[0,T]$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
&|b(x,t)|\le M(1+|x|),\qquad |\sigma(x,t)|\le M(1+|x|) &&\text{(linear growth)}\\
&|b(x,t)-b(y,t)|\le L|x-y|,\qquad |\sigma(x,t)-\sigma(y,t)|\le L|x-y| &&\text{(global Lipschitz)}
\end{aligned}
$$
Both bounds are **uniform in time**. Remarks:

- The a priori bounds (Theorem 8.10) need only measurability + linear growth.
- For **time-independent** coefficients, Lipschitz implies linear growth: $|b(x)|\le|b(0)|+L|x|\le\max(|b(0)|,L)(1+|x|)$. For time-dependent coefficients one also needs $\sup_t|b(0,t)|<\infty$ (and likewise for $\sigma$). Example: $b(x,t)=x+\frac1{T-t}$ on $[0,T)$ is Lipschitz in $x$ uniformly in $t$, but not of linear growth uniformly in $t$.
- $|\sigma|$ denotes any matrix norm; the Hilbert–Schmidt norm $|\sigma|^2=\sum_{ij}\sigma_{ij}^2$ is the natural one since $E|\int\sigma\,dB|^2=E\int|\sigma|^2ds$.

**Elementary inequality** used constantly: for $p\ge1$ and $x_1,\dots,x_n\in\mathbb R^m$,
$$
\Big|\sum_{i=1}^nx_i\Big|^p\le n^{p-1}\sum_{i=1}^n|x_i|^p
$$
(convexity of $|\cdot|^p$ / Jensen). In particular $(1+|x|)^p\le2^{p-1}(1+|x|^p)$.

**Hölder for time integrals:** $\big|\int_u^tf(r)\,dr\big|^p\le(t-u)^{p-1}\int_u^t|f(r)|^p\,dr$.

**Maximal $L^p$ estimate (Proposition 6.21):** for $p\ge2$, $X\in M^2_{loc}$,
$$
E\Big[\sup_{u\le s\le t}\Big|\int_u^sX_r\,dB_r\Big|^p\Big]\le c_p\,E\Big[\Big(\int_u^t|X_r|^2dr\Big)^{p/2}\Big]\le c_p\,(t-u)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\,E\int_u^t|X_r|^p\,dr .
$$
For $p=2$ this is Doob's inequality: $E[\sup_{s\le t}|\int_0^sX\,dB|^2]\le4E\int_0^t|X_r|^2dr$.

### 2.9 Gronwall's lemma (Lemma 8.9)

Let $w,v:[a,b]\to[0,\infty)$, $w$ integrable, $v$ **bounded** and measurable. If $c\ge0$ and
$$
v(t)\le c+\int_a^tw(s)v(s)\,ds,\qquad a\le t\le b,
$$
then $v(t)\le c\exp\big(\int_a^tw(s)\,ds\big)$. In particular, $c=0$ implies $v\equiv0$.

*Proof (short, worth knowing).* Put $V(t)=c+\int_a^tw(s)v(s)\,ds$; this is finite because $v$ is bounded and $w$ integrable, and it is absolutely continuous with $V'(t)=w(t)v(t)\le w(t)V(t)$ for a.e. $t$. Hence $\frac{d}{dt}\big(V(t)e^{-\int_a^tw}\big)=(V'-wV)e^{-\int_a^tw}\le0$ a.e., so $V(t)e^{-\int_a^tw}\le V(a)=c$. Thus $v(t)\le V(t)\le ce^{\int_a^tw}$. $\square$

**Why "bounded" matters:** the boundedness of $v$ is what makes $V$ finite. In the SDE proofs this is the reason one must first show that $v(t)=E[\sup_{s\le t}|\cdot|^p]$ is finite, by **localization** (Theorem 8.10) or by **already having** Theorem 8.10 (Theorems 8.13, 8.22).

### 2.10 Remarks 8.11, 8.12, 8.14

- **Remark 8.11.** If $\eta\in L^2$, every solution is in $M^2([u,T])$ (by Theorem 8.10), and so is $s\mapsto\sigma(\xi_s,s)$ (linear growth). Hence $\int_u^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\,dB_s$ is a square-integrable martingale and
$$
E[\xi_t]=E[\eta]+E\int_u^tb(\xi_s,s)\,ds .
$$
The drift gives the local direction of the mean motion; $\sigma$ produces zero-mean fluctuations.
- **Remark 8.12 (exit probability).** With $\tau_R=\inf\{t\ge u:|\xi_t|\ge R\}\wedge T$, Markov's inequality and Theorem 8.10 give
$$
P\Big(\sup_{u\le t\le T}|\xi_t|\ge R\Big)\le\frac{c(p,T,M)(1+E|\eta|^p)}{R^p},
$$
so $P(\tau_R<T)\to0$ as $R\to\infty$, uniformly for $\eta$ in bounded sets of $L^p$, independently of any Lipschitz constant.
- **Remark 8.14 (filtration of a solution).** Under (A), with $u=0$ and $\eta=x$ deterministic, let $\mathcal H_t=\sigma(\xi_s:s\le t)$ and $\mathcal G_t$ the augmented natural filtration of $B$. Then $\mathcal H_t\subseteq\mathcal G_t$: the solution of Theorem 8.13, constructed on the space with filtration $(\mathcal G_t)$, is $\mathcal G_t$-adapted, and by pathwise uniqueness every solution coincides with it. So under (A) the solution is a K–S strong solution. The reverse inclusion can fail (e.g. if some columns of $\sigma$ vanish, the corresponding Brownian components are invisible in $\xi$).

### 2.11 Random initial data

An $\mathcal F_u$-measurable initial variable is independent of the future increments $(B_t-B_u)_{t\ge u}$. Conversely, at time $0$, if $\eta$ is independent of $B$, one may enlarge the filtration to $\mathcal F'_t=\sigma(B_r:r\le t)\vee\sigma(\eta)$ (augmented); $B$ remains a Brownian motion for $(\mathcal F'_t)$.

### 2.12 Cameron–Martin setting (Assumption 8.24)

- $\sigma:\mathbb R^m\times[0,T]\to\mathbb R^{m\times m}$ measurable, **symmetric**, locally Lipschitz in $x$ uniformly in $t$, with $|\sigma(x,t)|\le C(1+|x|)$;
- **uniform ellipticity:** for some $\lambda>0$ every eigenvalue of $\sigma(x,t)$ is $\ge\lambda$, uniformly in $(x,t)$. Hence $\sigma^{-1}$ exists, is symmetric with eigenvalues in $(0,1/\lambda]$, and $\|\sigma(x,t)^{-1}\|_{op}\le1/\lambda$;
- $b:\mathbb R^m\times[0,T]\to\mathbb R^m$ **bounded and measurable** (no continuity at all).

The most important special case: $\sigma=I_m$ (so $\lambda=1$), $b$ bounded measurable: $dX_t=b(X_t,t)\,dt+dB_t$.

### 2.13 Typical non-examples and the role of each hypothesis

- **Lipschitz fails (non-uniqueness).** ODE $x'=2\sqrt{|x|}$, $x(0)=0$: solutions $x\equiv0$ and $x_a(t)=(t-a)^2\mathbf 1_{\{t\ge a\}}$ for every $a\ge0$. SDE $dX=3X^{1/3}dt+3X^{2/3}dB$, $X_0=0$: solutions $X\equiv0$ and $X=B^3$.
- **Linear growth fails (explosion).** ODE $x'=x^2$, $x(0)=1$: $x(t)=1/(1-t)$ explodes at $t=1$. SDE $d\xi=\xi^3dt+\xi^2dB$, $\xi_0=x$: $\xi_t=x/(1-xB_t)$ explodes when $B$ hits $1/x$.
- **Continuity fails, but weak solution still exists:** $b$ bounded measurable (Theorem 8.25), $\sigma=\operatorname{sgn}$ (Tanaka).

## 3. Theorems: statements

### Lemma 8.9 (Gronwall)
See Section 2.9.

### Theorem 8.10 (A priori moment bounds)
Assume $b,\sigma$ are measurable and satisfy the linear growth bounds $|b(x,t)|,|\sigma(x,t)|\le M(1+|x|)$. Let $p\ge2$ and let $\eta\in L^p$ be $\mathcal F_u$-measurable. If $\xi$ is a (continuous) solution of
$$
\xi_t=\eta+\int_u^tb(\xi_s,s)\,ds+\int_u^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\,dB_s,\qquad u\le t\le T,
$$
then there is a constant $c=c(p,T,M)$ such that
$$
E\Big[\sup_{u\le s\le T}|\xi_s|^p\Big]\le c\,(1+E|\eta|^p),
$$
$$
E\Big[\sup_{u\le s\le t}|\xi_s-\eta|^p\Big]\le c\,(t-u)^{p/2}\,(1+E|\eta|^p),\qquad u\le t\le T .
$$
The constants do **not** depend on any Lipschitz constant, nor on $u$, nor on the particular solution.

### Theorem 8.13 (Strong existence and pathwise uniqueness)
Under Assumption 8.8, let $0\le u\le T$ and let $\eta$ be an $\mathbb R^m$-valued, square-integrable, $\mathcal F_u$-measurable random variable. Then, on the prescribed filtered probability space carrying the Brownian motion $B$, there exists a continuous adapted process $\xi\in M^2([u,T])$ such that
$$
\xi_t=\eta+\int_u^tb(\xi_s,s)\,ds+\int_u^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\,dB_s,\qquad u\le t\le T .
$$
If $\tilde\xi$ is another solution with the same initial variable and the same driving Brownian motion, then $P(\xi_t=\tilde\xi_t\text{ for every }t\in[u,T])=1$.

### Theorem 8.20 (Uniqueness of the joint law)
Assume (A') (local Lipschitz + linear growth; in particular (A) suffices). For $i=1,2$ let $B^i$ be standard $d$-dimensional Brownian motions on possibly different filtered probability spaces, let $\eta^i\in L^2(\mathcal F^i_u)$ have the same law, and let $\xi^i$ solve $\xi^i_t=\eta^i+\int_u^tb(\xi^i_s,s)\,ds+\int_u^t\sigma(\xi^i_s,s)\,dB^i_s$. Then the pairs $\big((\xi^i_t)_{t\in[u,T]},(B^i_t-B^i_u)_{t\in[u,T]}\big)$, $i=1,2$, have the same law. In particular, uniqueness in law holds.

### Lemma 8.21 (Equality in law after integration)
Assume (A). Let $B^i$ be standard Brownian motions and $\eta^i\in L^2(\mathcal F^i_0)$, $i=1,2$. Let $\xi^i\in M^2_{loc,B^i}([0,T])$ be progressively measurable with $(\eta^1,\xi^1,B^1)\overset{law}{=}(\eta^2,\xi^2,B^2)$. Define $Y^i_t=\eta^i+\int_0^tb(\xi^i_s,s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi^i_s,s)\,dB^i_s$. Then $(\eta^1,Y^1,B^1)$ and $(\eta^2,Y^2,B^2)$ have the same finite-dimensional distributions.

### Theorem 8.22 (Stability with respect to initial data)
(Called Proposition 8.22 on the slides and Theorem 8.22 in the syllabus.) Under Assumption 8.8, let $\xi^1,\xi^2$ be solutions driven by the same Brownian motion with initial data $(\eta_1,u)$ and $(\eta_2,v)$, where $u\le v$, $\eta_1\in L^p(\mathcal F_u)$, $\eta_2\in L^p(\mathcal F_v)$, $p\ge2$. That is, $\xi^1_t=\eta_1+\int_u^tb(\xi^1_s,s)ds+\int_u^t\sigma(\xi^1_s,s)dB_s$ for $t\ge u$ and $\xi^2_t=\eta_2+\int_v^tb(\xi^2_s,s)ds+\int_v^t\sigma(\xi^2_s,s)dB_s$ for $t\ge v$. Then
$$
E\Big[\sup_{v\le t\le T}|\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t|^p\Big]\le c(L,T,M,p)\,\big(1+E|\eta_1|^p\big)\,\big(|u-v|^{p/2}+E|\eta_1-\eta_2|^p\big).
$$

### Theorem 8.23 (A jointly continuous version)
Under Assumption 8.8 there is a family $(Z^{x,s}(t))_{x,s,t}$ such that (a) for every $\omega$, $(x,s,t)\mapsto Z^{x,s}(t,\omega)$ is continuous on $\{(x,s,t):x\in\mathbb R^m,0\le s\le t\}$; (b) for all fixed $x,s\le t$, $Z^{x,s}(t)=\xi^{x,s}_t$ a.s. Moreover, paths are locally Hölder continuous in time of every order $\gamma<\frac12$.

### Theorem 8.25 (Weak existence; the Girsanov construction)
Under Assumption 8.24, the equation
$$
d\xi_t=b(\xi_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(\xi_t,t)\,dB_t,\qquad \xi_0=x,
$$
admits a weak solution on $[0,T]$. (The slides add: there is uniqueness in law. That part is **not** examinable.)

### Proposition 8.26 (Cameron–Martin formula for path laws)
Let $\xi$ be the solution of the driftless equation $d\xi=\sigma(\xi_t,t)dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$ under $P$, and $Z_T=\exp\big(\int_0^T\Phi_s\cdot dB_s-\frac12\int_0^T|\Phi_s|^2ds\big)$ with $\Phi_s=\sigma(\xi_s,s)^{-1}b(\xi_s,s)$, $d\tilde P=Z_T\,dP$. For every Borel set $A\subseteq C([0,T];\mathbb R^m)$,
$$
\tilde P(\xi\in A)=E_P\big[Z_T\mathbf 1_{\{\xi\in A\}}\big],\qquad E_{\tilde P}[F(\xi)]=E_P[Z_TF(\xi)]
$$
for every bounded measurable path functional $F$. So the law of the solution **with** drift is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of the driftless solution (in fact equivalent, since $Z_T>0$).

### Lemma 8.27 (Tanaka's example; notes page 8.13)
Consider
$$
dX_t=\operatorname{sgn}(X_t)\,dW_t,\qquad X_0=0,\qquad \operatorname{sgn}(x)=\begin{cases}-1,&x\le0,\\ \ \ 1,&x>0.\end{cases}
$$
Then:
1. there exists a weak solution;
2. uniqueness in law holds (every solution is a Brownian motion);
3. pathwise uniqueness **fails**;
4. there is **no** strong solution.

### Example (notes page 8.15): no uniqueness in law
$dX_t=\mathbf 1_{\{X_t\neq0\}}\,dB_t$, $X_0=0$, has the two strong solutions $X^{(1)}\equiv0$ and $X^{(2)}=B$, which have different laws.

## 4. Proofs

Throughout, $|\cdot|$ is the Euclidean norm on vectors and the Hilbert–Schmidt norm on matrices. Constants $c,c_1,c_2,\dots$ may change from line to line but depend only on the indicated parameters.

### 4.1 Proof of Theorem 8.10 (a priori moment bounds)

**Proof roadmap.**

- Step 1 (idea): show that $v(t)=E[\sup_{u\le s\le t}|\xi_s|^p]$ satisfies $v(t)\le c_1(1+E|\eta|^p)+c_2\int_u^tv(r)\,dr$, then apply Gronwall.
- Step 2 (difficulty): Gronwall needs $v$ **bounded**, which we do not know. Fix this by **localization**: stop $\xi$ at $\tau_R$, the exit time from the ball of radius $R$.
- Step 3: estimate the stopped process by the elementary inequality, Hölder (drift) and the maximal $L^p$ estimate (stochastic integral), plus linear growth. Gronwall gives a bound **independent of $R$**.
- Step 4: let $R\to\infty$ with monotone convergence. Then the second inequality follows by the same estimates applied to $\xi-\eta$.

**Proof.** *Localization.* For $R>0$ let
$$
\tau_R=\inf\{t\in[u,T]:|\xi_t|\ge R\}\wedge T,\qquad \inf\emptyset=+\infty,
$$
the exit time of $\xi$ from the open ball of radius $R$ (a stopping time, since $\xi$ is continuous and adapted). Note $\tau_R=T$ if $|\xi_t|<R$ for all $t\in[u,T]$. Set $\xi_R(t)=\xi_{t\wedge\tau_R}$. By the integral equation and integration up to a stopping time (Theorem 5.21),
$$
\begin{aligned}
\xi_R(t)&=\eta+\int_u^{t\wedge\tau_R}b(\xi_r,r)\,dr+\int_u^{t\wedge\tau_R}\sigma(\xi_r,r)\,dB_r\\
&=\eta+\int_u^tb(\xi_r,r)\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}\,dr+\int_u^t\sigma(\xi_r,r)\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}\,dB_r\\
&=\eta+\int_u^tb(\xi_R(r),r)\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}\,dr+\int_u^t\sigma(\xi_R(r),r)\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}\,dB_r ,
\end{aligned}
$$
where in the last line we used $\xi_r=\xi_R(r)$ for $r<\tau_R$.

*Estimate.* Take $\sup_{u\le s\le t}$, the $p$-th power, expectations, and $|x_1+x_2+x_3|^p\le3^{p-1}(|x_1|^p+|x_2|^p+|x_3|^p)$:
$$
E\Big[\sup_{u\le s\le t}|\xi_R(s)|^p\Big]\le3^{p-1}\big(A_1+A_2+A_3\big),
$$
with
$$
A_1=E|\eta|^p,\quad A_2=E\Big[\sup_{u\le s\le t}\Big|\int_u^sb(\xi_R(r),r)\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}dr\Big|^p\Big],\quad A_3=E\Big[\sup_{u\le s\le t}\Big|\int_u^s\sigma(\xi_R(r),r)\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}dB_r\Big|^p\Big].
$$
*Drift term.* By Hölder's inequality and linear growth,
$$
A_2\le(t-u)^{p-1}E\int_u^t|b(\xi_R(r),r)|^p\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}dr\le T^{p-1}M^pE\int_u^t(1+|\xi_R(r)|)^p\,dr .
$$
*Stochastic term.* The integrand is bounded on $\{r<\tau_R\}$: there $|\xi_R(r)|\le R$ (or $|\xi_R(r)|=|\eta|$ if $|\eta|\ge R$, but then $\tau_R=u$ and the integrand is $0$), so it is in $M^p$. By the maximal $L^p$ estimate (Proposition 6.21) and linear growth,
$$
A_3\le c_p(t-u)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}E\int_u^t|\sigma(\xi_R(r),r)|^p\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}dr\le c_pT^{\frac{p-2}{2}}M^pE\int_u^t(1+|\xi_R(r)|)^p\,dr .
$$
Using $(1+|x|)^p\le2^{p-1}(1+|x|^p)$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
E\Big[\sup_{u\le s\le t}|\xi_R(s)|^p\Big]&\le3^{p-1}\Big(E|\eta|^p+M^p\big(T^{p-1}+c_pT^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\big)2^{p-1}\Big(T+\int_u^tE|\xi_R(r)|^p\,dr\Big)\Big)\\
&\le c_1(p,T,M)\big(1+E|\eta|^p\big)+c_2(p,T,M)\int_u^tE|\xi_R(r)|^p\,dr .
\end{aligned}
$$
Let $v_R(t)=E[\sup_{u\le s\le t}|\xi_R(s)|^p]$. Since $E|\xi_R(r)|^p\le v_R(r)$,
$$
v_R(t)\le c_1(1+E|\eta|^p)+c_2\int_u^tv_R(r)\,dr,\qquad u\le t\le T .
$$
*Boundedness of $v_R$.* If $|\eta|\ge R$ then $\tau_R=u$ and $\xi_R\equiv\eta$; otherwise, by continuity, $|\xi_R(t)|\le R$ for all $t$. Hence $|\xi_R(t)|\le R\vee|\eta|$ and
$$
v_R(t)\le E[R^p\vee|\eta|^p]\le R^p+E|\eta|^p<\infty .
$$
*Gronwall.* Lemma 8.9 (with $w\equiv c_2$) gives
$$
v_R(T)=E\Big[\sup_{u\le s\le T}|\xi_R(s)|^p\Big]\le c_1(1+E|\eta|^p)e^{c_2T}=:c(p,T,M)(1+E|\eta|^p),
$$
and this bound does **not depend on $R$**.

*Removing the localization.* If $R_1<R_2$ then $\tau_{R_1}\le\tau_{R_2}$, so $R\mapsto\tau_R$ is non-decreasing. Moreover, on $\{\tau_R<T\}$, $|\xi_{\tau_R}|=R$ by continuity, so $\sup_{u\le t\le T}|\xi_R(t)|^p\ge R^p$ there, and
$$
P(\tau_R<T)\le\frac1{R^p}E\Big[\sup_{u\le t\le T}|\xi_R(t)|^p\Big]\le\frac{c(1+E|\eta|^p)}{R^p}\xrightarrow[R\to\infty]{}0 .
$$
Since $\tau_R$ is non-decreasing in $R$, the events $\{\tau_R<T\}$ decrease, and $P(\tau_R<T\ \forall R)=0$: on a set of probability one there is $R_0(\omega)$ with $\tau_R(\omega)=T$ for all $R\ge R_0(\omega)$. (Equivalently: a continuous path is bounded on $[u,T]$, so $\tau_R=T$ once $R>\sup_{[u,T]}|\xi|$.) Consequently
$$
\sup_{u\le s\le T}|\xi_R(s)|=\sup_{u\le s\le\tau_R}|\xi_s|\ \uparrow\ \sup_{u\le s\le T}|\xi_s|\quad\text{a.s. as }R\uparrow\infty,
$$
and by monotone convergence
$$
E\Big[\sup_{u\le s\le T}|\xi_s|^p\Big]=\lim_{R\to\infty}E\Big[\sup_{u\le s\le T}|\xi_R(s)|^p\Big]\le c(p,T,M)(1+E|\eta|^p).
$$
*Second inequality.* Now we know $E[\sup|\xi|^p]<\infty$, so no localization is needed. Since $\xi_s-\eta=\int_u^sb(\xi_r,r)dr+\int_u^s\sigma(\xi_r,r)dB_r$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
E\Big[\sup_{u\le s\le t}|\xi_s-\eta|^p\Big]&\le2^{p-1}\Big((t-u)^{p-1}E\int_u^t|b(\xi_r,r)|^pdr+c_p(t-u)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}E\int_u^t|\sigma(\xi_r,r)|^pdr\Big)\\
&\le2^{p-1}M^p2^{p-1}\big((t-u)^{p-1}+c_p(t-u)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\big)\int_u^t\big(1+E|\xi_r|^p\big)dr\\
&\le c\,\big((t-u)^{p}+(t-u)^{p/2}\big)\big(1+c(1+E|\eta|^p)\big)\\
&\le c(p,T,M)\,(t-u)^{p/2}\,(1+E|\eta|^p),
\end{aligned}
$$
using $(t-u)^p=(t-u)^{p/2}(t-u)^{p/2}\le T^{p/2}(t-u)^{p/2}$ in the last step. $\square$

**What graders look for / pitfalls.**

- **The localization is the whole point.** Writing "$v(t)\le c_1+c_2\int v$, so by Gronwall..." without first proving $v<\infty$ is a serious gap: Gronwall requires $v$ bounded. Say explicitly why $v_R\le R^p+E|\eta|^p$.
- Correct use of Theorem 5.21: $\int_u^{t\wedge\tau}X\,dB=\int_u^tX\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau\}}dB$. Do not forget the indicator.
- Hölder gives the factor $(t-u)^{p-1}$ (not $(t-u)^p$); the maximal inequality gives $c_p(t-u)^{(p-2)/2}$.
- Only **linear growth** is used. If you invoke a Lipschitz constant, the result is weaker than stated and graders will notice: the remark "constants do not depend on $L$" is part of the theorem.
- Pass to the limit with **monotone convergence** (the sups increase in $R$), not dominated convergence (there is no dominating function yet).
- $p\ge2$ is needed for the maximal inequality in the form used.

**Memorize-this skeleton.**

1. $\tau_R=\inf\{t:|\xi_t|\ge R\}\wedge T$, $\xi_R=\xi_{\cdot\wedge\tau_R}=\eta+\int b(\xi_R)\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}dr+\int\sigma(\xi_R)\mathbf 1_{\{r<\tau_R\}}dB$.
2. $E\sup|\xi_R|^p\le3^{p-1}\big(E|\eta|^p+T^{p-1}E\int|b|^p+c_pT^{(p-2)/2}E\int|\sigma|^p\big)\le c_1(1+E|\eta|^p)+c_2\int_u^tv_R$.
3. $v_R\le R^p+E|\eta|^p<\infty$; Gronwall: $v_R(T)\le c_1e^{c_2T}(1+E|\eta|^p)$, uniform in $R$.
4. $\tau_R\uparrow T$ (eventually $=T$) a.s.; monotone convergence.
5. Second bound: same estimates on $\xi-\eta$, plug in the first bound, $(t-u)^p\le T^{p/2}(t-u)^{p/2}$.

### 4.2 Proof of Theorem 8.13 (strong existence and pathwise uniqueness)

Assume $u=0$ (the general case is identical with $[u,T]$ in place of $[0,T]$).

**Proof roadmap.**

- Step 0: Picard iteration $\xi_0\equiv\eta$, $\xi_{m+1}=\eta+\int b(\xi_m)ds+\int\sigma(\xi_m)dB$; check each iterate is well defined, continuous and in $M^2$.
- Step 1: by induction, $E[\sup_{s\le t}|\xi_{m+1}(s)-\xi_m(s)|^2]\le(Rt)^{m+1}/(m+1)!$ (Hölder for the drift, Doob for the stochastic integral, Lipschitz).
- Step 2: Markov + Borel–Cantelli give a.s. uniform convergence on $[0,T]$ to a continuous adapted $\xi$, which is also in $M^2$.
- Step 3: pass to the limit in the recursion (drift: uniform convergence; stochastic integral: Proposition 5.27, convergence in probability), so $\xi$ is a solution.
- Step 4: pathwise uniqueness: $v(t)=E[\sup_{s\le t}|\xi^1_s-\xi^2_s|^2]$ is bounded by Theorem 8.10 and satisfies $v\le c\int_0^tv$, so Gronwall with $c=0$ gives $v\equiv0$.

**Proof.** *Main inequalities.* If $Y\in M^2([0,T])$, linear growth gives $|b(x,s)|^2\le M^2(1+|x|)^2\le2M^2(1+|x|^2)$, hence
$$
E\int_0^t|b(Y_s,s)|^2ds\le2M^2\Big(t+E\int_0^t|Y_s|^2ds\Big),
$$
and by Doob's maximal inequality and the isometry,
$$
E\Big[\sup_{0\le r\le t}\Big|\int_0^r\sigma(Y_s,s)\,dB_s\Big|^2\Big]\le4E\int_0^t|\sigma(Y_s,s)|^2ds\le8M^2\Big(t+E\int_0^t|Y_s|^2ds\Big).
$$
In particular, if $Y\in M^2$ is continuous and adapted, then $\eta+\int_0^\cdot b(Y_s,s)ds+\int_0^\cdot\sigma(Y_s,s)dB_s$ is well defined (since $b(Y,\cdot)\in M^2\subseteq M^1$ and $\sigma(Y,\cdot)\in M^2$), continuous, adapted, and has finite $E[\sup_{t\le T}|\cdot|^2]$, so it is again in $M^2$.

*Step 0: Picard iteration.* Define
$$
\xi_0(t)\equiv\eta,\qquad \xi_{m+1}(t)=\eta+\int_0^tb(\xi_m(s),s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_m(s),s)\,dB_s .
$$
$\xi_0\in M^2$ since $\eta\in L^2$, so by the remark above and induction every $\xi_m$ is a well-defined continuous adapted process in $M^2([0,T])$.

*Step 1: the factorial bound.* We show by induction that for some constant $R>0$,
$$
E\Big[\sup_{0\le s\le t}|\xi_{m+1}(s)-\xi_m(s)|^2\Big]\le\frac{(Rt)^{m+1}}{(m+1)!},\qquad 0\le t\le T,\ m\ge0 .
$$
*Case $m=0$.* By $|x+y|^2\le2|x|^2+2|y|^2$ and Hölder (Cauchy–Schwarz),
$$
\sup_{0\le s\le t}|\xi_1(s)-\eta|^2\le2t\int_0^t|b(\eta,s)|^2ds+2\sup_{0\le s\le t}\Big|\int_0^s\sigma(\eta,r)\,dB_r\Big|^2 .
$$
Taking expectations and using the main inequalities with $Y\equiv\eta$,
$$
E\Big[\sup_{0\le s\le t}|\xi_1(s)-\eta|^2\Big]\le4M^2t\big(t+tE|\eta|^2\big)+16M^2\big(t+tE|\eta|^2\big)\le16M^2(T+1)(1+E|\eta|^2)\,t .
$$
*Induction step.* Assume the bound for $m-1$. Then
$$
\sup_{0\le s\le t}|\xi_{m+1}(s)-\xi_m(s)|^2\le2A_1+2A_2,
$$
$$
A_1=\sup_{0\le s\le t}\Big|\int_0^s\big(b(\xi_m(r),r)-b(\xi_{m-1}(r),r)\big)dr\Big|^2,\qquad A_2=\sup_{0\le s\le t}\Big|\int_0^s\big(\sigma(\xi_m(r),r)-\sigma(\xi_{m-1}(r),r)\big)dB_r\Big|^2 .
$$
By Hölder and the Lipschitz condition,
$$
A_1\le t\int_0^t|b(\xi_m(r),r)-b(\xi_{m-1}(r),r)|^2dr\le TL^2\int_0^t|\xi_m(r)-\xi_{m-1}(r)|^2dr,
$$
and by Doob's inequality and the Lipschitz condition,
$$
E[A_2]\le4E\int_0^t|\sigma(\xi_m(r),r)-\sigma(\xi_{m-1}(r),r)|^2dr\le4L^2\int_0^tE|\xi_m(r)-\xi_{m-1}(r)|^2dr .
$$
Choose
$$
R=\max\big\{16M^2(T+1)(1+E|\eta|^2),\ 2L^2(T+4)\big\}.
$$
Then, bounding $E|\xi_m(r)-\xi_{m-1}(r)|^2\le E[\sup_{s\le r}|\xi_m(s)-\xi_{m-1}(s)|^2]$ and using the induction hypothesis,
$$
E\Big[\sup_{0\le s\le t}|\xi_{m+1}(s)-\xi_m(s)|^2\Big]\le(2TL^2+8L^2)\int_0^tE|\xi_m(r)-\xi_{m-1}(r)|^2dr\le R\int_0^t\frac{(Rr)^m}{m!}dr=\frac{(Rt)^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}.
$$

*Step 2: uniform convergence.* By Markov's (Chebyshev's) inequality,
$$
P\Big(\sup_{0\le t\le T}|\xi_{m+1}(t)-\xi_m(t)|>2^{-m}\Big)\le2^{2m}E\Big[\sup_{0\le t\le T}|\xi_{m+1}(t)-\xi_m(t)|^2\Big]\le\frac{4^m(RT)^{m+1}}{(m+1)!},
$$
which is summable in $m$ (ratio test: the ratio of consecutive terms is $4RT/(m+2)\to0$). By Borel–Cantelli,
$$
P\Big(\sup_{0\le t\le T}|\xi_{m+1}(t)-\xi_m(t)|>2^{-m}\text{ infinitely often}\Big)=0 .
$$
So for a.e. $\omega$ there is $m_0(\omega)$ with $\sup_{t\le T}|\xi_{m+1}(t)-\xi_m(t)|\le2^{-m}$ for $m\ge m_0(\omega)$, and the series
$$
\xi_m(t)=\eta+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\big(\xi_{k+1}(t)-\xi_k(t)\big)
$$
converges uniformly on $[0,T]$, a.s. Let $\xi_t=\lim_m\xi_m(t)$ (and $\xi=0$ on the exceptional null set). As a uniform limit of continuous adapted processes, $\xi$ is continuous and adapted (the filtration is complete), hence progressively measurable.

Moreover $\xi\in M^2$: by Minkowski in $L^2(\Omega)$,
$$
\Big\|\sup_{t\le T}|\xi_t-\xi_m(t)|\Big\|_{L^2}\le\sum_{k\ge m}\Big\|\sup_{t\le T}|\xi_{k+1}(t)-\xi_k(t)|\Big\|_{L^2}\le\sum_{k\ge m}\sqrt{\frac{(RT)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}}\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}0,
$$
so $E[\sup_{t\le T}|\xi_t|^2]<\infty$ (alternatively, apply Theorem 8.10 once Step 3 shows that $\xi$ is a solution).

*Step 3: the limit is a solution.* Recall
$$
\xi_{m+1}(t)=\eta+\int_0^tb(\xi_m(s),s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_m(s),s)\,dB_s .
$$
The left-hand side converges (uniformly, a.s.) to $\xi_t$. By the Lipschitz condition,
$$
\sup_{0\le t\le T}|b(\xi_m(t),t)-b(\xi_t,t)|\le L\sup_{0\le t\le T}|\xi_m(t)-\xi_t|\to0\quad\text{a.s.},
$$
and similarly for $\sigma$. Hence $\int_0^tb(\xi_m(s),s)ds\to\int_0^tb(\xi_s,s)ds$ a.s., and
$$
\int_0^T|\sigma(\xi_m(s),s)-\sigma(\xi_s,s)|^2ds\le L^2T\sup_{s\le T}|\xi_m(s)-\xi_s|^2\to0\quad\text{a.s., hence in probability.}
$$
By Proposition 5.27 (convergence in probability of stochastic integrals),
$$
\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_m(s),s)\,dB_s\xrightarrow{P}\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\,dB_s .
$$
Limits in probability are a.s. unique, so for each $t$,
$$
\xi_t=\eta+\int_0^tb(\xi_s,s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\,dB_s\quad\text{a.s.};
$$
both sides are continuous in $t$, so they are indistinguishable. Thus $\xi$ is a solution, constructed on the prescribed space from the given $B$: a strong solution.

*Step 4: pathwise uniqueness.* Let $\xi^1,\xi^2$ be two solutions on the same space, with the same $B$ and the same $\eta\in L^2$. Subtracting the equations, using $|x+y|^2\le2|x|^2+2|y|^2$, Hölder, Doob and the Lipschitz condition,
$$
\begin{aligned}
E\Big[\sup_{0\le s\le t}|\xi^1_s-\xi^2_s|^2\Big]&\le2E\Big[\sup_{s\le t}\Big|\int_0^s\big(b(\xi^1_r,r)-b(\xi^2_r,r)\big)dr\Big|^2\Big]+2E\Big[\sup_{s\le t}\Big|\int_0^s\big(\sigma(\xi^1_r,r)-\sigma(\xi^2_r,r)\big)dB_r\Big|^2\Big]\\
&\le2tE\int_0^t|b(\xi^1_r,r)-b(\xi^2_r,r)|^2dr+8E\int_0^t|\sigma(\xi^1_r,r)-\sigma(\xi^2_r,r)|^2dr\\
&\le L^2(2T+8)\int_0^tE|\xi^1_r-\xi^2_r|^2dr .
\end{aligned}
$$
Let $v(t)=E[\sup_{0\le s\le t}|\xi^1_s-\xi^2_s|^2]$. By Theorem 8.10 (with $p=2$; only linear growth is needed), $v(t)\le2E[\sup|\xi^1|^2]+2E[\sup|\xi^2|^2]\le4c(1+E|\eta|^2)<\infty$, so $v$ is bounded, and
$$
v(t)\le L^2(2T+8)\int_0^tv(r)\,dr,\qquad0\le t\le T .
$$
Gronwall with $c=0$ gives $v\equiv0$, in particular $E[\sup_{t\le T}|\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t|^2]=0$, i.e. $\xi^1$ and $\xi^2$ are indistinguishable. $\square$

**What graders look for / pitfalls.**

- **Well-posedness of each iterate.** Say why $\int\sigma(\xi_m,s)\,dB_s$ makes sense: $\xi_m\in M^2$ and linear growth give $\sigma(\xi_m,\cdot)\in M^2$.
- **The factorial.** The estimate must be in terms of $E[\sup]$ (needed for uniform convergence), and the induction uses $E|\xi_m(r)-\xi_{m-1}(r)|^2\le E\sup_{s\le r}|\cdots|^2$. The $1/(m+1)!$ comes from iterating $\int_0^t r^m\,dr$.
- **Doob, not just the Itô isometry**, for the stochastic integral term with a sup: constant $4$.
- **Borel–Cantelli** needs a summable series; show it (ratio test or comparison with $e^{4RT}$).
- **Limit of the stochastic integrals**: almost sure uniform convergence of $\xi_m$ does not by itself give a.s. convergence of $\int\sigma(\xi_m)dB$. Use Proposition 5.27 (or the isometry plus $L^2$ convergence) and then identify limits in probability.
- **Uniqueness needs boundedness of $v$**: cite Theorem 8.10. Without it, Gronwall does not apply.
- "Same Brownian motion, same space, same initial condition" must be stated in the uniqueness part (that is what *pathwise* means).
- The solution is adapted to the filtration of the prescribed space: this is why it is a **strong** solution. Under deterministic $\eta=x$ it is even adapted to the Brownian filtration (Remark 8.14).

**Memorize-this skeleton.**

1. $\xi_0=\eta$, $\xi_{m+1}=\eta+\int_0^tb(\xi_m)ds+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_m)dB$; all in $M^2$ by linear growth.
2. $E\sup_{s\le t}|\xi_{m+1}-\xi_m|^2\le2TL^2\int_0^tE|\xi_m-\xi_{m-1}|^2+8L^2\int_0^tE|\xi_m-\xi_{m-1}|^2\Rightarrow\le(Rt)^{m+1}/(m+1)!$.
3. $P(\sup|\xi_{m+1}-\xi_m|>2^{-m})\le4^m(RT)^{m+1}/(m+1)!$ summable $\Rightarrow$ Borel–Cantelli $\Rightarrow$ uniform a.s. limit $\xi$, continuous, adapted, in $M^2$.
4. Drift: $|b(\xi_m)-b(\xi)|\le L\sup|\xi_m-\xi|$. Stochastic integral: $\int|\sigma(\xi_m)-\sigma(\xi)|^2\to0\Rightarrow$ Prop 5.27. Limit equation holds.
5. Uniqueness: $v(t)\le L^2(2T+8)\int_0^tv$, $v$ bounded by 8.10 $\Rightarrow v\equiv0$.

### 4.3 Proof of Theorem 8.22 (stability with respect to initial data)

**Proof roadmap.**

- Step 1: on $[v,T]$ both processes solve the SDE from time $v$; write $\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t=(\xi^1_v-\eta_2)+\int_v^t(\Delta b)dr+\int_v^t(\Delta\sigma)dB$.
- Step 2: Hölder + maximal $L^p$ estimate + Lipschitz give $f(s)\le3^{p-1}E|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p+c\int_v^sf(r)dr$ with $f(s)=E\sup_{v\le t\le s}|\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t|^p$, which is bounded by Theorem 8.10.
- Step 3: Gronwall: $f(T)\le cE|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p$.
- Step 4: split $\xi^1_v-\eta_2=(\xi^1_v-\eta_1)+(\eta_1-\eta_2)$ and use the **second** inequality of Theorem 8.10 to get $E|\xi^1_v-\eta_1|^p\le c(1+E|\eta_1|^p)(v-u)^{p/2}$.

**Proof.** For $t\ge v$, since $\xi^1_t=\xi^1_v+\int_v^tb(\xi^1_r,r)dr+\int_v^t\sigma(\xi^1_r,r)dB_r$ (subtract the equation at time $v$ from the one at time $t$),
$$
\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t=\big(\xi^1_v-\eta_2\big)+\int_v^t\big(b(\xi^1_r,r)-b(\xi^2_r,r)\big)dr+\int_v^t\big(\sigma(\xi^1_r,r)-\sigma(\xi^2_r,r)\big)dB_r .
$$
For $v\le s\le T$, by the elementary inequality with $n=3$, Hölder in the Lebesgue integral, the maximal $L^p$ estimate (Proposition 6.21) in the stochastic integral, and the Lipschitz condition,
$$
\begin{aligned}
E\Big[\sup_{v\le t\le s}|\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t|^p\Big]&\le3^{p-1}E|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p+3^{p-1}T^{p-1}E\int_v^s|b(\xi^1_r,r)-b(\xi^2_r,r)|^pdr\\
&\quad+3^{p-1}c_pT^{\frac{p-2}{2}}E\int_v^s|\sigma(\xi^1_r,r)-\sigma(\xi^2_r,r)|^pdr\\
&\le3^{p-1}E|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p+c(L,T,p)\int_v^sE\Big[\sup_{v\le t\le r}|\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t|^p\Big]dr .
\end{aligned}
$$
The function $f(s)=E[\sup_{v\le t\le s}|\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t|^p]$ is bounded: $f(s)\le2^{p-1}\big(E\sup|\xi^1|^p+E\sup|\xi^2|^p\big)<\infty$ by Theorem 8.10 ($\eta_1,\eta_2\in L^p$). Gronwall's lemma yields
$$
E\Big[\sup_{v\le t\le T}|\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t|^p\Big]\le3^{p-1}e^{c(L,T,p)T}\,E|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p .
$$
Now, by the elementary inequality and the **second** inequality of Theorem 8.10 applied to $\xi^1$ (started at time $u$ from $\eta_1$, evaluated at time $v$),
$$
\begin{aligned}
E|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p&\le2^{p-1}\big(E|\xi^1_v-\eta_1|^p+E|\eta_1-\eta_2|^p\big)\\
&\le2^{p-1}\big(c(p,T,M)(1+E|\eta_1|^p)\,|v-u|^{p/2}+E|\eta_1-\eta_2|^p\big)\\
&\le c'(p,T,M)\,(1+E|\eta_1|^p)\big(|v-u|^{p/2}+E|\eta_1-\eta_2|^p\big),
\end{aligned}
$$
using $1\le1+E|\eta_1|^p$ for the last term. Combining,
$$
E\Big[\sup_{v\le t\le T}|\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t|^p\Big]\le c(L,T,M,p)\,(1+E|\eta_1|^p)\big(|u-v|^{p/2}+E|\eta_1-\eta_2|^p\big).\qquad\square
$$

**What graders look for / pitfalls.**

- Recognise that **after time $v$** both processes solve the **same** equation; the "initial condition" of $\xi^1$ at time $v$ is the random variable $\xi^1_v$, which is $\mathcal F_v$-measurable.
- The Gronwall step gives a bound **proportional to** $E|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p$ (the handwritten notes write $c(1+E|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p)$, which is a slip; with "$1+$" the estimate would be useless).
- Boundedness of $f$ for Gronwall: cite Theorem 8.10, which needs $\eta_1,\eta_2\in L^p$.
- Use the **second** a priori bound for $E|\xi^1_v-\eta_1|^p\le c(1+E|\eta_1|^p)(v-u)^{p/2}$; this is where the factor $|u-v|^{p/2}$ (Brownian scaling) comes from.
- Here the Lipschitz constant **is** used (difference of coefficients), so $c$ depends on $L$.

**Memorize-this skeleton.**

1. $t\ge v$: $\xi^1_t-\xi^2_t=(\xi^1_v-\eta_2)+\int_v^t\Delta b\,dr+\int_v^t\Delta\sigma\,dB$.
2. $f(s)\le3^{p-1}E|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p+c\int_v^sf$, $f$ bounded (8.10) $\Rightarrow f(T)\le cE|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p$.
3. $E|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p\le2^{p-1}(E|\xi^1_v-\eta_1|^p+E|\eta_1-\eta_2|^p)\le c(1+E|\eta_1|^p)(|v-u|^{p/2}+E|\eta_1-\eta_2|^p)$.

### 4.4 Proof of Theorem 8.25 (weak existence via Girsanov)

**Proof roadmap.**

- Step 1: solve the **driftless** equation $d\xi=\sigma(\xi_t,t)dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$, on some filtered space (strong existence for locally Lipschitz $\sigma$ with linear growth; for $\sigma=I$ simply $\xi=x+B$).
- Step 2: $\Phi_t=\sigma(\xi_t,t)^{-1}b(\xi_t,t)$ is progressively measurable and **bounded** by $\|b\|_\infty/\lambda$; by the bounded-energy criterion (Proposition 7.3) the stochastic exponential $Z$ is a true martingale; define $d\tilde P=Z_TdP$.
- Step 3: Girsanov (Theorem 7.5): $\tilde B_t=B_t-\int_0^t\Phi_sds$ is a $\tilde P$-Brownian motion.
- Step 4: substitute $dB=d\tilde B+\Phi\,dt$: $d\xi=\sigma\Phi\,dt+\sigma\,d\tilde B=b\,dt+\sigma\,d\tilde B$; so $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),\tilde P,\xi,\tilde B)$ is a weak solution.

**Proof.** *Step 1.* Under Assumption 8.24, $\sigma$ is locally Lipschitz in $x$ uniformly in $t$ and has linear growth, so (with zero drift) the driftless equation
$$
d\xi_t=\sigma(\xi_t,t)\,dB_t,\qquad\xi_0=x,
$$
has a strong solution on any filtered space $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),P)$ carrying an $m$-dimensional Brownian motion $B$ (Theorem 8.17; if $\sigma$ is globally Lipschitz, Theorem 8.13 suffices; if $\sigma=I_m$ the solution is just $\xi_t=x+B_t$).

*Step 2.* Define
$$
\Phi_t=\sigma(\xi_t,t)^{-1}b(\xi_t,t),\qquad0\le t\le T .
$$
$\Phi$ is progressively measurable (a Borel function of $(\xi_t,t)$ with $\xi$ continuous and adapted; matrix inversion is continuous on invertible matrices). Since $\sigma(x,t)$ is symmetric with all eigenvalues $\ge\lambda>0$, $\sigma(x,t)^{-1}$ is symmetric with eigenvalues in $(0,1/\lambda]$, so $|\sigma(x,t)^{-1}y|\le|y|/\lambda$. With $K=\sup|b|<\infty$,
$$
|\Phi_t|\le\frac K\lambda,\qquad\int_0^T|\Phi_s|^2ds\le\frac{K^2T}{\lambda^2}\quad\text{(a deterministic constant).}
$$
By the bounded-energy criterion (Proposition 7.3), the stochastic exponential
$$
Z_t=\exp\Big(\int_0^t\Phi_s\cdot dB_s-\frac12\int_0^t|\Phi_s|^2ds\Big),\qquad0\le t\le T,
$$
is a (true) martingale, so $E_P[Z_T]=Z_0=1$. Since also $Z_T>0$, $d\tilde P=Z_T\,dP$ defines a probability measure on $(\Omega,\mathcal F_T)$ that is **equivalent** to $P$.

*Step 3.* By Girsanov's theorem (Theorem 7.5),
$$
\tilde B_t=B_t-\int_0^t\Phi_s\,ds=B_t-\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)^{-1}b(\xi_s,s)\,ds,\qquad0\le t\le T,
$$
is an $m$-dimensional $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion under $\tilde P$.

*Step 4.* Under $P$, $\xi_t=x+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\,dB_s$. Write $B=\tilde B+\int_0^\cdot\Phi_sds$. Since $\tilde P\sim P$, the stochastic integral computed under $P$ coincides $\tilde P$-a.s. with the one computed under $\tilde P$ (stochastic integrals are limits in probability of elementary integrals, and convergence in probability is preserved under an equivalent change of measure; this is the "drift of an Itô process after a measure change" on slides 7). Hence, $\tilde P$-a.s., for all $t$:
$$
\xi_t=x+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\,d\tilde B_s+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\Phi_s\,ds=x+\int_0^tb(\xi_s,s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma(\xi_s,s)\,d\tilde B_s,
$$
because $\sigma(\xi_s,s)\Phi_s=\sigma(\xi_s,s)\sigma(\xi_s,s)^{-1}b(\xi_s,s)=b(\xi_s,s)$. Integrability: $b(\xi,\cdot)$ is bounded, so in $M^1_{loc}$; $\int_0^T|\sigma(\xi_s,s)|^2ds<\infty$ $P$-a.s., hence $\tilde P$-a.s. (null sets are the same), so $\sigma(\xi,\cdot)\in M^2_{loc}$ under $\tilde P$. $\xi$ is continuous and $(\mathcal F_t)$-adapted, and $\tilde B$ is an $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion under $\tilde P$. Therefore
$$
\big(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),\tilde P,\ \xi,\ \tilde B\big)
$$
is a weak solution of $d\xi_t=b(\xi_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(\xi_t,t)\,dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$, on $[0,T]$. $\square$

**Remarks.**

- The solution is only **weak**: the driving Brownian motion $\tilde B$ is constructed from $\xi$ (and $B$), not prescribed; in general $\xi$ need not be adapted to the filtration of $\tilde B$.
- As a by-product, for every bounded measurable path functional $F$, $E_{\tilde P}[F(\xi)]=E_P[Z_TF(\xi)]$: this is the Cameron–Martin formula, Proposition 8.26.
- The *uniqueness in law* part (notes pages 8.10 to 8.12) runs Girsanov backwards on another solution and needs the joint law of $(\xi,Z_T)$; the notes point out that Baldi's argument (via Lemma 8.21) is not valid since Lemma 8.21 assumes (A). It is **not** examinable.

**What graders look for / pitfalls.**

- **Why $Z$ is a true martingale.** This is the key check: $\Phi$ is bounded because $b$ is bounded and $\sigma^{-1}$ is bounded by uniform ellipticity. Cite Proposition 7.3 (bounded energy) or Novikov ($E e^{\frac12\int|\Phi|^2}\le e^{K^2T/(2\lambda^2)}<\infty$). Without it, $\tilde P$ might not be a probability measure.
- Girsanov gives $\tilde B=B-\int\Phi\,ds$ (minus sign), so $B=\tilde B+\int\Phi\,ds$; mixing up the sign gives drift $-b$.
- State clearly **which** objects form the weak solution: the same $\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t)$, the **new** measure $\tilde P$, the **old** process $\xi$, the **new** Brownian motion $\tilde B$.
- Mention that the stochastic integral is unchanged under the equivalent measure change.
- No regularity of $b$ is needed: measurable and bounded is enough.

**Memorize-this skeleton.**

1. $d\xi=\sigma(\xi,t)dB$, $\xi_0=x$ under $P$ (for $\sigma=I$: $\xi=x+B$).
2. $\Phi=\sigma^{-1}b(\xi,t)$, $|\Phi|\le\|b\|_\infty/\lambda$ $\Rightarrow$ $Z_t=\mathcal E(\int\Phi\cdot dB)_t$ is a martingale (Prop 7.3); $d\tilde P=Z_TdP$.
3. Girsanov: $\tilde B=B-\int_0^\cdot\Phi\,ds$ is a $\tilde P$-BM.
4. $d\xi=\sigma(dB)=\sigma(d\tilde B+\Phi dt)=b\,dt+\sigma\,d\tilde B$. Weak solution $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),\tilde P,\xi,\tilde B)$.

### 4.5 Proof of Lemma 8.27 (Tanaka's example)

Recall $\operatorname{sgn}(x)=-1$ for $x\le0$ and $+1$ for $x>0$, so $\operatorname{sgn}(x)^2=1$ for **all** $x$. Equation: $dX_t=\operatorname{sgn}(X_t)\,dW_t$, $X_0=0$.

**Proof roadmap.**

- Weak existence: start from a Brownian motion $\beta$, **define** $W=\int\operatorname{sgn}(\beta)\,d\beta$; Lévy says $W$ is a BM, and $\int\operatorname{sgn}(\beta)\,dW=\int\operatorname{sgn}(\beta)^2d\beta=\beta$.
- Uniqueness in law: any solution is a continuous local martingale with $\langle X\rangle_t=t$, hence a BM by Lévy.
- No pathwise uniqueness: $-\beta$ solves the equation with the same $W$ (the set $\{s:\beta_s=0\}$ has Lebesgue measure zero).
- No strong solution: a strong solution would satisfy $\mathcal F^X\subseteq\mathcal F^W$, but by Tanaka's formula $W=|X|-L^0(X)$ is a functional of $|X|$, giving $\mathcal F^X\subseteq\mathcal F^{|X|}$, which is absurd for a Brownian motion.

**Proof.** *Facts used.* (i) Lévy's characterization: a continuous $(\mathcal F_t)$-local martingale $X$ with $X_0=0$ and $\langle X\rangle_t=t$ is an $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion (notes page 8.12; equivalently Theorem 4.42 via the exponential martingale $e^{i\lambda X_t+\frac12\lambda^2t}$). (ii) Associativity of stochastic integrals: if $dW=H\,d\beta$ then $\int K\,dW=\int KH\,d\beta$. (iii) For a Brownian motion $\beta$, the random set $\{s\le T:\beta_s=0\}$ has Lebesgue measure zero a.s., because by Fubini
$$
E\int_0^T\mathbf 1_{\{\beta_s=0\}}ds=\int_0^TP(\beta_s=0)\,ds=0 .
$$

**1. Weak existence.** Let $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),(\beta_t),P)$ be a Brownian motion and define
$$
W_t=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\,d\beta_s .
$$
The integrand is bounded and progressively measurable, hence in $M^2$, so $W$ is a continuous square-integrable martingale with
$$
\langle W\rangle_t=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)^2ds=\int_0^t1\,ds=t .
$$
By Lévy's characterization, $W$ is an $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motion under $P$. By associativity,
$$
\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\,dW_s=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\,d\beta_s=\int_0^t1\,d\beta_s=\beta_t .
$$
Hence $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),P,X=\beta,W)$ is a weak solution of $dX=\operatorname{sgn}(X)\,dW$, $X_0=0$.

**2. Uniqueness in law.** Let $(\Omega',\mathcal F',(\mathcal F'_t),P',X,W')$ be any weak solution: $X_t=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(X_s)\,dW'_s$. Then $X$ is a continuous $(\mathcal F'_t)$-(local) martingale with $X_0=0$ and
$$
\langle X\rangle_t=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(X_s)^2ds=t .
$$
By Lévy's characterization, $X$ is a Brownian motion. So **every** solution has the law of Brownian motion (Wiener measure): uniqueness in law holds.

**3. Pathwise uniqueness fails.** With $\beta,W$ as in part 1, put $\tilde\beta_t=-\beta_t$. For $x\neq0$, $\operatorname{sgn}(-x)=-\operatorname{sgn}(x)$; at $x=0$ both equal $-1$. Hence $\operatorname{sgn}(-\beta_s)+\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)=-2\cdot\mathbf 1_{\{\beta_s=0\}}$, and by the Itô isometry and fact (iii),
$$
E\Big[\Big(\int_0^t\big(\operatorname{sgn}(-\beta_s)+\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\big)dW_s\Big)^2\Big]=4E\int_0^t\mathbf 1_{\{\beta_s=0\}}ds=0 .
$$
Therefore, a.s.,
$$
\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\tilde\beta_s)\,dW_s=-\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\,dW_s=-\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)^2d\beta_s=-\beta_t=\tilde\beta_t ,
$$
so $\tilde\beta$ is also a solution, on the **same** space, with the **same** Brownian motion $W$ and the same initial value $0$. But
$$
P(\tilde\beta_t=\beta_t\ \ \forall t\in[0,T])=P(\beta_t=0\ \ \forall t\in[0,T])=0
$$
(e.g. $P(\beta_T=0)=0$). So $\beta$ and $\tilde\beta$ are not indistinguishable: pathwise uniqueness fails.

**4. No strong solution.** Suppose a strong solution exists: on the space of part 1, say, there is a continuous process $X$ adapted to the augmented filtration $(\overline{\mathcal F}^W_t)$ of $W$, with $X_t=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(X_s)\,dW_s$. (Under Baldi's definition, apply it to the prescribed filtration $(\overline{\mathcal F}^W_t)$; this is the "Baldi $\Rightarrow$ K–S" direction.) Then:

- By part 2, $X$ is a Brownian motion.
- By associativity, $\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(X_s)\,dX_s=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(X_s)^2dW_s=W_t$.
- **Tanaka's formula** for the Brownian motion $X$:
$$
|X_t|=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(X_s)\,dX_s+L^0_t(X),\qquad L^0_t(X)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\frac1{2\varepsilon}\lambda\big(\{s\in[0,t]:|X_s|\le\varepsilon\}\big),
$$
where $\lambda$ is Lebesgue measure and $L^0(X)$ is the **local time** of $X$ at $0$. (The value of $\operatorname{sgn}(0)$ does not matter, by fact (iii) and the isometry as in part 3.)

Hence $W_t=|X_t|-L^0_t(X)$. The local time $L^0_t(X)$ is a functional of $(|X_s|)_{s\le t}$ only, so $W_t$ is $\overline{\mathcal F}^{|X|}_t$-measurable: $\overline{\mathcal F}^W_t\subseteq\overline{\mathcal F}^{|X|}_t$. Combined with the strong-solution property,
$$
\overline{\mathcal F}^X_t\subseteq\overline{\mathcal F}^W_t\subseteq\overline{\mathcal F}^{|X|}_t .
$$
This is absurd: knowing $|X|$ on $[0,t]$ cannot determine the sign of $X_t$. Precisely: the event $\{X_t>0\}$ would be in $\overline{\mathcal F}^{|X|}_t$, so $\mathbf 1_{\{X_t>0\}}=g\big((|X_s|)_{s\le t}\big)$ a.s. for some measurable $g$ on path space. Since $X$ and $-X$ are both Brownian motions, $(X,|X|)$ and $(-X,|-X|)=(-X,|X|)$ have the same law, so also $\mathbf 1_{\{-X_t>0\}}=g\big((|X_s|)_{s\le t}\big)$ a.s. Thus $\mathbf 1_{\{X_t>0\}}=\mathbf 1_{\{X_t<0\}}$ a.s., which is impossible since $P(X_t=0)=0$ for $t>0$ (exactly one of the indicators equals $1$ a.s.). Hence no strong solution exists. $\square$

**Consistency with the general theory.** $\sigma=\operatorname{sgn}$ is not Lipschitz (not even continuous), so Theorem 8.13 does not apply. By Yamada–Watanabe, pathwise uniqueness would have given a strong solution; Tanaka's example shows that uniqueness in law is strictly weaker than pathwise uniqueness and that weak existence is strictly weaker than strong existence.

**What graders look for / pitfalls.**

- **Lévy's characterization** is invoked twice (for $W$ and for any solution $X$). State its hypotheses: continuous local martingale, starts at $0$, $\langle X\rangle_t=t$.
- **The convention $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=-1$** makes $\operatorname{sgn}^2\equiv1$. In part 3 you must handle the zero set: $\operatorname{sgn}(-x)=-\operatorname{sgn}(x)$ only for $x\neq0$, and $\{s:\beta_s=0\}$ is Lebesgue-null.
- In part 3, explain why $\beta$ and $-\beta$ are **not** indistinguishable.
- In part 4, the key steps are: strong $\Rightarrow\mathcal F^X\subseteq\mathcal F^W$; Tanaka's formula $\Rightarrow\mathcal F^W\subseteq\mathcal F^{|X|}$; contradiction because a Brownian motion takes both signs and $|X|$ forgets the sign.
- Do not claim Tanaka's example contradicts Theorem 8.13: the coefficient is discontinuous.

**Memorize-this skeleton.**

1. $W:=\int\operatorname{sgn}(\beta)d\beta$, $\langle W\rangle_t=t\Rightarrow$ BM (Lévy); $\int\operatorname{sgn}(\beta)dW=\int\operatorname{sgn}^2(\beta)d\beta=\beta$: weak solution.
2. Any solution: $\langle X\rangle_t=\int\operatorname{sgn}^2=t\Rightarrow$ BM: uniqueness in law.
3. $-\beta$ solves with the same $W$ ($\lambda\{s:\beta_s=0\}=0$), $P(\beta\equiv-\beta)=0$: no pathwise uniqueness.
4. Strong $\Rightarrow\mathcal F^X\subseteq\mathcal F^W$; $W=\int\operatorname{sgn}(X)dX=|X|-L^0(X)\Rightarrow\mathcal F^W\subseteq\mathcal F^{|X|}$; so $\mathcal F^X\subseteq\mathcal F^{|X|}$: absurd (sign of a BM is not a function of $|X|$).

### 4.6 The example $dX=\mathbf 1_{\{X\neq0\}}dB$ (notes page 8.15)

*Claim.* With $X_0=0$ there are two strong solutions with different laws.

- $X^{(1)}\equiv0$: $\int_0^t\mathbf 1_{\{0\neq0\}}dB_s=0=X^{(1)}_t$.
- $X^{(2)}=B$: $\int_0^t\mathbf 1_{\{B_s\neq0\}}dB_s=B_t-\int_0^t\mathbf 1_{\{B_s=0\}}dB_s$, and by the Itô isometry $E\big[\big(\int_0^t\mathbf 1_{\{B_s=0\}}dB_s\big)^2\big]=\int_0^tP(B_s=0)\,ds=0$. So $\int_0^t\mathbf 1_{\{B_s\neq0\}}dB_s=B_t$ a.s.

Both are adapted to the Brownian filtration (strong solutions) but have different laws ($\delta_0$ versus Wiener measure): **no uniqueness in law**, hence also no pathwise uniqueness. The coefficient $\mathbf 1_{\{x\neq0\}}$ is discontinuous at $0$.

## 5. Exercises

**General toolbox for linear SDEs.**

1. *Homogeneous multiplicative noise* $dX=bX\,dt+\kappa X\,dB$: the solution is $\Phi_t=\exp\big((b-\frac{\kappa^2}{2})t+\kappa B_t\big)$ times $x$.
2. *Additive noise* $dX=bX\,dt+c\,dB$ (or time-dependent $b(t),c(t)$): multiply by the integrating factor $e^{-\int_0^tb}$; the bracket term vanishes because the factor has finite variation.
3. *General affine* $dX=(a+bX)dt+(c+\kappa X)dB$: write $X=\Phi Y$ with $\Phi$ from (1), and find $dY$ by the product rule (variation of constants).
4. *Moments*: $m(t)=E X_t$ solves the ODE obtained by taking expectations (the $dB$ part is a true martingale, justified by Theorem 8.10 and Remark 8.11); for $E X_t^2$ apply Itô to $X^2$ first.
5. *Gaussianity*: $\int_0^tf(s)\,dB_s$ with **deterministic** $f\in L^2$ is $N(0,\int_0^tf^2)$, and finite families of such integrals are jointly Gaussian with $\operatorname{Cov}(\int_0^sf\,dB,\int_0^tg\,dB)=\int_0^{s\wedge t}fg$.

### Exercise 1 (★, 15 min). Geometric Brownian motion

Let $b,\sigma\in\mathbb R$, $x>0$, and $d\xi_t=b\xi_t\,dt+\sigma\xi_t\,dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$.

(a) Show that $\xi_t=x\exp\big((b-\frac{\sigma^2}2)t+\sigma B_t\big)$ is a solution and that it is the only one.
(b) Compute $E[\xi_t]$, $E[\xi_t^2]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\xi_t)$.
(c) Show that if $0<b<\sigma^2/2$, then $E[\xi_t]\to\infty$ but $\xi_t\to0$ a.s.

**Strategy.** Verify with Itô's formula applied to the **exponential** (a $C^2$ function on $\mathbb R$), not to $\log\xi$. Uniqueness: Theorem 8.13. Moments: Gaussian Laplace transform $E e^{\theta B_t}=e^{\theta^2t/2}$.

**Full solution.**
(a) Let $f(t,y)=x\exp\big((b-\frac{\sigma^2}2)t+\sigma y\big)$, a $C^{1,2}$ function on $[0,\infty)\times\mathbb R$ with $\partial_tf=(b-\frac{\sigma^2}2)f$, $\partial_yf=\sigma f$, $\partial_{yy}f=\sigma^2f$. By Itô's formula with $\xi_t=f(t,B_t)$,
$$
d\xi_t=\Big(b-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)\xi_t\,dt+\sigma\xi_t\,dB_t+\frac12\sigma^2\xi_t\,dt=b\xi_t\,dt+\sigma\xi_t\,dB_t,
$$
and $\xi_0=f(0,0)=x$. The coefficients $b(x)=bx$, $\sigma(x)=\sigma x$ satisfy $|bx-by|\le|b||x-y|$, $|\sigma x-\sigma y|\le|\sigma||x-y|$ and $|bx|\le|b|(1+|x|)$, so Assumption (A) holds with $L=M=\max(|b|,|\sigma|)$. By Theorem 8.13 the solution is pathwise unique (and it is strong, being a function of $B$).

(b) $E[\xi_t]=xe^{(b-\sigma^2/2)t}E[e^{\sigma B_t}]=xe^{(b-\sigma^2/2)t}e^{\sigma^2t/2}=xe^{bt}$. Next $\xi_t^2=x^2e^{(2b-\sigma^2)t+2\sigma B_t}$, so $E[\xi_t^2]=x^2e^{(2b-\sigma^2)t}e^{2\sigma^2t}=x^2e^{(2b+\sigma^2)t}$ and
$$
\operatorname{Var}(\xi_t)=x^2e^{2bt}\big(e^{\sigma^2t}-1\big).
$$
(Check via Remark 8.11: $m(t)=E\xi_t$ satisfies $m(t)=x+\int_0^tbm(s)ds$, so $m=xe^{bt}$.)

(c) $E\xi_t=xe^{bt}\to\infty$ since $b>0$. But $\log\xi_t=\log x+t\big(b-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}+\sigma\frac{B_t}{t}\big)$ and $B_t/t\to0$ a.s. (strong law for Brownian motion / LIL), so $\log\xi_t\to-\infty$, i.e. $\xi_t\to0$ a.s.

**Common pitfalls.** Writing $d\log\xi=\frac{d\xi}{\xi}-\frac12\frac{d\langle\xi\rangle}{\xi^2}$ as a *proof* (log is not $C^2$ on $\mathbb R$; fine as heuristics, but then verify). Forgetting the $-\sigma^2/2$. Confusing $E\xi_t\to\infty$ with a.s. behaviour.

### Exercise 2 (★, 20 min). Ornstein–Uhlenbeck: mean, covariance, limit law, stationarity

Let $\lambda>0$, $\sigma\in\mathbb R$ and $d\xi_t=-\lambda\xi_t\,dt+\sigma\,dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$ (Example 8.6, Baldi Exercise 9.1).

(a) Solve the equation with an integrating factor.
(b) Compute $E\xi_t$, $\operatorname{Var}\xi_t$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\xi_s,\xi_t)$, $s\le t$.
(c) Show that $\xi_t$ converges in law as $t\to\infty$ to a limit $\mu$ independent of $x$.
(d) Show that if $\xi_0=\eta\sim\mu$ is independent of $B$, then $\xi_t\sim\mu$ for every $t$.

**Strategy.** Integrating factor $e^{\lambda t}$; Wiener integral is Gaussian; Itô isometry for covariance; characteristic functions for convergence in law.

**Full solution.**
(a) By the product rule ($e^{\lambda t}$ has finite variation, so no bracket),
$$
d(e^{\lambda t}\xi_t)=\lambda e^{\lambda t}\xi_t\,dt+e^{\lambda t}(-\lambda\xi_t\,dt+\sigma\,dB_t)=\sigma e^{\lambda t}\,dB_t,
$$
so $e^{\lambda t}\xi_t=x+\sigma\int_0^te^{\lambda s}dB_s$ and
$$
\xi_t=e^{-\lambda t}x+\sigma\int_0^te^{-\lambda(t-s)}dB_s .
$$
Uniqueness: affine coefficients satisfy (A), so Theorem 8.13 applies.

(b) The stochastic integral of a deterministic $L^2$ function has mean zero, so $E\xi_t=e^{-\lambda t}x$. For $s\le t$, by the isometry (polarized),
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\xi_s,\xi_t)=\sigma^2e^{-\lambda(s+t)}E\Big[\int_0^se^{\lambda r}dB_r\int_0^te^{\lambda r}dB_r\Big]=\sigma^2e^{-\lambda(s+t)}\int_0^se^{2\lambda r}dr=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}\big(e^{-\lambda(t-s)}-e^{-\lambda(t+s)}\big).
$$
In particular $\operatorname{Var}\xi_t=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}(1-e^{-2\lambda t})$. The process is Gaussian (Toolbox item 5).

(c) $\xi_t\sim N\big(e^{-\lambda t}x,\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}(1-e^{-2\lambda t})\big)$, so its characteristic function is $\exp\big(i\theta e^{-\lambda t}x-\frac{\theta^2\sigma^2}{4\lambda}(1-e^{-2\lambda t})\big)\to\exp\big(-\frac{\theta^2\sigma^2}{4\lambda}\big)$. By Lévy's continuity theorem $\xi_t\Rightarrow\mu=N(0,\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda})$, whatever $x$.

(d) With $\xi_0=\eta$ (which is $\mathcal F_0$-measurable after enlarging the filtration, Section 2.11), the same computation gives $\xi_t=e^{-\lambda t}\eta+\sigma\int_0^te^{-\lambda(t-s)}dB_s$. The two terms are independent centred Gaussians (the first is a function of $\eta$, the second of $B$), so $\xi_t$ is centred Gaussian with variance
$$
e^{-2\lambda t}\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}+\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}(1-e^{-2\lambda t})=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}.
$$
So $\xi_t\sim\mu$ for all $t$: $\mu$ is the stationary distribution.

**Common pitfalls.** Writing $\operatorname{Var}=\sigma^2\int_0^te^{2\lambda s}ds$ without the factor $e^{-2\lambda t}$. Using $s\vee t$ instead of $s\wedge t$ in the covariance. For $\lambda<0$ there is no limit law (variance blows up).

### Exercise 3 (★★, 25 min). Brownian bridge (Baldi 9.2(b), Øksendal 5.11)

For $0\le t<1$ consider
$$
d\xi_t=-\frac{\xi_t}{1-t}\,dt+dB_t,\qquad\xi_0=x .
$$
(a) Explain why the equation has a unique strong solution on $[0,T]$ for every $T<1$, but Theorem 8.13 does not apply on $[0,1)$.
(b) Solve it explicitly.
(c) Compute mean and covariance; show that for $x=0$ it is a Brownian bridge (centred Gaussian with covariance $s\wedge t-st$).
(d) Show $\xi_t\to0$ in $L^2$ as $t\uparrow1$.

**Strategy.** Time-dependent linear drift; integrating factor $1/(1-t)$.

**Full solution.**
(a) $b(x,t)=-x/(1-t)$, $\sigma\equiv1$. On $[0,T]$ with $T<1$: $|b(x,t)-b(y,t)|\le\frac{|x-y|}{1-T}$ and $|b(x,t)|\le\frac{1}{1-T}(1+|x|)$, uniformly in $t\in[0,T]$, so (A) holds and Theorem 8.13 gives a unique strong solution on $[0,T]$; by uniqueness, solutions on $[0,T]$ and $[0,T']$ agree on the common interval, so they patch into a solution on $[0,1)$. On $[0,1)$ the Lipschitz constant $1/(1-t)$ is not bounded, so (A) fails there.

(b) Since $\frac1{1-t}$ has finite variation,
$$
d\Big(\frac{\xi_t}{1-t}\Big)=\frac{\xi_t}{(1-t)^2}dt+\frac1{1-t}\Big(-\frac{\xi_t}{1-t}dt+dB_t\Big)=\frac{dB_t}{1-t},
$$
hence
$$
\xi_t=(1-t)\Big(x+\int_0^t\frac{dB_s}{1-s}\Big),\qquad0\le t<1 .
$$
(c) $E\xi_t=x(1-t)$. For $s\le t<1$,
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\xi_s,\xi_t)=(1-s)(1-t)\int_0^s\frac{dr}{(1-r)^2}=(1-s)(1-t)\Big(\frac1{1-s}-1\Big)=(1-s)(1-t)\frac{s}{1-s}=s(1-t).
$$
For $x=0$ the process is centred Gaussian with covariance $s(1-t)=s\wedge t-st$ for $s\le t$: this is the covariance of the Brownian bridge, and a centred Gaussian process is determined in law by its covariance. So $\xi$ is a Brownian bridge.

(d) $E\xi_t^2=x^2(1-t)^2+t(1-t)\to0$ as $t\uparrow1$. (In fact $\xi_t\to0$ a.s., which needs more work.)

*Generalisation.* $dY_t=\frac{b-Y_t}{1-t}dt+dB_t$, $Y_0=a$, gives $Y_t=a(1-t)+bt+(1-t)\int_0^t\frac{dB_s}{1-s}$, which goes from $a$ to $b$.

**Common pitfalls.** Applying Theorem 8.13 directly on $[0,1]$. Computing $\int_0^s(1-r)^{-2}dr$ wrongly (it is $\frac{s}{1-s}$).

### Exercise 4 (★★, 15 min). A Gaussian process that is not a Brownian bridge (Baldi 9.4)

For $t<1$ let $d\xi_t=-\frac12\frac{\xi_t}{1-t}dt+\sqrt{1-t}\,dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$.

(a) Solve the equation. (b) Compare $\operatorname{Var}\xi_t$ with the Brownian bridge variance. Is $\xi$ a Brownian bridge (for $x=0$)?

**Strategy.** Variation of constants: homogeneous solution $x\sqrt{1-t}$; try $\xi_t=\sqrt{1-t}\,C_t$.

**Full solution.**
(a) $\frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{1-t}=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-t}}=-\frac12\frac{\sqrt{1-t}}{1-t}$, so $x\sqrt{1-t}$ solves the homogeneous equation. For $\xi_t=\sqrt{1-t}\,C_t$, the product rule (no bracket) gives
$$
d\xi_t=-\frac12\frac{\xi_t}{1-t}dt+\sqrt{1-t}\,dC_t,
$$
so we need $\sqrt{1-t}\,dC_t=\sqrt{1-t}\,dB_t$, i.e. $C_t=x+B_t$:
$$
\xi_t=\sqrt{1-t}\,(x+B_t).
$$
(b) $\operatorname{Var}\xi_t=(1-t)t$, the same as for a Brownian bridge. But for $s\le t$,
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\xi_s,\xi_t)=\sqrt{(1-s)(1-t)}\,E[B_sB_t]=s\sqrt{(1-s)(1-t)}\neq s(1-t)\quad(s<t),
$$
since $\sqrt{1-s}>\sqrt{1-t}$. So $\xi$ is a Gaussian process with the one-dimensional marginals of a Brownian bridge (for $x=0$), but **not** a Brownian bridge.

**Common pitfalls.** Concluding equality of processes from equality of one-dimensional marginals.

### Exercise 5 (★★, 20 min). Linear equation with time-dependent coefficients (Baldi 9.2(a), scalar case)

Let $b,\sigma:[0,T]\to\mathbb R$ be bounded measurable and $d\xi_t=b(t)\xi_t\,dt+\sigma(t)\,dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$. Find the solution, show it is Gaussian, and compute mean and covariance.

**Strategy.** Integrating factor $e^{-\beta(t)}$ with $\beta(t)=\int_0^tb(s)ds$.

**Full solution.** $\beta$ is absolutely continuous with $\beta'=b$ a.e., so $e^{-\beta(t)}$ has finite variation and
$$
d\big(e^{-\beta(t)}\xi_t\big)=-b(t)e^{-\beta(t)}\xi_tdt+e^{-\beta(t)}\big(b(t)\xi_tdt+\sigma(t)dB_t\big)=e^{-\beta(t)}\sigma(t)\,dB_t .
$$
Therefore
$$
\xi_t=e^{\beta(t)}\Big(x+\int_0^te^{-\beta(s)}\sigma(s)\,dB_s\Big).
$$
(Assumption (A) holds with $L=M=\sup|b|+\sup|\sigma|$, so this is the unique solution.) The integrand $e^{-\beta(s)}\sigma(s)$ is deterministic and bounded, so $(\xi_{t_1},\dots,\xi_{t_n})$ is an affine image of a jointly Gaussian vector of Wiener integrals: $\xi$ is a Gaussian process. Mean: $E\xi_t=xe^{\beta(t)}$. Covariance:
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\xi_s,\xi_t)=e^{\beta(s)+\beta(t)}\int_0^{s\wedge t}e^{-2\beta(r)}\sigma(r)^2dr .
$$
*Checks.* $b\equiv-\lambda$, $\sigma$ constant gives Exercise 2. $b(t)=-\frac1{1-t}$, $\sigma=1$ gives $\beta(t)=\log(1-t)$ and Exercise 3.

**Common pitfalls.** Writing $e^{-b(t)t}$ instead of $e^{-\int_0^tb}$ when $b$ depends on $t$.

### Exercise 6 (★★, 30 min). The general affine equation (Baldi 9.10, 9.11; Øksendal 5.6)

Consider $dX_t=(a+bX_t)\,dt+(c+\kappa X_t)\,dB_t$, $X_0=x$, with $a,b,c,\kappa\in\mathbb R$. (The task sheets often write $d$ for $\kappa$; we rename it to avoid confusion with the differential.)

(a) Let $\Phi_t=\exp\big((b-\frac{\kappa^2}2)t+\kappa B_t\big)$. Show that
$$
X_t=\Phi_t\Big(x+(a-c\kappa)\int_0^t\Phi_s^{-1}ds+c\int_0^t\Phi_s^{-1}dB_s\Big)
$$
is the unique solution.
(b) Compute $m(t)=E X_t$.
(c) Show that $v(t)=E X_t^2$ solves $v'=(2b+\kappa^2)v+2(a+c\kappa)m+c^2$, and solve it when $a=b=0$.
(d) Special case $\kappa=0$: show that $X$ is Gaussian and find its limit law when $b<0$.

**Strategy.** Variation of constants: $\Phi$ solves the homogeneous equation $d\Phi=\Phi(b\,dt+\kappa\,dB)$. Look for $X=\Phi Y$ with $dY=\alpha\,dt+\gamma\,dB$, match coefficients. Moments by taking expectations in the integral equation.

**Full solution.**
(a) *Derivation.* By Itô, $d\Phi_t=\Phi_t\big((b-\frac{\kappa^2}{2})dt+\kappa dB_t\big)+\frac12\kappa^2\Phi_tdt=\Phi_t(b\,dt+\kappa\,dB_t)$. Let $dY=\alpha\,dt+\gamma\,dB$. By the product rule,
$$
d(\Phi Y)=\Phi(\alpha\,dt+\gamma\,dB)+Y\Phi(b\,dt+\kappa\,dB)+\kappa\Phi\gamma\,dt=\big(\Phi\alpha+\kappa\Phi\gamma+bX\big)dt+\big(\Phi\gamma+\kappa X\big)dB .
$$
To get $(a+bX)dt+(c+\kappa X)dB$ we need $\Phi\gamma=c$ and $\Phi\alpha+\kappa c=a$, i.e. $\gamma=c\Phi^{-1}$, $\alpha=(a-c\kappa)\Phi^{-1}$. With $Y_0=x$ this is the stated formula, and running the computation backwards verifies that it is a solution (all integrands are continuous adapted processes, so the integrals exist). The coefficients are affine, hence Lipschitz with linear growth, so the solution is unique (Theorem 8.13).

(b) By Theorem 8.10, $E\sup_{t\le T}|X_t|^2<\infty$, so $(c+\kappa X)\in M^2$ and $\int_0^t(c+\kappa X_s)dB_s$ is a martingale with mean $0$ (Remark 8.11). Taking expectations,
$$
m(t)=x+\int_0^t(a+bm(s))\,ds\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m'=a+bm,\ m(0)=x,
$$
so $m(t)=xe^{bt}+\frac ab(e^{bt}-1)$ if $b\neq0$, and $m(t)=x+at$ if $b=0$. If $b<0$, $m(t)\to-a/b$; if $x=-a/b$, $m\equiv x$.

(c) By Itô, $d(X_t^2)=2X_t\,dX_t+d\langle X\rangle_t=\big(2X_t(a+bX_t)+(c+\kappa X_t)^2\big)dt+2X_t(c+\kappa X_t)\,dB_t$. By Theorem 8.10 with $p=4$, $X(c+\kappa X)\in M^2$, so the $dB$ term is a martingale. Taking expectations, and expanding $(c+\kappa X)^2=c^2+2c\kappa X+\kappa^2X^2$,
$$
v'(t)=(2b+\kappa^2)v(t)+2(a+c\kappa)m(t)+c^2,\qquad v(0)=x^2 .
$$
For $a=b=0$: $m\equiv x$ and $v'=\kappa^2v+2c\kappa x+c^2$. With $K=2c\kappa x+c^2$, if $\kappa\neq0$,
$$
v(t)=\Big(x^2+\frac{K}{\kappa^2}\Big)e^{\kappa^2t}-\frac{K}{\kappa^2},\qquad\operatorname{Var}X_t=v(t)-x^2=\Big(x^2+\frac{K}{\kappa^2}\Big)\big(e^{\kappa^2t}-1\big).
$$
(Check: as $\kappa\to0$, $\operatorname{Var}X_t\to c^2t$, the variance of $x+cB_t$.)

(d) If $\kappa=0$: $\Phi_t=e^{bt}$ is deterministic and
$$
X_t=e^{bt}x+\frac ab(e^{bt}-1)+c\int_0^te^{b(t-s)}dB_s,
$$
a Gaussian process with mean $m(t)$ and variance $c^2\int_0^te^{2b(t-s)}ds=\frac{c^2}{2b}(e^{2bt}-1)$. If $b<0$, mean $\to-a/b$ and variance $\to\frac{c^2}{2|b|}$, so $X_t\Rightarrow N\big(-\frac ab,\frac{c^2}{2|b|}\big)$ (characteristic functions).

**Common pitfalls.** Forgetting the bracket term $\kappa c\,dt$ in the product rule (this is where $a-c\kappa$ comes from). Justifying $E\int(\cdots)dB=0$: say that the integrand is in $M^2$ by Theorem 8.10.

### Exercise 7 (★, 15 min). Checking Assumption (A)

For each pair, decide whether Assumption (A) holds (on $[0,T]$), and what can be concluded.

(a) $b(x)=\sin x$, $\sigma(x)=2+\cos x$.
(b) $b(x)=\log(1+x^2)$, $\sigma(x)=x\mathbf 1_{\{x>0\}}$ (Øksendal 5.9).
(c) $b(x,t)=\frac{x}{1+t}$, $\sigma(x,t)=tx$.
(d) $b(x)=\sqrt{1+x^2}+\frac x2$, $\sigma(x)=\sqrt{1+x^2}$ (Baldi 9.13).
(e) $b(x)=-x^3$, $\sigma=1$.
(f) $b=0$, $\sigma(x)=\sqrt{|x|}$.
(g) $b(x)=\mathbf 1_{\{x>0\}}$, $\sigma=1$.

**Strategy.** For $C^1$ functions, Lipschitz with constant $L$ iff $\sup|f'|\le L$ (mean value theorem). For time-independent coefficients Lipschitz implies linear growth.

**Full solution.**
(a) $|b'|=|\cos x|\le1$, $|\sigma'|=|\sin x|\le1$: Lipschitz with $L=1$; bounded, hence linear growth. (A) holds: unique strong solution.
(b) $b'(x)=\frac{2x}{1+x^2}$ and $|2x|\le1+x^2$, so $|b'|\le1$: Lipschitz, and $|b(x)|\le|b(0)|+|x|=|x|$. $\sigma(x)=\max(x,0)$ satisfies $|\max(x,0)-\max(y,0)|\le|x-y|$ and $|\sigma(x)|\le|x|$. (A) holds.
(c) $|b(x,t)-b(y,t)|\le|x-y|$, $|\sigma(x,t)-\sigma(y,t)|\le T|x-y|$, $|b|\le|x|$, $|\sigma|\le T|x|$ for $t\le T$. (A) holds with $L=M=\max(1,T)$ on every bounded $[0,T]$.
(d) $\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{1+x^2}=\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}\in(-1,1)$, so $\sigma$ is 1-Lipschitz and $b$ is $\frac32$-Lipschitz. (A) holds.
(e) $b'(x)=-3x^2$ is unbounded: not globally Lipschitz; $|x^3|\le M(1+|x|)$ fails: no linear growth. (A) fails; Theorem 8.13 does not apply. (The coefficient is locally Lipschitz; global existence actually holds since the drift points inwards, but that is outside the syllabus.)
(f) $\sqrt{|x|}/|x|\to\infty$ as $x\to0$: not Lipschitz at $0$. Linear growth holds ($\sqrt{|x|}\le1+|x|$), so Theorem 8.10 applies to any solution, but Theorem 8.13 does not.
(g) Discontinuous, so not Lipschitz. Bounded and measurable, $\sigma=I$: Theorem 8.25 gives a weak solution.

**Common pitfalls.** Forgetting that linear growth must hold **uniformly in $t$**. Saying "$\log(1+x^2)$ is not Lipschitz since it is unbounded": unbounded is irrelevant, the derivative is bounded.

### Exercise 8 (★★, 20 min). An explicit nonlinear SDE (Baldi 9.13)

$d\xi_t=\big(\sqrt{1+\xi_t^2}+\frac12\xi_t\big)dt+\sqrt{1+\xi_t^2}\,dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$.

(a) Does it have a unique strong solution? (b) Compute the differential of $Y_t=\log\big(\sqrt{1+\xi_t^2}+\xi_t\big)=\operatorname{arcsinh}(\xi_t)$. (c) Solve the equation and compute $E\xi_t$.

**Strategy.** Lamperti-type transformation: $f$ with $f'=1/\sigma$ turns the noise into additive noise.

**Full solution.**
(a) Yes: Exercise 7(d) and Theorem 8.13.
(b) $f(z)=\log(\sqrt{1+z^2}+z)$ is $C^\infty$ on $\mathbb R$ (the argument is $>0$), with
$$
f'(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+z^2}+z}\Big(\frac{z}{\sqrt{1+z^2}}+1\Big)=\frac1{\sqrt{1+z^2}},\qquad f''(z)=-\frac{z}{(1+z^2)^{3/2}} .
$$
By Itô, with $d\langle\xi\rangle_t=(1+\xi_t^2)dt$,
$$
dY_t=\frac{\sqrt{1+\xi_t^2}+\frac12\xi_t}{\sqrt{1+\xi_t^2}}dt+dB_t-\frac12\frac{\xi_t}{(1+\xi_t^2)^{3/2}}(1+\xi_t^2)dt=dt+dB_t .
$$
(c) $Y_t=\operatorname{arcsinh}(x)+t+B_t$, and since $\operatorname{arcsinh}$ is the inverse of $\sinh$,
$$
\xi_t=\sinh\big(\operatorname{arcsinh}(x)+t+B_t\big).
$$
With $\alpha=\operatorname{arcsinh}(x)$: $E\xi_t=\frac12\big(e^{\alpha+t}E e^{B_t}-e^{-\alpha-t}Ee^{-B_t}\big)=\frac12\big(e^{\alpha+\frac32t}-e^{-\alpha-\frac12t}\big)$.

### Exercise 9 (★★, 20 min). Non-uniqueness without Lipschitz continuity

(a) Show that the ODE $x'(t)=2\sqrt{|x(t)|}$, $x(0)=0$, has infinitely many solutions.
(b) Show that $dX_t=3X_t^{1/3}dt+3X_t^{2/3}dB_t$, $X_0=0$ (real cube roots) has the two solutions $X\equiv0$ and $X_t=B_t^3$. Which hypothesis of Theorem 8.13 fails? Which uniqueness notions fail? Are these strong solutions?

**Strategy.** Verify by direct differentiation / Itô; identify the non-Lipschitz point $x=0$.

**Full solution.**
(a) $x\equiv0$ is a solution. For $a\ge0$ let $x_a(t)=(t-a)^2\mathbf 1_{\{t\ge a\}}$. It is $C^1$ with $x_a'(t)=2(t-a)\mathbf 1_{\{t\ge a\}}=2\sqrt{x_a(t)}$ (for $t\ge a$, $\sqrt{(t-a)^2}=t-a$; for $t<a$ both sides vanish), and $x_a(0)=0$. So there is a one-parameter family of solutions. The reason: $x\mapsto2\sqrt{|x|}$ is not Lipschitz at $0$ ($2\sqrt{|x|}/|x|\to\infty$), so the Picard–Lindelöf / Gronwall uniqueness argument breaks down.

(b) $X\equiv0$: both integrands vanish. $X=B^3$: by Itô, $d(B_t^3)=3B_t^2dB_t+3B_tdt$, and $X_t^{1/3}=B_t$, $X_t^{2/3}=B_t^2$, so $dX_t=3X_t^{1/3}dt+3X_t^{2/3}dB_t$, $X_0=0$. In fact, for any $a\ge0$, $X^{(a)}_t=(B_t-B_a)^3\mathbf 1_{\{t\ge a\}}$ is also a solution (apply Itô on $[a,t]$).

*Hypotheses.* Linear growth holds ($|x|^{1/3}\le1+|x|$, $|x|^{2/3}\le1+|x|$), so Theorem 8.10 applies to every solution. The Lipschitz condition fails at $0$: $|x^{1/3}|/|x|=|x|^{-2/3}\to\infty$.

*Uniqueness.* The two solutions live on the same space with the same $B$ and are not indistinguishable: pathwise uniqueness fails. They have different laws ($\delta_0$ versus the law of $B^3$): uniqueness in law fails too.

*Strong?* Both are functionals of $B$ (adapted to the Brownian filtration), so both are strong solutions. Strong existence without uniqueness.

**Common pitfalls.** Forgetting that $x^{1/3}$ is the **real** cube root (odd function), so that $X^{1/3}=B$ also when $B<0$. Saying that linear growth fails.

### Exercise 10 (★★, 20 min). Explosion without linear growth (Baldi 9.23)

(a) Solve $x'=x^2$, $x(0)=1$. (b) For $x\neq0$ let $\tau_x=\inf\{t:B_t=1/x\}$. Show that $\xi_t=\frac{x}{1-xB_t}$, $t<\tau_x$, solves $d\xi_t=\xi_t^3dt+\xi_t^2dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$. (c) Show that $\xi$ explodes in finite time a.s., and compute $P(\tau_x\le T)$ for $x>0$.

**Strategy.** Itô for $f(y)=\frac{x}{1-xy}$ on $\{y<1/x\}$ (localize). Reflection principle.

**Full solution.**
(a) $x(t)=\frac1{1-t}$, which explodes at $t=1$: superlinear drift.
(b) Let $x>0$ (the case $x<0$ is symmetric). $f(y)=\frac x{1-xy}$ is $C^\infty$ on $(-\infty,1/x)$, with $f'(y)=\frac{x^2}{(1-xy)^2}=f(y)^2$ and $f''(y)=2f(y)f'(y)=2f(y)^3$. For $n$ large, apply Itô to a $C^2$ function equal to $f$ on $(-\infty,1/x-1/n]$ and stop at $\tau_n=\inf\{t:B_t=1/x-1/n\}$: for $t<\tau_n$,
$$
d\xi_t=f'(B_t)dB_t+\frac12f''(B_t)dt=\xi_t^2dB_t+\xi_t^3dt .
$$
Since $\tau_n\uparrow\tau_x$, the equation holds on $[0,\tau_x)$.
(c) Brownian motion hits every level a.s., so $\tau_x<\infty$ a.s., and $\xi_t\to+\infty$ as $t\uparrow\tau_x$. By the reflection principle, $P(\tau_x\le T)=P(\sup_{t\le T}B_t\ge1/x)=2P(B_T\ge1/x)=2\big(1-\Phi\big(\frac1{x\sqrt T}\big)\big)>0$. So on any interval $[0,T]$ there is a positive probability of explosion: no global solution, consistent with the failure of linear growth (Remark 8.19).

**Common pitfalls.** Applying Itô to $f$ on all of $\mathbb R$ ($f$ is singular at $1/x$). Thinking that local Lipschitz alone guarantees global existence.

### Exercise 11 (★★, 25 min). A priori $L^2$ estimate via Itô and Gronwall (Øksendal 5.10)

Assume $|b(x,t)|^2+|\sigma(x,t)|^2\le K(1+|x|^2)$, and let $X$ be a solution with $X_0=Z\in L^2$, $Z$ $\mathcal F_0$-measurable. Show
$$
E|X_t|^2\le(1+E|Z|^2)e^{(1+K)t}-1\le(1+E|Z|^2)e^{(1+K)t}.
$$

**Strategy.** Itô for $|X|^2$, localize with $\tau_R$ so that the stochastic integral has mean zero and everything is bounded, Gronwall for $1+\varphi_R$, then Fatou.

**Full solution.** By Itô (scalar notation; the vector case is identical with $x\cdot b$ and $|\sigma|^2$),
$$
d|X_t|^2=\big(2X_t\cdot b(X_t,t)+|\sigma(X_t,t)|^2\big)dt+2X_t\cdot\sigma(X_t,t)\,dB_t .
$$
Let $\tau_R=\inf\{t:|X_t|\ge R\}\wedge T$ and $\varphi_R(t)=E|X_{t\wedge\tau_R}|^2$, which is bounded by $R^2+E|Z|^2$. On $\{s<\tau_R\}$ the $dB$-integrand is bounded (if $|Z|\ge R$, $\tau_R=0$), so the stopped stochastic integral has mean $0$. Using $2x\cdot b\le|x|^2+|b|^2$,
$$
2x\cdot b+|\sigma|^2\le|x|^2+K(1+|x|^2)\le(1+K)(1+|x|^2).
$$
Hence
$$
\varphi_R(t)=E|Z|^2+E\int_0^{t\wedge\tau_R}\big(2X_s\cdot b+|\sigma|^2\big)ds\le E|Z|^2+(1+K)\int_0^t\big(1+\varphi_R(s)\big)ds,
$$
where we used $\int_0^{t\wedge\tau_R}g(X_s)ds\le\int_0^tg(X_{s\wedge\tau_R})ds$ for $g\ge0$. So $\psi_R=1+\varphi_R$ satisfies $\psi_R(t)\le(1+E|Z|^2)+(1+K)\int_0^t\psi_R(s)ds$, and Gronwall gives $\psi_R(t)\le(1+E|Z|^2)e^{(1+K)t}$. As $R\to\infty$, $X_{t\wedge\tau_R}\to X_t$ a.s. (continuity), and Fatou's lemma gives $E|X_t|^2\le\liminf_R\varphi_R(t)\le(1+E|Z|^2)e^{(1+K)t}-1$. $\square$

**Common pitfalls.** Taking $E\int X\sigma\,dB=0$ without justification (we do not know yet that $X\sigma\in M^2$: that is what localization is for). Using dominated instead of Fatou in the last step.

### Exercise 12 (★★, 20 min). Gronwall: $L^2$-continuity in the initial point

Under (A), let $X^x,X^y$ be the solutions started at $x,y\in\mathbb R^m$ at time $0$ with the same $B$. Show that
$$
E|X^x_t-X^y_t|^2\le3|x-y|^2e^{3L^2(T+1)t},\qquad0\le t\le T .
$$
Deduce pathwise uniqueness again.

**Strategy.** Subtract the equations, square, Cauchy–Schwarz for $dt$, isometry for $dB$, Lipschitz, Gronwall (boundedness from Theorem 8.10).

**Full solution.** Let $D_t=X^x_t-X^y_t=(x-y)+\int_0^t\Delta b_s\,ds+\int_0^t\Delta\sigma_s\,dB_s$, with $\Delta b_s=b(X^x_s,s)-b(X^y_s,s)$, etc. By $|a_1+a_2+a_3|^2\le3\sum|a_i|^2$, Cauchy–Schwarz and the isometry,
$$
E|D_t|^2\le3|x-y|^2+3tE\int_0^t|\Delta b_s|^2ds+3E\int_0^t|\Delta\sigma_s|^2ds\le3|x-y|^2+3L^2(T+1)\int_0^tE|D_s|^2ds .
$$
$t\mapsto E|D_t|^2$ is bounded on $[0,T]$ by Theorem 8.10. Gronwall gives the claim. If $x=y$, $E|D_t|^2=0$ for all $t$, so $X^x_t=X^y_t$ a.s. for each $t$; by continuity of paths (take rational $t$), they are indistinguishable.

### Exercise 13 (★★, 30 min). Weak solution via Girsanov for $dX=b(X)\,dt+dB$

Let $b:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be bounded and measurable, $|b|\le K$, and $x\in\mathbb R$.

(a) Construct a weak solution of $dX_t=b(X_t)\,dt+dB_t$, $X_0=x$, on $[0,T]$.
(b) Take $b\equiv\mu$ constant. Identify the law of $X_T$ under the new measure in two ways.
(c) Show that for every $t\in(0,T]$ the law of $X_t$ (in the solution of (a)) has a density with respect to Lebesgue measure.
(d) Show that $E_P[Z_T^2]\le e^{K^2T}$ and deduce $\tilde P(X\in A)\le e^{K^2T/2}\sqrt{P(x+W\in A)}$ for Borel $A\subseteq C([0,T])$.

**Strategy.** Theorem 8.25 with $\sigma=1$: start with $X=x+W$, change measure to remove the drift from $W$.

**Full solution.**
(a) Let $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),P)$ carry a Brownian motion $W$, and set $X_t=x+W_t$ (solution of the driftless equation). Let $\Phi_t=b(X_t)=b(x+W_t)$: progressively measurable, $|\Phi|\le K$, so $\int_0^T\Phi_s^2ds\le K^2T$. By Proposition 7.3,
$$
Z_t=\exp\Big(\int_0^tb(x+W_s)\,dW_s-\frac12\int_0^tb(x+W_s)^2ds\Big)
$$
is a martingale with $E Z_T=1$; define $d\tilde P=Z_TdP$. By Girsanov (Theorem 7.5), $\tilde B_t=W_t-\int_0^tb(x+W_s)ds$ is a $\tilde P$-Brownian motion. Then
$$
X_t=x+W_t=x+\int_0^tb(X_s)\,ds+\tilde B_t,
$$
so $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),\tilde P,X,\tilde B)$ is a weak solution.

(b) With $b\equiv\mu$: $Z_T=e^{\mu W_T-\mu^2T/2}$, $\tilde B_t=W_t-\mu t$, and $X_t=x+\mu t+\tilde B_t$, so $X_T\sim N(x+\mu T,T)$ under $\tilde P$. Directly: for bounded measurable $f$,
$$
E_{\tilde P}[f(X_T)]=E_P\big[e^{\mu W_T-\mu^2T/2}f(x+W_T)\big]=\int f(x+y)\,\frac{e^{\mu y-\mu^2T/2-y^2/(2T)}}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}dy=\int f(x+y)\frac{e^{-(y-\mu T)^2/(2T)}}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}dy,
$$
which is $E f(Y)$ for $Y\sim N(x+\mu T,T)$. (Completing the square: $\mu y-\frac{\mu^2T}2-\frac{y^2}{2T}=-\frac{(y-\mu T)^2}{2T}$.)

(c) If $\lambda(A)=0$, then $P(X_t\in A)=P(x+W_t\in A)=0$ (Gaussian), and $\tilde P\ll P$, so $\tilde P(X_t\in A)=E_P[Z_T\mathbf 1_{\{X_t\in A\}}]=0$. So the $\tilde P$-law of $X_t$ is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure; by Radon–Nikodym it has a density.

(d) $Z_T^2=\exp\big(\int2\Phi\,dW-\frac12\int(2\Phi)^2ds\big)\exp\big(\int_0^T\Phi_s^2ds\big)\le\mathcal E(2\Phi)_Te^{K^2T}$, and $E\,\mathcal E(2\Phi)_T=1$ by Proposition 7.3 ($|2\Phi|\le2K$). So $E Z_T^2\le e^{K^2T}$. By Cauchy–Schwarz (Cameron–Martin formula, Proposition 8.26),
$$
\tilde P(X\in A)=E_P[Z_T\mathbf 1_{\{x+W\in A\}}]\le\sqrt{E Z_T^2}\sqrt{P(x+W\in A)}\le e^{K^2T/2}\sqrt{P(x+W\in A)} .
$$

**Common pitfalls.** Sign in Girsanov. Forgetting to justify that $Z$ is a martingale. Saying the solution is strong: $\tilde B$ was built from $X$.

### Exercise 14 (★★★, 30 min). Around Tanaka's example

Let $\beta$ be a Brownian motion, $\operatorname{sgn}(x)=-1$ for $x\le0$, $+1$ for $x>0$, and $W_t=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\,d\beta_s$.

(a) Show that $W$ is a Brownian motion and $\langle W,\beta\rangle_t=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)ds$.
(b) Show $E[W_t\beta_t]=0$.
(c) Show $E[W_t\beta_t^2]=\frac43\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\,t^{3/2}$. Conclude that $W_t$ and $\beta_t$ are uncorrelated but not independent, and that $(W,\beta)$ is not a two-dimensional Brownian motion.
(d) Show that $\beta$ and $-\beta$ both solve $dX=\operatorname{sgn}(X)dW$, $X_0=0$. What does this say?

**Strategy.** Lévy; polarized Itô isometry $E[\int H\,d\beta\int K\,d\beta]=E\int HK\,ds$; $\beta_t^2=t+2\int\beta\,d\beta$.

**Full solution.**
(a) As in the proof of Lemma 8.27: $W$ is a continuous martingale with $\langle W\rangle_t=\int\operatorname{sgn}^2=t$, so a BM by Lévy. $\langle W,\beta\rangle_t=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\,d\langle\beta\rangle_s=\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)ds$.
(b) $E[W_t\beta_t]=E\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\cdot1\,ds=\int_0^t\big(P(\beta_s>0)-P(\beta_s\le0)\big)ds=\int_0^t\big(\tfrac12-\tfrac12\big)ds=0$.
(c) $\beta_t^2=t+2\int_0^t\beta_sd\beta_s$, and $E[W_t]=0$, so
$$
E[W_t\beta_t^2]=2E\Big[\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)d\beta_s\int_0^t\beta_sd\beta_s\Big]=2\int_0^tE[\operatorname{sgn}(\beta_s)\beta_s]\,ds=2\int_0^tE|\beta_s|\,ds,
$$
because $\operatorname{sgn}(y)y=|y|$ for all $y$ (both sides are $0$ at $y=0$). With $E|\beta_s|=\sqrt{2s/\pi}$,
$$
E[W_t\beta_t^2]=2\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\int_0^t\sqrt s\,ds=\frac43\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\,t^{3/2}\neq0=E[W_t]\,E[\beta_t^2].
$$
So $W_t$ and $\beta_t^2$ are correlated, hence $W_t,\beta_t$ are not independent, though uncorrelated by (b). If $(W,\beta)$ were a 2-dimensional BM, $(W_t,\beta_t)$ would be a Gaussian vector with zero covariance, hence independent: contradiction.
(d) See part 3 of the proof of Lemma 8.27. Same space, same $W$, same initial value, different solutions: pathwise uniqueness fails, although both have the law of BM.

**Common pitfalls.** Concluding independence from zero correlation without joint Gaussianity. Forgetting that $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=-1$ matters only on a Lebesgue-null set of times.

### Exercise 15 (★★, 15 min). A martingale built from the OU process (adapted from Baldi 9.12)

Let $\lambda\neq0$ and $d\xi_t=-\lambda\xi_t\,dt+\sigma\,dB_t$, $\xi_0=x$. Show that $Z_t=e^{2\lambda t}\big(\xi_t^2-\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}\big)$ is a martingale and deduce $E\xi_t^2$.

**Strategy.** Itô with $u(x,t)=e^{2\lambda t}(x^2-\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda})$; check $\partial_tu+Lu=0$ with $L=\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\partial_{xx}-\lambda x\partial_x$.

**Full solution.** $\partial_tu=2\lambda u$, $\partial_xu=2xe^{2\lambda t}$, $\partial_{xx}u=2e^{2\lambda t}$. So

$$
and by Remark 8.5, $dZ_t=2\sigma e^{2\lambda t}\xi_t\,dB_t$. The integrand is in $M^2([0,T])$ since $E\xi_t^2$ is bounded on $[0,T]$ (Theorem 8.10, or Exercise 2), so $Z$ is a true martingale. Hence $E Z_t=Z_0=x^2-\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}$ and

### Exercise 16 (★★, 20 min). GBM: a martingale power and an exit probability (Baldi 9.8)

Let $d\xi_t=b\xi_tdt+\sigma\xi_tdB_t$, $\xi_0=1$, $\sigma\neq0$, $b\neq\sigma^2/2$.
(a) Find $\alpha\neq0$ such that $\xi^\alpha$ is a martingale. (b) Let $\tau$ be the exit time of $\xi$ from $(\frac12,2)$. Compute $P(\xi_\tau=2)$.

**Strategy.** $\xi^\alpha=\exp\big(\alpha(b-\frac{\sigma^2}2)t+\alpha\sigma B_t\big)$ is an exponential martingale iff the drift exponent is $-\frac12\alpha^2\sigma^2$. Optional stopping + dominated convergence.

**Full solution.**
(a) $\xi_t^\alpha=\exp\big(\alpha\sigma B_t-\frac12\alpha^2\sigma^2t\big)\exp\big(\alpha(b-\frac{\sigma^2}{2})t+\frac12\alpha^2\sigma^2t\big)$. The first factor is a martingale; the product is a martingale iff the deterministic factor is $\equiv1$: $\alpha(b-\frac{\sigma^2}{2})+\frac12\alpha^2\sigma^2=0$, i.e.
$$
\alpha=1-\frac{2b}{\sigma^2}.
$$
(b) $\log\xi_t=(b-\frac{\sigma^2}{2})t+\sigma B_t$ is a BM with drift, which leaves the bounded interval $(-\log2,\log2)$ in finite time a.s.: with $c=b-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\neq0$, $\sigma B_t+ct=t(c+\sigma B_t/t)\to\pm\infty$ because $B_t/t\to0$ a.s. So $\tau<\infty$ a.s. By the stopping theorem, $E[\xi^\alpha_{\tau\wedge t}]=1$. For $t\le\tau$, $\xi_t\in[\frac12,2]$, so $\xi^\alpha_{\tau\wedge t}\le2^{|\alpha|}$; dominated convergence as $t\to\infty$ gives, with $p=P(\xi_\tau=2)$,
$$
1=2^\alpha p+2^{-\alpha}(1-p)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p=\frac{1-2^{-\alpha}}{2^\alpha-2^{-\alpha}} .
$$
*Check:* $b=0$ gives $\alpha=1$, $p=\frac{1/2}{3/2}=\frac13$, as for the martingale $\xi$ itself. As $\alpha\to0$, $p\to\frac12$ (symmetric interval in log-scale).

**Common pitfalls.** Applying optional stopping at an unbounded $\tau$ without the bounded-convergence argument.

### Exercise 17 (★★★, 25 min). A nonlinear equation solved by an integrating factor (Øksendal 5.16 type)

Let $x>0$, $\alpha\in\mathbb R$, and consider $dX_t=\frac1{X_t}dt+\alpha X_tdB_t$, $X_0=x$. Let $F_t=\exp\big(-\alpha B_t+\frac12\alpha^2t\big)$.
(a) Show that $Y_t=F_tX_t$ satisfies the pathwise ODE $\frac{dY_t}{dt}=\frac{F_t^2}{Y_t}$.
(b) Deduce $X_t=F_t^{-1}\sqrt{x^2+2\int_0^tF_s^2ds}$ and verify that this is a positive solution.

**Strategy.** $F$ is the inverse of the GBM that solves $dX=\alpha X\,dB$; the product rule kills the noise.

**Full solution.**
(a) By Itô, $dF_t=F_t\big(-\alpha dB_t+\frac12\alpha^2dt\big)+\frac12\alpha^2F_tdt=F_t(-\alpha\,dB_t+\alpha^2dt)$. Then (product rule, $d\langle F,X\rangle_t=(-\alpha F_t)(\alpha X_t)dt$)
$$
d(F_tX_t)=F_t\Big(\frac{dt}{X_t}+\alpha X_tdB_t\Big)+X_tF_t(-\alpha dB_t+\alpha^2dt)-\alpha^2F_tX_tdt=\frac{F_t}{X_t}dt=\frac{F_t^2}{Y_t}dt .

Conversely, define $X$ by this formula; $Y=FX$ is $C^1$ in $t$ with $Y'=F^2/Y$, and reversing the computation (Itô for $X=F^{-1}Y$, with $F^{-1}$ the GBM $d(F^{-1})=\alpha F^{-1}dB$) gives $dX=\frac1Xdt+\alpha X\,dB$.

**Common pitfalls.** Forgetting the bracket term in $d(FX)$, or the $\alpha^2$ (not $\frac12\alpha^2$) drift of $F$. Note that $1/x$ is not Lipschitz near $0$, so Theorem 8.13 does not directly apply; the formula shows the solution never reaches $0$.

### Exercise 24 (★★, 20 min). Multidimensional GBM (Baldi 9.6)

E[\xi_i(t)\xi_j(t)]=x_ix_je^{(b_i+b_j+a_{ij})t},\qquad\operatorname{Cov}(\xi_i(t),\xi_j(t))=x_ix_je^{(b_i+b_j)t}\big(e^{a_{ij}t}-1\big).
$$
*Alternative:* $d(\xi_i\xi_j)=\xi_id\xi_j+\xi_jd\xi_i+a_{ij}\xi_i\xi_jdt$, so $v=E[\xi_i\xi_j]$ solves $v'=(b_i+b_j+a_{ij})v$ (the $dB$ part is a martingale since $\xi_i\xi_j\in M^2$).

**Common pitfalls.** Forgetting the cross term $a_{ij}$ (it is the bracket $d\langle\xi_i,\xi_j\rangle=\xi_i\xi_ja_{ij}dt$).

### Exercise 25 (★★★, 25 min). An explicit Girsanov density for $dX=-\operatorname{sgn}(X)\,dt+dB$

Let $W$ be a BM under $P$, $x\in\mathbb R$, $X=x+W$, and $b(y)=-\operatorname{sgn}(y)$.
(a) Write the density $Z_T$ of Theorem 8.25 and explain why $(X,\tilde B)$ is a weak solution of $dX=-\operatorname{sgn}(X)dt+d\tilde B$ under $\tilde P$.
(b) Using Tanaka's formula $|x+W_T|=|x|+\int_0^T\operatorname{sgn}(x+W_s)dW_s+L^0_T$, where $L^0$ is the local time of $X$ at $0$, show that
$$
Z_T=\exp\big(|x|-|X_T|+L^0_T-\tfrac T2\big).
$$
(c) Interpret: which paths are favoured by $\tilde P$?

**Full solution.**
(a) $\Phi_s=b(X_s)=-\operatorname{sgn}(x+W_s)$, $|\Phi|=1$, so $\int_0^T\Phi^2ds=T$ and $Z_T=\exp\big(-\int_0^T\operatorname{sgn}(x+W_s)dW_s-\frac T2\big)$ is the terminal value of a true martingale (Prop 7.3). Girsanov: $\tilde B_t=W_t+\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(X_s)ds$ is a $\tilde P$-BM and $X_t=x+W_t=x-\int_0^t\operatorname{sgn}(X_s)ds+\tilde B_t$.
(b) From Tanaka's formula, $-\int_0^T\operatorname{sgn}(x+W_s)dW_s=|x|-|X_T|+L^0_T$. Substitute.
(c) $Z_T$ is large when $|X_T|$ is small and the local time at $0$ is large: $\tilde P$ favours paths that stay near $0$, as expected from a drift pointing towards the origin.

**Common pitfalls.** Sign of the drift in $\tilde B$. The value of $\operatorname{sgn}(0)$ is irrelevant (Lebesgue-null set of times).

### Exercise 26 (★★, 10 min). K–S strong solutions in practice

(a) Show that the GBM solution of Exercise 1 is a K–S strong solution by exhibiting the map $\Phi$ with $\xi=\Phi(B)$.
(b) Explain why, in Tanaka's example, the weak solution $\beta$ is not of the form $\Phi(W)$ for a non-anticipative measurable $\Phi$.

**Full solution.**
(a) $\Phi(w)_t=x\exp\big((b-\frac{\sigma^2}{2})t+\sigma w_t\big)$, a continuous (hence measurable) map $C([0,T])\to C([0,T])$, and $\Phi(w)_t$ depends only on $w_t$, so it is non-anticipative. $\xi=\Phi(B)$ is $\overline{\mathcal F}^B$-adapted and solves the equation: a K–S strong solution.
(b) If $\beta=\Phi(W)$ with $\Phi$ non-anticipative, then $\beta$ would be $\overline{\mathcal F}^W$-adapted, i.e. a strong solution, contradicting part 4 of Lemma 8.27. Concretely, $W=\int\operatorname{sgn}(\beta)d\beta=|\beta|-L^0(\beta)$ only "sees" $|\beta|$, so it cannot recover the sign of $\beta$. Indeed $-\beta$ gives the same $W$.

## 6. Predicted exam questions (ranked)

1. **State and prove Theorem 8.13** (strong existence and pathwise uniqueness under (A)). *Outline:* Picard iterates in $M^2$; $E\sup|\xi_{m+1}-\xi_m|^2\le(Rt)^{m+1}/(m+1)!$ via Hölder + Doob + Lipschitz; Markov + Borel–Cantelli give uniform a.s. convergence; pass to the limit (Prop 5.27); uniqueness by Gronwall with $v$ bounded by Theorem 8.10.
2. **Tanaka's example (Lemma 8.27): state and prove.** *Outline:* $W=\int\operatorname{sgn}(\beta)d\beta$ is BM (Lévy), $\beta$ is a weak solution; any solution is BM (Lévy) so uniqueness in law; $-\beta$ solves with same $W$ so no pathwise uniqueness; strong solution would give $\mathcal F^X\subseteq\mathcal F^W\subseteq\mathcal F^{|X|}$ by Tanaka's formula, absurd.
3. **Solve a linear SDE and compute mean/variance** (OU, GBM, Brownian bridge, affine $dX=(a+bX)dt+(c+\kappa X)dB$). *Outline:* integrating factor / variation of constants; Gaussian Wiener integrals; Itô isometry; moment ODEs justified by Theorem 8.10.
4. **Theorem 8.25: construct a weak solution of $dX=b(X)dt+dB$ (or $dX=b\,dt+\sigma dB$) with $b$ bounded measurable.** *Outline:* $X=x+W$ (driftless), $\Phi=\sigma^{-1}b(X)$ bounded, $Z=\mathcal E(\int\Phi dW)$ martingale by Prop 7.3, $\tilde B=W-\int\Phi\,ds$ is $\tilde P$-BM, $dX=b\,dt+\sigma\,d\tilde B$.
5. **State and prove Theorem 8.10** (a priori bounds). *Outline:* localize at $\tau_R$, $3^{p-1}$ split, Hölder, maximal $L^p$ inequality, linear growth, Gronwall uniform in $R$, monotone convergence; second bound by the same estimates.
6. **State and prove Theorem 8.22** (stability). *Outline:* after time $v$ both solve the same SDE; Gronwall gives $cE|\xi^1_v-\eta_2|^p$; split and use the second bound of 8.10.
7. **Define weak/strong solutions, pathwise uniqueness, uniqueness in law; give examples separating them.** *Outline:* Defs 8.1 to 8.4; Tanaka (law-unique, not pathwise); $\mathbf 1_{\{X\neq0\}}dB$ or $3X^{1/3}dt+3X^{2/3}dB$ (strong, not law-unique); Yamada–Watanabe.
8. **Karatzas–Shreve strong solution and the equivalence with Baldi's definition.** *Outline:* adapted to $\overline{\mathcal F}^B$; Baldi $\Rightarrow$ K–S by choosing the Brownian filtration; K–S $\Rightarrow$ Baldi via $X=\Phi(B)$, transplant $\tilde X=\Phi(\tilde B)$, same joint law, SDE identity transfers.
9. **Check (A) for given coefficients / explain what goes wrong without Lipschitz or linear growth.** *Outline:* derivative bounds; $x^{1/3}$ and $\sqrt{|x|}$ examples (non-uniqueness); $x^2$, $x^3$ (explosion).
10. **State Gronwall's lemma and use it** (e.g. $L^2$ dependence on initial data, Exercise 12). *Outline:* $v\le c+\int wv$, $v$ bounded $\Rightarrow v\le ce^{\int w}$.
11. **State the Cameron–Martin formula (Prop 8.26)** and compute a law with it (constant drift example).
12. **Remark 8.11/8.12-type questions**: $E\xi_t=E\eta+E\int b$; exit probability $\le c(1+E|\eta|^p)/R^p$.

## 7. Flashcards

1. Weak solution? → A filtered space, a BM $B$ and a continuous adapted $\xi$ with $\xi_t=x+\int b(\xi)ds+\int\sigma(\xi)dB$; the space and $B$ are part of the solution.
2. Strong solution (Baldi)? → For every BM on every prescribed filtered space, there is an adapted solution driven by it.
3. Strong solution (Karatzas–Shreve)? → A solution adapted to the augmented natural filtration of the given BM $B$; equivalently $X=\Phi(B)$ with $\Phi$ non-anticipative.
4. Pathwise uniqueness? → Two solutions on the same space, same BM, same initial condition are indistinguishable.
5. Uniqueness in law? → Any two solutions (possibly on different spaces) with the same initial point have the same law on $C([u,T])$.
6. Yamada–Watanabe? → Weak existence + pathwise uniqueness $\Rightarrow$ strong existence + uniqueness in law.
7. Assumption (A)? → $|b|,|\sigma|\le M(1+|x|)$ and $|b(x)-b(y)|,|\sigma(x)-\sigma(y)|\le L|x-y|$, uniformly in $t$.
8. Gronwall? → $v\le c+\int_a^twv$, $v$ bounded, $w\ge0$ integrable $\Rightarrow v(t)\le ce^{\int_a^tw}$; $c=0\Rightarrow v\equiv0$.
9. Theorem 8.10 bound? → $E\sup_{[u,T]}|\xi|^p\le c(1+E|\eta|^p)$ and $E\sup_{[u,t]}|\xi-\eta|^p\le c(t-u)^{p/2}(1+E|\eta|^p)$, $c=c(p,T,M)$.
10. Why localize in Theorem 8.10? → Gronwall needs $v$ bounded; stopping at $\tau_R$ gives $|\xi_R|\le R\vee|\eta|$.
11. Factorial bound in Theorem 8.13? → $E\sup_{s\le t}|\xi_{m+1}-\xi_m|^2\le(Rt)^{m+1}/(m+1)!$.
12. How do you get a.s. uniform convergence of Picard iterates? → Markov: $P(\sup|\xi_{m+1}-\xi_m|>2^{-m})\le4^m(RT)^{m+1}/(m+1)!$, summable, Borel–Cantelli.
13. How do you pass to the limit in the stochastic integral? → $\int_0^T|\sigma(\xi_m)-\sigma(\xi)|^2\le L^2T\sup|\xi_m-\xi|^2\to0$, then Prop 5.27 (convergence in probability).
14. Stability rate in Theorem 8.22? → $c(1+E|\eta_1|^p)(|u-v|^{p/2}+E|\eta_1-\eta_2|^p)$.
15. OU solution? → $\xi_t=e^{-\lambda t}x+\sigma\int_0^te^{-\lambda(t-s)}dB_s$; mean $e^{-\lambda t}x$, variance $\frac{\sigma^2}{2\lambda}(1-e^{-2\lambda t})$.
16. OU limit law ($\lambda>0$)? → $N(0,\sigma^2/(2\lambda))$, also the stationary law.
17. GBM moments? → $E\xi_t=xe^{bt}$, $E\xi_t^2=x^2e^{(2b+\sigma^2)t}$.
18. Brownian bridge SDE and solution? → $d\xi=-\frac{\xi}{1-t}dt+dB$; $\xi_t=(1-t)(x+\int_0^t\frac{dB_s}{1-s})$; covariance $s(1-t)$, $s\le t$.
19. Affine SDE $dX=(a+bX)dt+(c+\kappa X)dB$? → $X=\Phi(x+(a-c\kappa)\int\Phi^{-1}ds+c\int\Phi^{-1}dB)$, $\Phi=e^{(b-\kappa^2/2)t+\kappa B_t}$.
20. Mean of affine SDE? → $m'=a+bm$: $m(t)=xe^{bt}+\frac ab(e^{bt}-1)$.
21. Non-uniqueness SDE example? → $dX=3X^{1/3}dt+3X^{2/3}dB$, $X_0=0$: $X\equiv0$ and $X=B^3$.
22. Non-uniqueness ODE example? → $x'=2\sqrt{|x|}$, $x(0)=0$: $0$ and $(t-a)^2\mathbf 1_{\{t\ge a\}}$.
23. Explosion example? → $d\xi=\xi^3dt+\xi^2dB$: $\xi_t=x/(1-xB_t)$ until $B$ hits $1/x$.
24. Girsanov weak solution: what is $\tilde B$? → $\tilde B_t=B_t-\int_0^t\sigma^{-1}b(\xi_s,s)ds$, a BM under $d\tilde P=Z_TdP$.
25. Why is $Z$ a true martingale in 8.25? → $\Phi=\sigma^{-1}b$ bounded by $\|b\|_\infty/\lambda$: bounded energy (Prop 7.3) or Novikov.
26. Tanaka: why no pathwise uniqueness? → $\beta$ and $-\beta$ both solve with $W=\int\operatorname{sgn}(\beta)d\beta$.
27. Tanaka: why uniqueness in law? → Any solution is a continuous local martingale with $\langle X\rangle_t=t$, hence BM (Lévy).
28. Tanaka: why no strong solution? → $W=|X|-L^0(X)$ gives $\mathcal F^X\subseteq\mathcal F^W\subseteq\mathcal F^{|X|}$, impossible.
29. $dX=\mathbf 1_{\{X\neq0\}}dB$, $X_0=0$? → Strong solutions $0$ and $B$ with different laws: no uniqueness in law.
30. Cameron–Martin formula? → $\tilde P(\xi\in A)=E_P[Z_T\mathbf 1_{\{\xi\in A\}}]$: law with drift $\ll$ law without drift.
